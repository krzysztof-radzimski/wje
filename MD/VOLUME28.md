Mention the abundant evidence that the history was written by Moses, and refer to my treatise on this subject.[^001-note1]

The continuance of the KENITES to the captivity, a monument. See 1 Chronicles 2:55.

KENITES. The posterity of Jethro, which remained in a known, visible distinction among the children of Israel, even to the captivity, was one among many other standing monuments of the truth of the Mosaic history. See. 1 Chronicles 2:55.

He that adds to the Law is severely threatened.

The keeping in a known and visible distinction the Kenites, of which manner the Rechabites, even till the captivity, is one among many evidences of their keeping up their GENEALOGIES.

There were many devout persons that used to write out the Law, and therefore are called SCRIBES, as the Kenites, 1 Chronicles 2:55. There are other passages in Scripture that show that these Kenites gave themselves very much up to a contemplative life.

There was an order of men among them before the captivity, who made it their business to write the Law, as appears by Jeremiah 8:8, "How do ye say, We are wise, the law of the Lord is with us? Lo, certainly in vain made he it; the pen of the SCRIBES is in vain."

Many of the Levites made that their business; see 2 Chronicles 34:13 [^001-note2]

Such was the nature of the Law, so many and so punctilious the services and ceremonies to be observed by ALL THE PEOPLE, that it naturally lead, and as it were obliged and compelled, the whole people to look into the Law, to study it and take care to get a good acquaintance with it.

LAW PUBLICLY KNOWN. The book of the Law was commonly known among the people through their generations. It appears to have been a custom to give the king a copy of it, when he was crowned and enthroned, 2 Kings 11:12.

If any would palm this book containing the laws and history of facts, they must make the people believe that they always had those laws and this account of facts among them, and all those things in their state dependent on the laws and facts from the beginning, though the senses of the whole nation would contradict it, and all would know that it was now new, and that they never had heard of it before. Because the book in many places plainly supposes it would always remain, and be in being from thenceforth: a book pretended to be laid up in a most public repository, in the most public building they had in the nation, to which all the nation were to resort three times in the year, Deuteronomy 31:24-26; commanded to be read before the whole nation once in seven years, their sabbatical year, Deuteronomy 31:10.

Moses was commanded to write a memorial of the oppression of Amalek, that it might remain forever, to put the children in mind of it through all generations, Exodus 14:17 to the end.

Moses delivered the book in a public manner to be laid up in the side of the ark, that it might be there to be a witness against 'em, Deuteronomy 31:26; which supposes it would always be known of.

The king was commanded to write out a copy of the Law [^001-note3].[^001-note4] The people were required to be continually talking of it, to wear it for a sign on their heads, to write it on the post of their houses, etc.

THE FACTS PUBLICLY KNOWN. Concerning those great, miraculous facts, it is written in the book of the Law, Deuteronomy 4:9-12, "Only take heed to thyself, and keep thy soul diligently, lest thou forget the things that thine eyes have seen, and lest they depart from thine heart all the days of thy life: but teach them thy sons, and thy sons' sons; specially the day when thou stoodst before the Lord thy God in Horeb,. .. and the mountain burnt with fire unto the midst of heaven, with darkness, clouds, and thick darkness. And the Lord spake unto you out of the midst of the fire." It would be natural to expect from hence that the history of these great miracles should all along be universally known among the people, and not that there should be any time when the account of 'em should be new in the nations, the people in general not having received the account from their forefathers.

If the history had been forged, it must be introduced to the people's belief and reception with very great SUBTILTY, and those subtil forgers of the history would not be likely to put into it such things as would be likely to lead people to suspect the truth, such as those which have been already mentioned, which naturally awaken people's admiration and inquiry why they never heard those things before, and such precepts as those, where they were commanded to lay up a part of their manna to be kept for their generations, that their posterity might see what bread their forefathers lived upon in the wilderness; which naturally, whenever the history first came abroad, would excite such inquiries as these: Where is this pot of manna? what is become of it? how came it to be lost? And so of Aaron's rod that budded, in memory of the many great miracles done by that rod, and particularly the miraculous destruction of Korah and his company.

The book of the Law contained some prayers, wherein all the people, every head of a family, were required every year, in a very public manner, to rehearse some of the principal facts and expressly mention the great miracles,[^001-note5] as the law of him that offered the firstfruits; and a public profession was to be made at the offering the tithes that included a reference to the same things. See concerning both, in Deuteronomy 26. And care was taken to erect monuments of miraculous events in Joshua's time, after Moses' death, as the monument at Gilgal, and in the midst of Jordan.

And there was a law, Deuteronomy 6:20 to the end, for every head of a family to declare the chief facts to their children.

The Law requires the priests and Levites to teach the people the Law on all occasions, which supposes 'em to be thoroughly acquainted with the book of the Law [^001-note6].[^001-note7] And the Law also supposes the elders of the land, and their judges and civil rulers in general, to be well acquainted with the book of the Law; and it supposes the people in general to be sufficiently taught in the Law, to enable them to be well acquainted with it, by its being said concerning all in general, the word is not "far off," out of sight and out of reach, but is "nigh thee, in thy mouth, and in thy heart, that thou mayst" hear it, and "do it." See that very remarkable place, Deuteronomy 30:11-14. Also, such places as these suppose that sufficient means are provided to make 'em known fully to all; Deuteronomy 6:6-8, and ch. Deuteronomy 11:18-20 and ch. Deuteronomy 4:9.

Concerning the law of the Passover, to be kept in remembrance of the greatest plague in Egypt: it was appointed to be kept forever by the nation, every year from that time, Exodus 12:14, Exodus 24-26, ch. Exodus 13:10. Every man, woman and child were to be active in this memorial; every household was to kill the lamb, and was to strike the blood on the post and lintel of the door, and were to eat it with unleavened bread, and to eat unleavened bread seven days. And in the law by which it was commanded, it was commanded that their children should be instructed in the history of the facts that it was in remembrance of, Exodus 12:27, ch. Exodus 13:8-10.

And the fact, which is the ground of the Law, is much insisted upon and repeated in the Law, as what they should very carefully keep in remembrance, Exodus 12:2, Exodus 11-14, Exodus 17, Exodus 42-43, Exodus 47-49; ch. Exodus 13; Exodus 34:1-10, and Exodus 23, Exodus 25; Deuteronomy 16:1 , Deuteronomy 16:3, Deuteronomy 16:6.

And the injunction of this observance was so strict, that whosoever should neglect it was to be cut off from Israel; and this is urged and repeated with great strictness, Exodus 12:15, Exodus 12:19, and Numbers 9:13, Exodus 23:15 and Exodus 34:18.

And concerning the law of the first-born, in like manner the reason and reference to the fact is contained in the law itself, with a particular injunction to teach their children in all future generations this reason, rehearsing to 'em the principal circumstances of the facts as we find them in the history. See Exodus 13:1-2, Exodus 13:11-16, Numbers 8:17, Numbers 3:12-13. Here Exodus 13, in the 16th verse, is an injunction that those facts shall be continually kept in remembrance, as a token on their hand, and frontlets between their eyes, v. Exodus 13:9, and vv. Exodus 13:15-16; that there might always a continual, universal and perfect remembrance of this fact, and constant and great notice taken of it, and that it should never be out of mind.

MONUMENTS. One of the laws of Moses was to set up great stones as an altar in the land of Canaan, and engrave the words of the Law very plainly, Deuteronomy 27:1, Deuteronomy 27:8; which we have an account of the fulfillment of, in Joshua 8:30-32.

STANDING MIRACLES. The Law of Moses, which we have now, giving an account of the facts, not only appoints the ark, etc., as standing monuments, but speaks of and promises a standing miracle, which would be a confirmation of these facts that should remain in after-ages; such as God's appearing over the mercy seat, and miraculously giving answers from above the mercy seat in their tabernacle, Exodus 25:22-23, and ch. Exodus 29:42, ch. Exodus 30:36, Numbers 17:4, Leviticus 16:2, Leviticus 9:6.

ANOTHER STANDING MIRACLE promised, was God's manifesting his mind by Urim and Thummim, if that was different, as Exodus 28:30, Numbers 27:21.

And[^001-note8] another standing miracle promised was the spirit of prophecy, or foretelling things to come, with that mark given by which to know false prophets, viz. if their prophecies did not come to pass, they were to be rejected; with that severe law annexed, that if any presumed to prophecy and God had not sent him, that man should die, Deuteronomy 18.

Another strange law with a promise of a great standing miracle annexed, was that all their males should go three times in the year to the sanctuary to worship, and that at those times their enemies should not desire their land, Exodus 34:23-24.

Now 'tis not at all likely that any cunning impostors that should forge the Pentateuch afterwards, would insert such things as these for its confutation, or to raise suspicions of their forgery.

Another STANDING MIRACLE was the causing the land to be so extraordinarily fruitful every sixth year, that it produced enough for three years, Leviticus 24:20-21.

Another standing miracle was the effect of the bitter water, Numbers 5.

So much said in the history against the notion {that their} their FAULTS related in such a manner.

They had a regular, orderly history of their nation up to that very time, giving an account of the state of things from age to age, as gradually and naturally proceeding from that miraculous state of things which Moses gave account of.

'Tis abundantly manifest from that history of their nation, continued through all succeeding times, that they had this history of Moses in being among them from the very times that the history gives an account of.

The history is blended with the great Law, on which their whole constitution was built, so that the book of history and book of laws was the same.

UNIVERSAL. The whole constitution of the nation, in every part of it, civil and ecclesiastical, appears to be founded on the facts, and even the very being, and all the public and important things belonging to the nation; even their possession of their land, and possession of all their rights and privileges; their religion, and all the innumerable rites belonging to it; and the distinction of the tribes, and the division of the land among them, and the bounds of each tribe; and more than that, even the distinction of families, and particularly the extraordinary distinction and business of one whole tribe, even that of Levi, that were dispersed through all the nation, and yet were kept up distinct; and one remarkable family, even that of Aaron, also dispersed through the whole country in a very distinguishing business: all these were monuments and witnesses of the truth, as every law, every custom, every inheritance, the very being of the nation, and their possession of the country, and the names of the cities, everything belonging to the state of the nation, was a monument of the facts, and a standing witness of the truth of 'em.

UNIVERSAL. The things belonging to the religious and civil state and constitution of the nation, were so exceeding complicated, and withal so exceeding orderly, regular, exact and punctilious, and so steady and constant: as their elders, judges, officers, heads of thousands, hundreds, fifties, tens; the tribe of Levi, their divisions and services; the family of the priests, their exact order, their clothing, their beauteous, curious robes, wonderfully contrived; the tabernacle with its opulent utensils, so exceeding costly, exact, curious, convenient, useful and beautiful; and their rules and forms of worship; the services of the priests, of the Levites, of the whole nation, with their infinite varieties, without any confusion, all fixed and settled precisely without difference of different tribes; and all very different from all other nations, especially neighbor nations, so that it evidently had scarce anything that was akin to the customs of other people: I say, considering these things, there must be sometime wherein this constitution was made, and there must be some great establishment; and the establishment must be some very great and notable thing, by some remarkable authority, or some wonderful and strange and universal agreement of the nation.

And if so, it would be strange if nothing of it should be remembered among a nation, noted far above all nations in the world for keeping records of their affairs, herein vastly distinguished from all nations under heaven. And if there was some public account of the true fact, this would make it far more difficult for any imposters that should endeavor to palm on the nation a forged account of such an extremely strange original, altogether consisting of a series[^001-note23] of prodigious miracles: appearances from heaven, leading the whole nation through seas and rivers on dry ground, God's appearing from heaven for so long a time in thunder, lightning, fire and earthquake on a mountain, etc., etc., etc., when all was mere fiction and nothing else.

LAWS UNIVERSAL. The laws of the Jews affected their concerns much more universally than the laws of any people: in their bodies, that painful rite of circumcision; their clothes, wearing fringes, not woolen and linen; their meat and drink, only clean things specified, and a multitude of things are mentioned--unclean animals and unclean parts, blood and fat forbidden; their women in their diseases, their lying-in, their purifications; their time, a multitude of days and years consecrated, and to be observed after a particular manner; their marriages; their funerals; their daily necessary occasions to ease nature; their behavior; their disposal of their money; their lands, their building battlements on them; their cities and suburbs; the possessions of every tribe, every family; their conversation with neighbor nations; their conjugal intercourse, Leviticus 15:18; the accidental emission of seed, Leviticus 15:16; the feast of the new moon, for the beginning of every month; their weekly sabbath to be strictly kept every seventh day by a strict rest; their sabbatical years, every seventh year, Leviticus 25; their year of jubilee, Leviticus 25:9, etc., once in fifty years, and then an universal release of all servants, all debts, return of all men's lands to their original owners: things greatly affecting the state and concerns of the people in general.

Besides the many universal feasts, there was their strict yearly feast for the whole nation.

The yearly feasts were much more affecting the state of the nation than ordinary public feasts among other nations, in that the whole nation were to travel so far, and in some of the feasts were to spend so many days. The seventh month was, great part of it, taken up in holy days, wherein great things were to be done.

Everything they did within doors and without, at home and abroad, day and night, in their beds and when up, they must not live, move or breath as it were but by law.

These things, all put together, were more than if they had erected thousands of brazen or marble monuments, obelisks, pyramids in all parts of the land, and had been obliged to visit them once every month in the year.

The law of worshipping one God only under their circumstances, was a law that required constant care, concern, watchfulness and resistance of others, and so occasioning a continual, sensible impression on the mind.

The laws with respect to their marriages were very many; see Leviticus 18, Exodus 21:7, Exodus 21:11 and Exodus 22:16-17, Leviticus 21:7, Leviticus 21:13-15.

UNIVERSAL. Persons of all ranks, orders, and offices and relations, all sorts of societies and all nations, were regulated by that Law: kings, judges, officers; the great counsel of their nation; the heads of their tribes; the captains of thousands, hundreds, fifties and tens; the elders of cities; military officers; heads of families, husbands and wives, parents and children, mothers and servants. And particularly persons with respect to their private conduct; the concerns of the nation in general, their behavior in their general meetings or great congregation; their tribes, their cities, their armies; respecting other nations, strangers sojourning within their country, other nations in their own country.

UNIVERSAL. The division of the land, distinction of tribes and family, the business of the posterity of Aaron and the business of the Levites, with all those many civil and religious observances, which concerned and constantly employed everybody, and was so blended in all their concerns, must have always been by some written law or constitution. The observances were too many and complicated, and exact and precise, and long-maintained to be known and kept, so that the whole nation should agree in them for any long time without a written constitution. Among so many observances, maintained only by tradition, there would soon be innumerable variations, and one part of the people would have things one way, and others another, and all would quickly run into confusion. 'Tis plain to a demonstration that, in order to an uniformity, there must be standard that all could appeal to, and that all errors might be rectified by. If it be said all were regulated by the priests, that would not be sufficient without a written rule: for they lived distant one from another, in all parts of the country.

We see, as to those things that depend on report without a written account, how quickly they vary, and very innumerable differences,[^001-note24] as stories of strange events that are carried only by word of mouth and common fame.

And if there was a law, and a very particular law, appointing most of these things from the beginning, a law which all knew from generation to generation, which law had no reference to these facts, nor was dependent upon them: 'tis not likely that this law would be set aside for the sake of one agreeable to a new, forged history; or that it should be altered and have a set of new precepts added to it, requiring things to be performed by the whole nation, and having reference to the facts, with a pretence of those new precepts originally belonging to the law, and having been in it from the beginning; and this be done successfully so as to persuade the whole nation, from year to year, to comply with these new observances.

RECAPITULATION. A forgery of a great and wonderful fact pretended to be in SIGHT of a WHOLE NATION, every man, woman and child in it, is more easily detected than what is pretended to be seen only by a few; and a forgery published among that very nation, before whom the fact is pretended to be done, is more likely to be detected than that which was pretended to be before some other nation; and a forgery of such a fact pretended to be done on the spot where the forgery is first published, or in places in view, is more easily detected than of a fact pretended to be in some remote corner.

And a forgery of a fact pretended to be in the view of a whole nation--which fact, as it is related, vastly affects the state of the nation, and that not only for the present but their continued state, their perpetual constitution, and affects their state in every public and important interest--that all seems to be founded upon it, would be more difficulty palmed on a nation than a fact that was indifferent, and only is supposed to be seen and admired, and to excite the admiration of the nation,[^001-note57] but to have no lasting effect. And the forgery of a long series of such facts for fifty years together, all in sight of the whole nation, and all in the highest degree affecting them for the present, and in the highest degree and most universal manner affecting their state, and all their interests in all future generations, must be more difficultly imposed on a people than one single facts, or facts for a very short time.

And such an imposture is least likely to take effect with a nation used to the use of letters and records than other nations that had not these things, especially a nation that had the most exact and precise records of their nation up to the very times of the facts, even so precise that every man could tell his ancestors that lived at that very time; and especially must it be hard to palm such an imposture on that nation within so short a time, as within two hundred years of the time that many of the eyewitnesses lived, or were pretended to live. And especially facts which were the ground of all their laws, etc., etc. And it must be more difficult still to make a forgery of such facts take effect, that were pretended to be done not only in sight of that nation, but all nations round about, and the most considerable nations in the world, etc., etc., etc. Here observe many other particulars: every man's farm in such a case must be a kind of monument of the truth of the history, for the Law makes it impossible to be alienated; and almost everything[^001-note58] visible among such a people is a monument. And 'tis perhaps impossible to devise a state of things tending more to a continued, clear and certain evidence of a history.

If the history had been forged, the falsehood might have been the more easily detected for pretending to give so PARTICULAR an ACCOUNT of the whole state of the nation at the time: of their rulers, teachers, heads of tribes, the number of every tribe; all the important actions of the nation, such in particular as most affected the present and future state of the nation, the manner of their settlement in the land, the means and instruments, the cause and manner: introducing and establishing everything pertaining to the whole state of the nation, the names of the princes and heads of tribes which were appointed to divide the land, Numbers 34.

The after-history of their nation gives an account from time to time of facts that represent OTHER NATIONS as remembering and owning those facts, as the Ammonites that warred with Jephthah, and the Philistines when they took the ark.

Neither the history of the Pentateuch, nor the OTHER HISTORIES of the Jews in the Old Testament, were forged after the captivity. THREE GREAT FEASTS. What made it infinitely more difficult to introduce such a fraud into the nation gradually, and as it were secretly, and by little and little, was this thing which attended the state of the nation, viz. that the whole nation met together three times in a year. This prevented all opportunity to introduce this fraud, to creep about by degrees from one place to another, as sometimes false reports do, till in great length of time it become generally established, that nation were together three times in a year.

THREE GREAT FEASTS. Very much at some of their feasts, they were together eight or ten days; and then, when they were coming down and going, it was in companies. No nation in the world was so united by a public union. So that nothing of this nation could be kept secret for one half year; nothing could be established but by public, open establishment and agreement. They were all as one family, the whole acquainted with the affairs of all parts: no new thing, especially that greatly concerned the whole nation, could be set on foot and begun to be introduced, but it must be as it were with blowing a trumpet and proclaiming it about in the ears of the whole nation; and not only so, but the whole nation together having opportunity and necessity to consult one another, the poorer and weaker being under the eye and instruction of their great and wise men. This circumstance greatly tended to keep up the records of their nation with the greatest exactness, especially as to their religious affairs, and affairs greatly concerning the public as to its continued state.

This circumstance of these three great meetings of the nation every year, is considerable, that these meetings were about those very affairs mentioned in the history. According to the Law, they were to commemorate some of the principal of those very facts. Now, if we suppose the account of the facts forged, and the custom of thus meeting in being before the forgery, then the whole nation, coming together immediately upon it all at once, must have notice of the novelty; and this circumstance of it would naturally lead 'em to confer upon it at the meeting, that the novelty pretends to assign the end and prescribe the business of the meetings. But if no such meetings were in practice before, how hard a task indeed must the forgers have on their hands, to bring the nation to give such heed to their fiction as to make 'em believe their report of such strange facts, to such a degree as to induce the whole nation to meet three times a year, and so spend at least a twelfth part of the year in their meeting and in their journies, to commemorate such facts that were now forged, and they never heard of before.

Another argument that the history of the facts wrought by Moses was not forged, is that MOSES' POSTERITY never were very considerable in Israel. If the priests had forged the history, 'tis most likely they would have feigned the great things to be done by their ancestor; or if any other of their great and considerable men in authority, 'tis most likely they would have ascribed the most honorable things to their ancestor: but it was not so.

But 'tis not likely to be the forgery of any of the more considerable sorts[^001-note61] and families of people, because if they had done it, they would have been likely to have contrived it to have aggrandized their families chiefly. But it don't appear to be so, because the person that appears most honored in the history was not belonging to any of those families of note that had great influence on the people.

That the HISTORY of the Old Testament in general is a true RECORD of the affairs of the nation, and not some grand fiction, may be argued from these things: it was manifest, it was the manner of the people to keep writings and records among them; these are referred to in these histories, now extant, from time to time as then extant, and agreeable to these things, as the BOOK OF JASHER, the book of the WARS OF THE LORD, the book of the CHRONICLES of the Kings; we read of such as handled the pen of the writer in Deborah's time, and David kept his recorders, and so all the kings afterwards.

The HISTORY a part of the LAW. Some of the STATUTES of the Law are delivered in the FORM OF HISTORY; and though it be evident they had the force of perpetual statutes to all intents, yet they are nowhere delivered in the form of a statute, as the law forbidding the offering any other fire before the Lord but the fire that came from heaven, Leviticus 9:24 and Leviticus 10:1-3. GENEALOGY. It was the manner of the Jews, from the beginning, to keep accounts of their pedigree; see Numbers 1:18. 'Tis manifest by many passages in the Old Testament, not only that the distinction of tribes was kept, but the distinction of FAMILIES, called "the houses of their fathers";[^001-note64] and it seems that everyone knew what family he belonged to, which could not be but by particular genealogies of the pedigree of every person. This was one thing that made it looked upon so terrible a calamity, to have one's family extinct. This was one reason why God orders that if a man died having no seed, his brother should marry his widow, to "raise up seed to his brother" Matthew 22:24 and "build up his brother's house" Deuteronomy 25:9. In this notion of the terribleness of having a family extinct, lies the strength of the widow of Tekoah's plea, 2 Samuel 14:7, "and we will destroy the heir also: and so they shall quench my coal that is left, and shall not leave to my husband neither name nor remainder upon the earth." The remembrance of ancestors in a posterity that is known to be their posterity, and in a continual inheritance, is called their NAME often in Scripture. They were exceeding careful that their name in this respect should be upheld: thus the daughters of Zelophehad say, Numbers 27:4, "Why should the name of our father be done away from among his family, because he hath no son? Give unto us therefore a possession among the brethren of our father." The extinguishing of a man's name in this respect, is spoken of as a terrible judgment, Psalms 109:13, "Let his posterity be cut off; and in the generation following let his name be blotted out"; Isaiah 14:22, "For I will rise[^001-note65] up against them, saith the Lord of hosts, and cut off from Babylon the name, and remnant, and son, and nephew, saith the Lord"; Deuteronomy 25:6, "shall succeed in the name of his brother which is dead, that his name be not put out of Israel"; v. HISTORY was CONTAINED IN THE LAW. Psalms 119:18, "Open thou mine eyes, that I may behold wondrous things out of thy law." There is great reason to think that what David chiefly intends by "wondrous things," are the wondrous works of God written in the book of the Law, and that this is a prayer that he may rightly apprehend those wondrous works. The Psalmist observes, Psalms 106:7, "Our fathers understood not thy wonders in Egypt," etc. Here he prays that it may not be so with him; and Moses observes, Deuteronomy 29:2-4, "Ye have seen all that the Lord did before your eyes in the land of Egypt unto Pharaoh, and unto all his servants, unto all his land; the great temptations which thine eyes have seen, the signs, and those great miracles: yet the Lord hath not given you an heart to perceive, and ears to hear, and eyes to see, unto this day." Here the Psalmist prays that it may not be so with him, but that his eyes may be opened, that he may have eyes to see, that he may understand God's wonders.

The word translated "wonders" is *Niphlaoth*. I find this word, for the most part, signifies "wonderful works" through out the Old Testament; and it is generally so translated in this book of Psalms, as Psalms 9:1, Psalms 26:7, and Psalms 40:5, and Psalms 71:17, and Psalms 72:18, and Psalms 75:1, and Psalms 86:10, and Psalms 96:3, and Psalms 98:1 and Psalms 107:8, Psalms 107:15, Psalms 107:21, Psalms 107:24, Psalms 107:31. Yea, it very commonly is used to signify the wonderful work written in the Pentateuch, Psalms 78:4, Psalms 78:11, Psalms 78:32; and Psalms 105:2, Psalms 105:5; and Psalms 106:7, Psalms 106:22; and Psalms 136:4, and Psalms 145:5 and Psalms 119:27. The place last mentioned much confirms this interpretation of this 78th verse of the 119th Psalm, and also confirms the thing I would prove by it, viz. that the history of the facts was in what they called the Law. In that 27th verse of the same Psalm, the Psalmist says, "Make me to understand the way of thy precepts: so shall I talk of thy wondrous works"; that is, "Open my eyes that I may rightly understand thy way revealed in thy Law, and then I shall be suitably affected with thy wondrous works: and so shall I discourse of," or "meditate on" [^001-note66], "thy wonderful work written in that Law."

When the Psalmist says in the 129th verse of the same psalm, "Thy testimonies are wonderful: therefore my soul keepeth them," 'tis reasonable to suppose that what he calls God's testimonies, which he says are wonderful, are not only the precepts of the Law but the revelations of it, including history and prophecy of the wonderful works of God.

All the history from the beginning of the world is one entire, consistent, natural whole, with a mutual relation, respect and correspondence of parts, as a tree or animal body, so that 'tis hard putting in parts and counterfeit pieces. The whole can't be counterfeit: none will pretend that there was no such nation as the Jews carried into Babylon, etc. And 'tis hard counterfeiting the root and stock of a tree for the real history, as branches to be set on, so as to make the whole like a natural tree, so but that a narrow search would soon discern the disagreement and ill connection.

Ezra was a very likely person to be acquainted with the ancient writings and records of Israel, being the son of Seraiah the high priest. Compare Ezra 2, at the beginning, with 1 Chronicles 6:14 with the preceding verses. The religious Jews in the captivity would repair to the priests for instruction in these things, and especially to the high priest and such as were next to him; and his brother Ezra, giving his mind greatly to these things, he must have all possible advantage for knowledge.

Insist on this argument of the impossibility of forging sacred writings among the Jews after the CAPTIVITY, viz. that whether the Jews had SYNAGOGUES before the captivity or no, yet 'tis beyond dispute that after the captivity, long before Christ's time, they had synagogues everywhere, all over the land, and all over the world, and everywhere it was the established custom to read all their sacred writings, the Law and the Prophets. It could not be extensively and universally, and with so full agreement established and settled, as it was in Christ's and the apostles' times, if something like it had not very long been a custom, at least in the land of Canaan.

That the Jews that remained in other countries, in all parts of the world, AFTER THE CAPTIVITY, held so much of one mind and in one way as to religion, is an argument that they kept a number of the SACRED BOOKS among 'em, even from the time of the captivity, and that they were not new writing or books sent among 'em afterwards from Judea, writing that they were unacquainted with before.

CAPTIVITY. 'Tis evident the prophet Daniel had the Law of God and the writings of the Prophets in his hand. 'Tis manifest the book of the prophet Daniel was not forged after the captivity.

'Tis not likely they would forge that prophecy of the Messiah in the 9th of Daniel, and that destruction to their country that should follow, as Stapferus observes.[^001-note79] There never was any controversy about the authenticity of this book, though Daniel 12:2 is very contrary to the sect of the Sadducees.

If it had been forged after the fulfillment of most of the events--as after the death of Antiochus Epiphanes--the defects in the Hebrew and the exceeding difference of the time, the temper, manners, etc., would have occasioned many things to be in the book that would have discovered the fallacy, it being about 400 years. There would have been many disagreements in chronology, and many inconsistencies with true history and other records of the Jews, that by an accurate examination might have been plainly discovered.

And how exceeding unlikely that, in those late times, this book could have been added to the canon and accepted by the Jews, who were exact and careful, even to superstition, about their canon. And then the sect of the Sadducees subsisted, who would never have received such a book as it is, with ch. 12:2.

Besides, the death of Antiochus Epiphanes is not like enough to account for the exact fulfillment of the prophecies: for nothing is more exactly fulfilled than the prophecy of the Roman Empire.

METHOD. 1. To show that the children of Israel, before the captivity into Babylon, did dwell in the land in that division of tribes, under those laws, and in general in that state in which they are represented to have dwelt there, in the books of the Old Testament in general.

The state, as to a great part of it, would be remembered by many then alive. How, show what parts.

2. That that history of their nation that we have after Moses' time, was a history they had in esteem among them before the captivity as their true history, and was in general a true history; and those Psalms, Proverbs and prophecies,[^001-note80] etc., were no counterfeit thing, as pretended to be of a different date and different original from what they truly were; and the Pentateuch in general was what they called the Law of Moses, and was handed down as such from ancient time.

METHOD. The first point to be proved: that the Jews had those writings before the captivity that are pretended to be written before the captivity.

Wherein[^001-note81] the revelation made by Nehemiah differed from that by Christ. It was not so happy a revelation.

It was by no means so great a revelation, considering the state the world was in before, especially if we measure[^001-note82] the greatness by the degree of good and positive effect; nor in any respect, if we consider the degenerate state the Christian world was in before Mahomet.

Infinitely more wonderful revelation if we consider the means.

Mahometanism might be by nature. .. the religion is more agreeable to nature.

EZRA DID NOT FORGE THE JEWISH SCRIPTURES AFTER THE CAPTIVITY. About Ezra's forging all the Jewish scriptures, pretended to be written before the captivity: how vast a work would it have been; how impossible in those days, when writing was difficult and slow in the character, and with their materials, in comparison of what it now is; how great a thing, to invent all these histories and put 'em all together, in such connection, with Psalms, songs, Proverbs and so many prophecies relating to innumerable things in the history, infinite ways connected with it: how could it be done without betraying the fraud in ways innumerable, by multitudes of inconsistencies, things spoken disagreeing with the time, etc., etc.? And the style is apparently different. There must have been vast labor in each part, to make it appear so much like real history: to mention the names of men and names of places, the particular, minute circumstances, to imitate nature so much, represent the working of their tempers, to invent speeches and deeds so worthy of God, so agreeable to the divine nature and majesty.

To cover all with a semblance of so much simplicity and sincerity, in representing[^001-note83] the faults of the people, and the faults of the best and most eminent and renowned of their prophets, princes, etc. To make all agree with their genealogies, to invent the genealogies in Genesis and other places. To make all parts of the geography agree. To invent the chronology.

To connect all with that part of the history that was within memory.

To invent all the knowledge and wisdom discovered in Psalms, Job, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes and in the Prophets, and the poetical and prophetic, exalted style.

What should move him to invent such a book as Solomon's Song?

Why did he invent such short prophecies as Obadiah?

Why such seeming differences as between Chronicles and Kings?

Why such a book as Ruth invented?

How diverse the style of different boks: Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Isaiah, Hosea and other short Prophets, Amos.

Writing so many verses beginning with the different letters of the alphabet, as in the 119th Psalm and Lamentations.

The impossibility of forging the books of Jeremiah and Ezekiel, because men's memories must confute them.

And if they were not forged, there would be the difficulty of connecting all the preceding history with them, which they allude to in almost all parts of it.

To have invented one such book as the book of Job, if it could be done at all, would have taken up great part of the life of such a man in that age, when the means of education to prepare men for it, were such as they were.

And so of the book of Psalms and Proverbs.

To have composed and woven and connected such a frame as the Pentateuch, with the various histories, revelations, doctrine, laws, etc., would have been a vast work indeed, filling up an age.

To make all the prophecies in the books of Moses to agree well with facts.

Would have made the prophecies more plain, to agree with facts.

Would not have put in any prophecies beyond his own time, as those of Balaam respecting ships from Chittim.

And the blessing of Judah respecting Shiloh's coming.

A vast work, to have invented the frame of the tabernacle so minutely, and the work of the Levites in carrying it.

That prophetical song, Deuteronomy 32.

If Ezra had not been of good character, 'tis not likely he would have been approved by the king as a man of good character, one liked and approved as such among his own people, and them that knew him.

And if he had been so grand a rogue, it is not likely he would have obtained so good a character, and be so well spoken of by Nehemiah, and so much trusted by the king and the people, who were a serious, honest generation.

How different a style is the book of Ecclesiastes from most other books, and how diverse a genius does it manifest. How unlikely a thing that a book of that great wisdom and depth of philosophy, should only be the fiction of a vile, rascally fellow; and so the book of Proverbs.

Who can suppose all these natural strains of elevated devotion, with so vast a variety, suiting such different occasions, different circumstances and different frames, and different times and persons as the book of Psalms, should be, with all the rest, a fiction of a deceitful imposter brought up in Babylon?

The plain difference of style between the books of Chronicles and the other Historical books before Ezra and Nehemiah, show that Ezra did not write both that and others.

If the books of the Prophets had been forged after the captivity by one of the Jews, they would have been written in a very different manner; and particularly, the prophecies there found of the restoration from captivity would have been quite otherwise composed than they are. There would have been more of a literal, plain agreement. The prophecies would have been more in the language of history, and like the history of their return, mentioning the particulars which Ezra saw, describing them after the manner of men, and such things concerning the Messiah and his kingdom: would not have been so blinded with the restoration from Babylon, as if they had been one event, and all to be accomplished at the same time; as the prophecies in the latter part of Isaiah from the 40th chapter, and many of the prophecies of Jeremiah, and the latter part of the Ezekiel from the 34th chapter to the end, with the description of such a temple, etc., so very diverse from that which Ezra saw built, and such a division of the land, etc.

It is sometimes spoken of as a great work of God, to deliver Abraham and Israel from idolatry, Joshua 22; and so when they returned from the captivity.

It is spoken of as a strange, unheard of thing, that a nation should change their gods.

It is manifest that the Jews in Jerusalem had the Law of Moses among them long before Ezra came up to Jerusalem, and probably before Ezra was born, by Ezra 3:2, Ezra 3:4 and Ezra 6:18, and Haggai 2:11-13, Zechariah 3:7.

And it appears that the Jews in Jerusalem had the book of the Prophets among them long before Ezra came thither, by Zechariah 1:4-6 and Zechariah 7:7.

And about eleven years after Ezra went up to Jerusalem, Nehemiah, the king's cupbearer at Shushan, had the Law of Moses there as a book he was well acquainted with; see Nehemiah 1:7-9.

"PAPER ON FREE WILL"

OF FREE WILL. A promise, the condition of which is not capable of being understood, is no promise at all. A promise is a signification of some some future good to the persons to whom the promise is made; if there be no signification thereof, there is no promise. For God merely to intend to show good within his own breast is no promise. 'Tis no promise till that design is signified or made known. And therefore words, the meaning of which are not capable of being understood, can be no promise: for they are no signification of anything; they make nothing known. The design of the supposed promiser still remains as much within his own breast as they did before. His design is no way signified: for though there are words spoken or letters written, yet they are no promise anymore than any other sounds or letters; and that for this reason: because they ben't capable of being understood or of having any intelligible meaning; or, which is the same thing, they are words without signification, and therefore can be no promise. They bring God under no obligation to the creature, for these sounds and letters are not spoken to man: for that which is in its own nature uncapable of being understood by man, is not spoken to man, because what is spoken to man is spoken to man's understanding. It is not mere sounds of a supposed promise, or marks upon paper, as such, that binds him that causes them; but 'tis the meaning of those marks or sounds that obliges, or what is signified by them to him to whom they are directed that binds and obliges him that causes them; and therefore, if they are not capable of signifying anything to his understanding, then to him they have no signification or meaning at all, and therefore can't bring the supposed promiser under any obligation to him

A[^003-note32] promise is a signifier of some future good to the person to whom the promise is made; and therefore, a conditional promise is a signification of some future good to be bestowed by the promiser, together with the signification of the condition of the promise. And therefore if there be no signification of the promises, if the condition of the promise is hid, the promise is not revealed; or, which is the same thing, is not made. If there be a design of bestowing good upon condition, yet there is no promise till that design is revealed. But if there be sounds or marks that ben't capable of being understood, [there is no promise].

If a man should meet another alone, and through mistake should speak to him in a language that he did not understand, and knew not a word of, intentionally making some promise to him: such a promise brings the promiser under no obligation, because 'tis no[^003-note33] signification of his mind to the other any more than if he had held his peace; and his design, notwithstanding such unknown words, is still kept to himself as much as before. But especially if his words were in themselves uncapable of an intelligible meaning.

But 'tis absurd to suppose that God would make a promise that was in its own nature uncapable of being understood. The end of all speech is to signify something. 'Tis therefore blasphemous to suppose that God would direct himself to us in that speech that was[^003-note34] not capable of signifying any[thing], or that could have no intelligible meaning.

To show what encouragement there is given to natural man's seeking, and how this is as much as can be in reason expected.

*Obj*. That text, "Ask, [and it shall be given you]," and "[every one that asks] receives," and will he not "give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?" [Luke 11:9-13].

*Ans*. 1. That it evidently intends the prayers of children by Christ's argument: which of you, "if his son ask [of] him bread, will give a stone? or if he ask a fish, will he for a fish give him a serpent?" [Luke 11:11].

*Ans*. [2]. No promise[^003-note35] made to any prayer but [the] prayer of faith. "Let him ask in faith, nothing wavering" [Jas. 1:6].

Prayers without faith and sincerity are no prayers at all indeed. They don't pray to God. Show how [they are] no prayers. And 'tis unreasonable therefore to suppose that in Scripture account there are no prayers [without faith and sincerity].

Again, 'tis unreasonable to suppose absolute promises made to faithless prayers: for to what purpose are promises made to such who, in their prayers, have no faith to depend on those promises?

'Tis plain that it is intended the prayer of faith by many places. "But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering" [Jas. 1:6].

When it is said, ask and receive, they therefore will allow that it is not asking. A man may ask in words in a very careless manner, yea, in a very profane and impudent manner: nobody will suppose the promise made to such asking as this. So that they themselves are forced not to stick to what is implied in the literal meaning of the words; and therefore, 'tis very unfair in them to argue from their literal meaning. Yea, they must allow that is not meant such asking as any wicked man whatsoever is capable of, or else the forementioned absurdities will follow.

'Tis unreasonable to think that promises are made to those who have faith to depend on those promises. Why should promises be made to those who are in such a state that don't allow of any believing use of the promise? Promises are made to faith: for God to make such promises to them that are in such a state, is incompatible with any faith. To believe and depend on the promises, or hope in the promises, is as absurd as to speak to them that have no ears. What is speech for, but that those that are spoken to might hear it? and what are promises for, but that those to whom the promises are made might believe them, depend on them, hope in them?

Again, if absolute promises of saving blessings are made to men that have no godliness, then either men may know that those promises belong to them while they are yet ungodly, or not. If not—if they can't know that they are made to them while ungodly—to what purpose are they made to them, if they can't know it? To what purpose do promises belong to them that, while they remain in that state, can't know it?

But if they can and sometimes do know that absolute promises belong to them while yet ungodly, see what consequences will follow.

A great argument of theirs to prove that God has put the condition of salvation within our reach, is that it would be unjust in God to require that of us, under pain of his wrath, that is not within our present power, or the power that we have in our present state since the fall. But this argument they themselves confute and overthrow in their own scheme: for they say that we in our present state ben't able to perfect performance, or that we can perform no other than an imperfect obedience; and yet that Christ died to satisfy for the imperfections of our obedience, that so our imperfect obedience may be accepted. Now how can these things hang together? If we can't perform any other than an imperfect obedience in our present state, and it ben't just in God to punish us for want of performing such an obedience as we can't perform in our present state, then what need of Christ's dying to satisfy for the imperfections of our obedience? What need of Christ's dying to satisfy for that which don't deserve to be punished? If our failing of perfect obedience don't deserve to be punished, or that God can't justly punish, then there is no need of Christ's suffering to satisfy for it. What need Christ suffer to satisy for that that is no fault, or that in its own nature deserves no suffering?

It may be they will answer that Christ died to satisfy the first law for us, which law required death, that so we might be at liberty from it, to be put under a new law that requires only sincere obedience.

I answer: how could Christ die to satisfy the first law for us, when, according to their scheme, we never broke that law? For according to them, we never broke that law in any sense. We never broke that law under Adam, for they say Adam was not our representative, and the guilt of his breaking of the law don't belong to us. And we never broke it in our persons, for according to their scheme we never were under it: and how can we break a law that we were never under? And therefore, how could Christ die to satisfy the law for us, who never broke it, nor even were under it?

It may be they will answer, Christ died to satisfy the law for us that we might not be under the law, and that we might be delivered from it. But what need of Christ's dying that we might not be under the law, when, according to their scheme, 'twould in itself be unjust that we should be under that law, because in our present state we are not able to keep it? What need therefore of Christ's dying, that we might not be under a law that it would be unjust that we should be under, whether Christ died or no?

To show upon how many accounts 'tis fit that absolute promises should not be made to the ungodly.

They say, it would be unjust for God to require that of us as the condition of salvation that is not within our present power, and that it is not just with God to punish that that we now, with our present power, cannot avoid.

And they allow that man in his fallen state cannot avoid sin, for they allow that a man now cannot perform a perfect sinless obedience. And yet they say that Christ died to satisfy for that sin, or that sinful imperfection of obedience. Now how do these things consist? What need of Christ's dying to satisfy for that which deserves no punishment, or that it would be in itself unjust for God to punish?

Concerning[^003-note36] absolute promises made to the ungodly, see Mr. Dickinson's *Five Discourses*, beginning p. 157. And concerning freedom of will, see three or four of the following pages, and also in his "Discourse on Election."[^003-note37]

Here to observe, that they that do a thing is no excuse, unless they would if they could, etc.

See the Paper on Justification, nos. 2-4, the argument there used,[^003-note38] whether it holds good against Arminians or no, who hold that godliness is not attained till persons have well improved both common and special grace; yet it holds good against those who own that godliness is wholly the gift of God, but yet God has obliged himself to bestow it on those that ask it, has obliged himself to such asking as natural men are capable of. Then it will certainly follow that that asking wherein there is no godliness is the proper condition of justification and salvation.

If anything that a natural man does gives him a right to an absolute promise of the saving grace of [God], then how can the godly only be the heirs of the promises, when here is one that is ungodly that is an heir of a promise, not only of temporal and finite blessings, but of a saving blessing, a blessing of infinite consequence, and where that doth wholly imply in it all other promises that the godly themselves are entitled to? So that by this indeed, an ungodly man becomes an heir of all the promises of the godly then, even while ungodly: for he can challenge he has God under obligation to bestow them; he that has God under a positive obligation to bestow that to which he has promised all other saving blessing, has God ipso facto under obligation to him to bestow all other saving blessings, and is truly an heir of the promises.

To hold that salvation is within a man's reach at any time, in such a sense that by doing what is properly in his own power, God may become absolutely obliged to bestow salvation upon him, though it be never so late, and though it be after never so much and so willful sins against never so great convictions of conscience: what doctrines can possibly more tend to harden men in wickedness and encourage them to give themselves their full swing? Would it not immediately have that effect? If this be thoroughly believed by all wicked men, how do these often flatter themselves and encourage themselves in willful sin, with hopes of future repentance, even though they are taught no such doctrine, but are taught the contrary doctrine?

Yea, it would have a direct tendency to encourage and embolden men in sin, if they were only taught that they might commit a few or only one willful sin, and yet when they have done have pardon in their own power.

But if this can't with just foundation be told to any man, that they may commit one willful sin against his conscience and yet have pardon within his reach, then by a parity it can't be said to them that have committed one willful sin. If I but with[out] good reason tell a man beforehand, that if he commits such a sin against his light that he may obtain pardon notwithstanding, then neither can I tell him so after he has committed it with any just foundation. But if no man that has ever committed any one willful sin can lay hold on that promise, who is there that can?

This is to make [out] that the condition of salvation should be that which is no more virtue than what a man may have without special grace: for the condition, according to them, is only the exercise of man's natural ability. If it be the improvement of common and special grace, there is no more virtue in using one's utmost natural ability in the improvement of special grace than common; if the virtue lies only in a man's diligent use of his own powers, they may be as diligently used in improving common grace as special. First, lay it down that, according to their scheme, the thing attained by endeavors is not the condition; but the endeavors themselves, the virtue that there is in using those endeavors, must be the condition, and not the faith in Christ or repentance or love to God that is attained, or any holy act.

How dishonorable it would be for God to be bound in such causes, see my second sermon on that text in Romans, "Every mouth shall be stopped" [Rom. 3:19].[^003-note39]

All the promises of saving good are included in the covenant of grace, but none are entitled to the promises of the covenant of grace but they that have performed the conditions of the covenant of grace. But there is nothing that a natural man does, while a natural man, that is the condition of the covenant of grace.

SCRIPTURES:

Rom. 9:16, "'Tis not of him that willeth nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy."

So 'tis said, "Who maketh thee to differ" [I Cor. 4:7]; whereas the difference has its original with man himself.

How is it possible that anything should be more evident than that the foundation of that distinction that there is between one man and another, whereby one is godly and another not godly, is with God, and that it is owing to his pleasure? Matt. 11:25-27, "At that time Jesus rejoiced in spirit and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes." Here the distinction is spoken of, viz. that some know those things and others know them not. Here, first, this distinction is attributed to God, as to him that caused it to be: for those that know it, know it because God revealed it; and the others know it, not because God hid it from them. 'Tis spoken of as what God caused.[^003-note40] Second, God is spoken of as the cause. There was this distinction between such persons, as there was why the wise and prudent had it not and on the contrary that babes had it. Third, this is more evident still, because Christ thanks the Father for this. Fourth, 'tis evident from the next words that this is owing to the mere pleasure of God: "Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight" [Matt. 11:26]. Fifth, 'tis evident from the words that follow,[^003-note41] that this distinction here spoken [of] is to be attributed to God as it relates to particular persons and not only to kinds or sorts of men: "No man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man [the] Father, but the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him."

The Scripture certainly seems to hold forth that the grace of God, or kindness of God to us, makes us to differ from others. But if God be obliged to bestow it upon our performance of what we do of ourselves, there is no particular kindness exercised to him that receives grace and is godly, no kindness in his receiving saving grace but what is common to all and to those that don't receive.

If we obtain the saving grace of God by virtue of an obligation that God has laid himself under to us, in common with the rest of mankind, his grace ceases to be of the nature of a present. By a "present" we mean something given in token of particular kindness to the person to whom it is given. But if this be the case, there is no kindness to him that actually receives God's grace but what is common to the whole race of mankind; 'tis no token of any particular benevolence to me. The kindness is in coming under obligation on such conditions, if anywhere; but that kindness is to all equally. There is no more of any gift or present in the case to a godly man than to an ungodly. But neither is this any kindness, according to their notion.

That persons entertain an opinion that God has obliged himself by absolute promises to what unconverted men can do, to bestow his saving grace upon them, without doubt generally arises from an apprehension in their own minds that it would be hard in God ever to refuse it; or, which is the same thing, that God is obliged to bestow it. And if so, what sort of promises are these? They ben't free promises; they are not promises of any free grace, but only God promises that which he is obliged to do, and which we could charge him with doing wrong if he should fail of. And therefore, what obligations to praise and thankfulness are [persons] under to him for those promises? And if this be so, there is no free grace in the case, either in the giving the promise or giving the thing promised: for if God ben't obliged to give the thing promised, neither is he obliged to make the promise. The whole gospel scheme of free grace is hereby overthrown, for by "free grace" in the gospel is surely meant that which God is not obliged to, which he might, without dealing any way unfairly or hardly, or in any respect wrongly, refuse. Debt and grace are opposed in Scripture as destroying one another.

And though perhaps they may say that though God ben't obliged to make any offer of salvation, yet seeing he does make men offers, it would be but an illusion to offer it when at the same time they can't attain it nor do anything whereby God is obliged to bestow it, and that the offer seems to oblige God to give it when they do what they can: I answer, that God offers it no otherwise than he does. If he offers it on what it is in their power to do, then God is obliged; but if he only reveals that they shall have it on such conditions which he directs them to seek, but at the same time reveals himself to be the sovereign bestower of it, then his offer don't oblige .

And whatever persons pretend, without doubt 'tis a secret apprehension of some goodness or worthiness in what natural men can do; that that is the thing that secretly prejudices and inclines them (though perhaps insensibly) to receive this doctrine, or at least a want of a sense of the miserable and sinful vile pollution of all that they do. If this were seen, it would not seem hard that God should make no absolute promises to it; yea, it would convince that it would not be suitable that God should oblige himself to it. And this doctrine is very dangerous on that account, because it tends greatly to confirm persons in an opinion of the worthiness of what they do, which they are so naturally inclined to.

Concerning[^003-note42] the NECESSITY of the mighty power of God to convert the soul and the IRRESISTABLENESS, or rather "victorious efficacy," of that grace in opposition to the effect's being suspended on men's free will, see the matter excellently cleared in Mr. Clark of Salem Village his sermon on that subject, in a sermon at the public lecture at Boston that I borrowed of Mr. Bull, beginning p. 15 to p. 22, of which some of the words are these:[^003-note43]

*First*. There is a necessity of a divine power and grace in the conversion of a sinner which appears,

1. From the nature of man in his fallen degenerate state.... Our Savior proves the necessity of the regenerating grace of the Spirit, in order to our being restored to the spiritual life, and to an inheritance in the kingdom of God, from the universal corruption and carnality of man's nature. John 3:6-7, "That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Marvel not" (therefore adds he) "that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again."... Without the restoring grace of the Redeemer, man fallen is not only impotent, but full of enmity against holiness and the duties of the spiritual life. [...] Rom. 8:7, "The carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be." [...] And how can such a degenerate creature, renew himself by repentance, or convert himself to God, without the special influences of divine grace?

2. It appears by the nature of that work of grace, by which this change is effected. It is called the workmanship of God, and ascribed to his creating power [...]. Eph. 2:10, "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works." Ch. 4:24, "That ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness." 'Tis also termed regeneration, or a begetting us again. Jas. 1:18, "Of his own will begat he us by the word of truth." I Pet. 1:3, "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again."... This is ascribed entirely to God, exclusive of all other causes. John. 1:13, "Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of men, but of God." And if such a change as amounts to a new birth, is necessary to the conversion of a sinner, surely man cannot be the author of it himself, no more than he may be said to beget himself at first; but the power and grace of God is necessary to the production of it. [...]

*Secondly*. There is a victorious efficacy in the grace of God, which being exerted shall infallibly convert a sinner, even the most obstinate and hardened sinner. "*Turn thou me*," and the effect shall answer the virtue of the cause, "*and I shall be turned*" [Jer. 31:18]. So Jer. 17:14, "Heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved.... As for God, his work is perfect; if he undertake any saving work, he can and will bring it to effect. Though the sinner may resist for a while, yet he shall not finally do so, but shall be made to yield at last in the day of God's power with him.... Yet some there are, who suppose, that when God has done all that he has promised, and furnished a sinner with sufficient aids of grace, whereby he may be converted if he will, it is at last left on the free will of man, whether he will be converted or no, and that he may finally resist the operations of divine grace. If so, then instead of saying, "Turn me, and I shall be turned," it may be said, "Though thou turnest me, I may never be effectually turned." But the contrary of this is manifest from Scripture.

1. Because the regeneration and conversion of a sinner is expressly ascribed to the will of God, and as expressly denied to the will of man. In the forecited, John 1:13, believers in Christ are said to be "born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God." The new birth is not the product of the will of man, but of the will of God. Jas. 1:18, "Of his own will begat he us." Again, Rom. 9:15-16, "He saith to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion." His inference hence is, so then, it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy, and that according to his own sovereign will. The phrase, "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy," as it speaks the absolute freedom of the divine will, so it imports the irresistable efficacy of it. If God will have mercy on any soul, nothing in the world shall hinder it; for there is a powerful efficacy in the will of God, he works all things according to "the counsel of his own will" (Eph. 1:11). He works them effectually (as the word signfies) with a potent, irresistable energy. If any are converted and saved, it is not of man that willeth originally, but of God that willeth and worketh according to his will.

2. It is ascribed to the mighty power of God, even to that power to which all things are possible. When our Savior had laid open the difficulties of salvation, or of entering into the kingdom of God, Mark. 10:26-27, the disciples were exceedingly surprized, and being "astonished out of measure," saith the Evangelist, said among themselves, "Who then can be saved? Jesus looking upon them said, With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible." He speaks it indeed, with reference to the Conversion and salvation of rich men, but the same is true of all sinners wedded to their lusts. The young man that came to Christ with that question, "Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?" [Mark 10:17] (on which occasion those words were spoken by our Savior), showed as good a disposition to the kingdom of God, as any mere natural man may ordinarily be supposed to do, yet it required the almighty power of divine grace to bring him into it, and to subdue his heart and will to the grace and government of Christ; and when this power is put forth, the most obstinate shall be made to yield to it. 'Tis not the preaching of an apostle, but the power of God accompanying it, that works faith (I Cor. 2:5), and captivates the souls of men to an obedient subjection to Christ (II Cor. 10:4-5). And this power of God to which all things are possible, is infinitely greater than that which is in the devil, or the corrupt nature of man to make resistance.

3. God has promised by his grace, to take away the principle of enmity and resistance in the carnal will; and herein lies the work of his grace principally, in subduing the obstinacy of men's hearts, in curing the will and affections of their carnality. Deut. 30:6, "The Lord thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the Lord thy God with all thine heart," etc. Ezek. 36:26, "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh & I will give you an heart of flesh. now what is it that may be supposed to resist, or frustrate the Operations of grace but the heart of stone or the perverse obstinacy of the will? but this God has promised shall be taken away by the prevailing efficacy of his grace.

4. Because in the work of grace, the will is renewed and powerfully inclined towards God; as the Scriptures clearly testify. Ps. 110. 3, "Thy people shall be willing" (or become thy willing subjects) "in the day of thy power. Philip. 2:13, "For it is God that worketh in you both to will and to do of his own good pleasure." John 6:44, "No man can come unto me, except the Father which hath sent me drew him" (saith Christ), which shows the necessity of divine grace; and our Savior's inference (v. 45, "Every man therefore that hath heard, and learned of the Father, cometh unto me), shows the victorious efficacy. If the Father draws, by the word and Spirit, the sinner infallibly comes to Christ.

5. Because of the absurd consequences of the contrary opinion, that leaves the work of conversion at last to the self-determining power of man's free will, for then the great and glorious designs God had in view, in the work of man's redemption by his Son Jesus Christ, would appear to have been built on a precarious bottom; then the decrees of God might be frustrated, and his intentions defeated, contrary to that declaration, Is. 46:10, "My counsel shall stand, and I will fulfill all my pleasure"; then God's promise to Christ of a numerous seed to serve him, is grounded on an uncertainty, [...] the fruits of his death and undertaking depending on the slippery and inconstant free will of man; then the elect have no more special grace than the reprobate, the saved than the damned; then the regenerate man may share in the glory of his salvation, if it be the good use of free will, and not the good work of free grace, that makes him to differ from those that perish.

That[^003-note44] duty which is not done in sincerity, is not done at all in the sight of God, who looks at the heart and not at bodily motions or external sounds. If there be no sincere prayer, or that prayer which is the voice of faith, there is no prayer to God at all in God's sight: for 'tis the voice of the heart and the voice of faith that God looks at, and 'tis unreasonable to suppose that the promises made to prayer are made to any other prayer than Christian prayer, the prayer of the heart, prayer as the act of an intelligent creature and not of a machine. 'Tis real prayer, and not a mere pretense of prayer. See sermon on Ps. 27:4, in the answer to the objection under the second inference.[^003-note45]

To handle that argument from our prayers to God, that he would grant that we may do thus or thus. How absurd this is: if the thing that determines whether we shall have or do such things or no be our own free will independently, and not to be dependent on any determination of God's, our very praying that such a thing may be, supposes that it depends on God's determination.

To deny a disposing or governing providence over the wills and so the acts of the intelligent world, is to deny the chief part of providence, the main of God's disposing government of the world, and to take away the glory and crown of providence: for what is his disposal of the actions and motions of brute creatures and the inanimate creation in comparison of this? 'Tis in a great measure to dethrone God. Indeed, it leaves him his moral government of the world, but it takes away the chief of his disposing government. Yea, in effect it takes away all absolute decrees whatsoever with respect to future events: for the main events, those that concern the acts of intelligent creatures, being undetermined and so yet remaining contingent, not determined by anything that is as yet, but remaining yet to receive their first determination from men themselves, this makes it necessary that an absolute determination about lesser events should be suspended. 'Tis those main events are determined, because 'tis needfull that the lesser events should be ordered one way or another according as the greater events shall be. The main events must govern the lesser, and if the lesser should be absolutely determined before the greater are, God may be disappointed: for those greater events still remaining contingent, they may come out so as to make it necessary that the lesser events should be ordered otherwise, that this may be accommodated one to another and that the compages and series of things may be consistent with itself. Without doubt, the habitation is made for its inhabitants, as the inferior, inanimate and irrational part of the universe is made for the intelligent part that inhabits it; and therefore 'tis necessary that events in the parts of the habitation should be accommodated to the state of the inhabitants. If God has any design by the events and changes in the various parts of the habitation, it must be something relating to intelligent inhabitants, for no end can be obtained any other way. If the universe had no inhabitatants, and there were nothing but unperceiving beings, it is easy to see that no end could be obtained by any motions or events on it: no end could be obtained, for nothing capable of good could in any respect be the better or worse for them in any respect, nor any glory of God manifested, no good shown, nor any perceived. And therefore it will follow that all events in the habitation could [not] have any end but with respect to the inhabitants, and therefore alterations and changes can't be wisely ordered but with respect to that end, or as accommodated to the state of the inhabitants. Therefore, if those alterations are absolutely predetermined, 'tis necessary that that state of intelligent creatures should also be predetermined. There is no one event in the inferior, brutal or inanimate world that we read of in the Scriptures or any other history that can be pitched upon as a predetermined event but what some way or other so concerns the state of intelligent inhabitants, that it was absolutely necessary that the state of those inhabitants should be predetermined in order to its being absolutely predetermined with wisdom; and but that if the state of intelligent beings that were concerned in it was undetermined, it would be evidently most wise to suspend the determination of that event. Especially is it so with respect to all important events: and if we deny that any important events are predetermined of God, then surely there is nothing left that it is important to contend about; if we deny that ever [God] absolutely decrees any event of any importance, 'tis of no importance to contend that he ever decreed any event at all: thus do they that deny God's absolute decrees and disposing providence with respect to the will and free actions of rational creatures in effect deny all absolute decrees whatsoever.

We have much more evidence of God's absolutely foreordaining the free action of men than anything thing else whatsoever: for most of the things that are mentioned in Scripture as the objects of God's decrees are acts and events depending on the wills of men, and there is no one event that can be mentioned that ever was foretold of God but what was either dependent on men's free will, or did so concern the acts and state of mankind depending on their will, so as that it is most easy to see it would be either impossible to predetermine it or unwise till the acts of men's wills were determined.

FOREKNOWLEDGE proves predetermination. 'Tis plainly impossible that an event should be foreknown that it shall certainly be, while it yet remains a thing merely contingent whether it should be or no. It is a contradiction; 'tis as much as to say that a thing is certainly to be, that is as yet absolutely contingent and uncertain whether it is to be or no. If the event be foreknown certainly that 'tis to be, then it is certainly to be; and if it be certainly to be, then something has already determined its being, either a divine decree or something else. Something has rendered a thing fixed and settled, and not vague and merely contingent, whether it is to be or no. If the future being of anything be already a thing fixed and settled, and not remaining vague and uncertain, then something has made it so. If the futurition of the event be fixed and limited, 'tis fixed and limited by something. If an event certainly hereafter be, then 'tis something more than merely possible. But[^003-note46] things that ben't necessarily existent in themselves are no more than merely possibly. And therefore if there be something more[^003-note47] besides possibility, there be certain futurition; there must be some cause of it, i.e. there must be something that predetermines its being, or brings it out of a state of possibility into a state of futurition. The futurition of the event is fixed and limited, so that the mark will certainly not be missed; otherwise it must remain in itself an uncertain thing whether it will be or no. If there be no certainty or fixation of the event, then it can't be discerned or known; 'tis impossible that more should be discerned than is. If the futurition of the event be contingent, then it is discerned to be contingent, and that is quite inconsistent with its being discerned to be certain.

The greatest difficulty is surely in God's predetermining those free acts of men that are evil or sinful. Of these, the crucifying [of] Christ was one of the blackest and worst, yea, was designed of God as a representative of all the wickedness of mankind. But yet there is exceeding clear evidence many ways that this act was foreordained. Show how... We have such clear evidence of the predetermination of no particular act whatsoever as we have of this.

To argue the disposal and ordering of God's decree and providence with respect to the wills of men from the use of prayer, not only with regard to ourselves and our own wills and acts, but also with regard to others: with regard to the actions of men, of rulers, of persecutors; the state of kingdoms and commonwealths and churches and all human societies; and with respect to particular persons, our friends or enemies. Almost all use of prayer with respect to all these things is at an end if God don't determine and order the acts of men's wills.

And how little business has thankfulness [and] thanksgiving for public mercies and good received through the virtues of men, which are the greater part of the outward good things of life, and in a sense the spiritual good things too. How can that be true, that "every good gift and every perfect gift is from above, and cometh down from the Father of lights" [Jas. 1:17].

The very name that Christian virtue is called by, viz. grace, shows it to be entirely a mere gift of God.

Concerning that promise, "Seek, and ye shall find" [Matt. 7:7], see note on Ps. 24. 6.[^003-note48]

The Apostle flatly contradicts that saying of the schools, *facienti quod in se est deus not deneget gratione—*or which is to the same purpose, "God will surely give special grace to him that well improves common grace"—in that, II Tim. 1:9, "He hath called us with an holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace."

The[^003-note49] Arminian notion of free will, or a self-determining power in the will, as they explain, is built on a supposition that overthrows the ground of all our evidence that the world did not begin to exist without a cause, and of the being of a God, and of the being of any other being but ourselves: for the evidence of all these things relies entirely on this principle, that nothing can be without some determining ground or reason why it is.

Faith[^003-note50] is not of ourselves; it is the gift of God. "It is God that worketh in you both to will and to do of his own good pleasure" [Phil. 2:13]. Eph. 2:1, "You hath he quickened, that were dead in trespasses and sins." I Pet. 1:3, "He hath begotten you again to a lively hope." John 3:5, "Born of the Spirit." Ps. 110:3, "Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power." Col. 2:13, "And you being dead in your sins and the uncircumcision of your flesh hath he quickened together with him." II Cor. 5:17, "Therefore if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature"; and elsewhere, "Created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath before ordained that we should walk in them" [Eph. 2:10]. Col. 2:12, "Through the faith of the operation of God." II Thess. 1:11, "Would fulfill in you all the pleasures of his goodness, and the work of faith with power." God is the author or beginner and furnisher of our faith. Heb. 12:2, "Looking unto Jesus the author and furnisher of our faith." Eph. 1:17-20, "That the God of our Lord Jesus Christ, the Father of glory, may give unto you the spirit of wisdom and revelation in the knowledge of him: the eyes of your understanding being enlightened; that you may know...what is the exceeding greatness of his power to us-ward who believe, according to the working of his mighty power, which he wrought in Christ."

The Spirit of God gives the hearing ear (Deut 29:4), the seeing eye (Prov. 20:12), the new heart (Ezek. 36:26). Heb. 13:21, "Make you perfect in every good work to do his will, working in you that which is well-pleasing in his sight." Ezek. 36:27, "I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them." Phil. 1:29, "For unto you it is given to believe." Christian virtue is called grace in Scripture, which signifies that it is the mere gift of God. See concerning the spiritual resurrection, Eph. 2:1-10, especially vv. 8-10: "For by grace ye are saved through faith; and that not of yourselves: it is the gift of God: not of works, lest any man should boast. For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works, which God hath foreordained that we should walk in them." Gal. 2:20, "I live; yet not I, but Christ liveth in me."

That which makes the call of the word effectual is God's almighty power, and that alone: for he calls the deaf and at the same time opens their ears, that they may hear (Job 36. 10). He calls to the dead, as to Lazarus of old, "*Lazarus, come forth*"; and by his infinite power he quickens and raises them to life (Eph. 2:1). He "calleth those things which be not as though they were" (Rom. 4:17), and so creates a principle of life in them (Eph. 2:10-22, ch. 4:24-32). The scriptures are taken out of *The Catechism Rescued*, on effectual calling.[^003-note51]

That there can nothing come to pass that is not necessarily existent without a cause, that is a NATURAL NOTION OF THINGS. And that men whose wills and inclinations are evil, that is a natural notion of things, etc. So that 'tis their scheme is the scheme that is contrary to our natural notions of things.

Take arguments out of the Scripture prayers, especially those that we have in the Psalms, against the Arminian notions of concurring assistance universally granted, but the effect's being determined by our free will.

To[^003-note52] make the works of the unregenerate whilst under the guilt and dominion of sin the condition of, or that to which, special grace is promised, evidently supposes special grace is dispensed by God as a moral governor according to those works; and this again supposes some degree of moral goodness or true excellency in the account of God in those works.

The Arminians do insist upon it, that God dispenses his special grace as a moral governor and righteous judge of the behavior and improvements of his creatures, and not as a sovereign. Mr. Mills.[^003-note53]

That saying, "To him that hath shall be given, and he shall have more abundance" [Matt. 13:12], parallel with that, John 15:2, "Every branch in me that bringeth forth fruit, he purgeth, that it may bring forth more fruit."

'Tis unreasonable to suppose that God has anywhere said thus, or that which is tantamount, "They that spend away the best part of their lives in sin against my most gracious calls and counsels and positive and repeated commands, most solemn warnings and terrible threatenings, in a presumption on my mercy afterwards, and then come and seek that mercy that they have so presumed upon and horribly abused to embolden themselves in doing the part of rebels and enemies: I will grant 'em that mercy or forgive their sins, and bestow eternal salvation upon 'em and the privileges of my dear children."

One[^004-note20] of the texts against atheism. Job 12:7-9, "But ask now the Beasts," etc. "Who knoweth not in all these that the hand of the Lord hath wrought this?"

*Doc*. When we consider the various creatures that we behold in the world, it is exceeding plain that it is God that hath made them. He that cometh to God must believe that he is.

See more texts in Mastrict, "de Existentia et Cognitiene dei," and see also Turretin.[^004-note21]

RATIONALNESS AND CONGRUITY OF CHRISTIANITY. 'Tis rational to suppose that there should be some way for the Creator and First Being to manifest the wonderfulness and boundlessness of his grace, as well as his power and wisdom. But no[^004-note22] way can possibly be thought of whereby this should be done so much, in so high a degree, as that which the gospel reveals.

____________________________________

BOOKS OF THE OLD TESTAMENT NOT FORGED. The Jews never would, in books that they forged, record themselves such a perverse, wicked people as they are recorded in those books. Vid. Is. 30:8-9, 19, with my note upon it;[^004-note23] and Deut. 32:20, Is. 1:4, Is. 65:2-4, etc.

____________________________________

APOSTLES SPAKE BY INSPIRATION. This is given as an infallible mark by the apostle John, whereby the primitive church might distinguish between those that maintained truth and those that maintained error were to be tried, and whereby even the spirits themselves that persons in these times were inspired with were to be tried: viz. their hearing the apostles, or their hearkening to and complying with these things that they taught. Which was making of their doctrine a rule immensely too much, if they in their doctrine were not under the infallible guidance of the Spirit of God, and if the Christian church in general had not clear evidence that they were so.

See texts in *Common-Place Book to the Scriptures*, in the chapter of the Scriptures.[^004-note24]

SUCCESS OF THE GOSPEL. "Tertullian tells the Roman Senate, that though the Christians were but of yesterday, yet they had filled all places and offices, *hesterni sumus et omnia implevimus*, that they were strong enough to master the Roman Empire; nay, that so great were their numbers, that if they should but agree to retire out of it, the world would wonder at its own solitude. And in his book against the Jews, he says, 'The Christian religion had enlarged its conquests beyond those of the Roman Empire, and had subdued those places which were inaccesible to their armies.' Pliny take notice in his letter to the Emperor concerning the Christians, that there were such multitudes of them that the 'temples and sacrifices of the gods were almost forsaken.' Tacitus speaks of *ingens multitudo*, an immense number, even in Nero's time, the very first age of Christianity. In short, the prevalency of the Chritian religion was such, that it gave occasion to the heathens to call it η χρατουσα Δοξα, the powerful and prevailing sect." Bennet's *Inspiration of the Scriptures*, p. 276.[^004-note28]

The epistles of the apostles were written for general use, with a design that the church of God in general should be benefited by them, and so were received in the apostles' days. The apostle Peter speaks of the epistles of the apostle Paul as things that were generally known and in use in the church of God, in his Second Epistle, third chapter, [verses] 15-16, though none of the epistles of Paul are supposed to have been written more than about seven years before that epistle of Peter.

## "Subjects of Inquiry" Subjects of Inquiry

Jonathan Edwards"Subjects of Inquiry"

A late manuscript booklet of memoranda outlining scholarly projects and study aims, "Subjects of Inquiry" provides clues to many intellectual interests Edwards was pursuing in the latter part of his life, from sketches for treatises to arguments about human nature and identity to thematic scripture reviews. Here also we see originally described the ideas for various notebooks such as "Defense of the Pentateuch as a Work of Moses" (an extension of "Notes on Scripture," no. 416), "Harmony of the Genius, Spirit, Doctrines, and Rules of the Old Testament and the New" (part of the larger project, "The Harmony of the Old and New Testaments," as described to the trustees of the College of New Jersey), and "Hebrew Idioms." Internal evidence suggests the book was constructed no earlier than September 1746, and entries date from the late 1740s through the end of his life.

*****

This notebook (BRBL, f. 12751) is twenty-two leaves, made mostly from discarded fan paper cut in irregular shapes, giving the book a butterfly effect when it lies open. For ease of presentation and reading, the text is here presented as entirely flush left. Leaf 13 is a discarded prayer bid. Leaves 2-12 were once numbered pp. 77-98 by Edwards as part of an index for "Types of the Messiah," but he abandoned this project (or completed it elsewhere), turned the pages over and re-numbered them for this booklet. Remains of the abandoned index are found on MS p. 24, with entries under the letters U-Z. The cover is made of brown ream wrapper and measures 3 3/4" x 7 3/4," inscribed front and back, "Subjects of Enquiry."
