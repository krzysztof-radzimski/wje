## Documents Relating to the "Bad Book" Case Bad Book Case

Jonathan EdwardsTHE BAD BOOK CONTROVERSY

In the early months of 1744, Edwards learned that a number of young people of the parish had been circulating an illustrated book on midwivery amongst themselves, which, as Samuel Hopkins relates, "they improved to promote lascivious and obscene Discourse" amongst themselves. Specifically, young men who circulated the book "made sport" of young women in the vicinity, claiming to know about their anatomy, menstruation, and childbirth. In March, Edwards preached a sermon on Heb. 12:15-16 as a way of introducing the scandal to the church. At a meeting after the service, he laid the situation before the members and obtained permission to investigate the matter. A committee, consisting of some of the most prestigious men in town, was appointed to conduct the inquiry. Then Edwards reportedly read a list of suspects and witnesses that were to appear before the committee to testify. Hopkins states that the zeal with which the congregation approved the investigation turned to resentment against Edwards because of his failure to distinguish between the accused and the innocent; the sons and daughters of prominent families were thereby associated with those of low repute. By the time that the committee was to meet, many objected to the manner in which the case was being managed. "The Town," Hopkins writes, "was suddenly all on a Blaze."

The first document presented here is the infamous list that Edwards purportedly read from the pulpit. It is reproduced with the shorthand marks that he made beside each name. Edward almost surely used these marks to distinguish in his own mind the degree to which the individuals were either involved as suspects or important as witnesses. The names at the top half of the list, as appears by the subsequent testimonies, are those of suspects, while those at the bottom half are witnesses. The suspects are all males, and the witnesses, with the exception of Dr. Hutchinson, females. Between the two groups of names is that of Dr. Samuel Mather, who, along with Hutchinson, was probably called in as a local expert on anatomy or because the book may have been taken from his library.

Edwards was active in collecting evidence. A letter draft to "Eleazar Hannam's wife," Dorothy Danks Hannam, of adjoining Southampton, dated March 26, 1744, is found, with its left margin trimmed, as a page in a sermon on Micah 5:7. In the letter, Edwards requests--practically orders--her to provide testimony to the committee on who had read the "unclean" book.

The next document is the collected statements against one of the main culprits, Oliver Warner. The handwriting and telegraphic composition of the document indicates that Edwards took down the testimonies as they were being given before the committee. Here the origin of the mischief is revealed. Rebeccah Strong, the first witness, states that she saw two young men, Charles Wright and Timothy Root, looking at the book as long before as five years, in 1739. The antics of the young men, all in their early twenties, are enumerated; at a time when many of them are coming to the age of marriage and sexual intimacy (indeed, one of the suspects, Noah Baker, was married in 1743), they use their knowledge to disparage and harass young women. In this series of testimonies, too, the title of the book is revealed. Two young women, Elizabeth Pomeroy and Katherine Wright, identify it as "The Midwife Rightly Instructed," which Thomas H. Johnson has identified as an edition of *The Problems of Aristotle*, first printed under that title in 1679.

But the nature of the case, and the offenses of the suspects, changed as the proceedings started. Testimonies revealed that Timothy and Simeon Root, the other two principal culprits, had behaved disrespectfully in church when the affair was initially "under consideration" in March. Furthermore, on the day that the committee first sat at the parsonage, "those two Roots," Warner, and other suspects who were expected to wait to be called, voiced their protest at having to wait, played games in the yard, and even went to the tavern for a mug of "flip." Timothy Root especially distinguished himself for his earthy, irreverent remarks, telling his comrades that they were "devilish cowards" for waiting on the committee, and declaring that he would not "worship a wig" and that he did not "care a turd" for any of the members of the committee. In the face of these revelations, the matter turned from one of lasciviousness to one of insolence, and Edwards was determined to maintain the traditional hierarchy of deference.

In June 1744, Timothy and Simeon Root made their confessions before the congregation; Oliver Warner's was probably not far separated in time from theirs. The confessions, written by Edwards, are remarkably brief and general, considering the great deal of evidence compiled against the subjects. In the Roots' there is no mention of, and scarcely an allusion to, the controversy over the midwivery book. Instead, their confessions focus on their sin of disrespect for authority. Warner's confession does account for his "very unclean and lascivious expressions," but even then, he is allowed to state that he did not remember using them.

The superficial nature of the confessions may be a reflection of the growing discontent over Edwards' efforts to assert his authority, which had been brewing at least since the covenant renewal of 1742. The final document in this series cannot categorically be assigned to the "bad book" controversy, but its content strongly suggests that Edwards wrote it in response to criticisms from the church over his proceedings in this discipline case. In addition, the dating of the piece is unknown. It could be located earlier in the controversy, before the confessions of the culprits, and even before the committee meeting. In any case, the first part of the document consists of notes, apparently for a public address, justifying his course of action. In them, Edwards denies the need for a formal complaint for the church to proceed against an individual, especially since the offense is not private. Also, he justifies his barring the suspects from the Lord's Supper because of the scriptural injunction to "put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean." The second part of the document is a short series of questions that Edwards apparently posed to himself as liable to public debate. Which part of the document came first is uncertain; perhaps the short series of questions prompted the notes defending his actions, or perhaps the series of questions was something Edwards contemplated separately.

* * * * *

The manuscripts related to the bad book controversy consist of mixed sizes of paper because they are mostly notes and drafts. The list of suspects and witnesses is a single rectangular scrap, and the letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam is now part of the sermon on Micah 5:7. Edwards trimmed the left side of the letter to conform to the size of the sermon notebook, causing some lacunae. The "Testimonies Against Oliver Warner" and "Papers Concerning Young Men's Reading Midwives Books," consisting of the notes and subsequent drafts, are on eight quarto leaves. Edwards made his initial notes at the actual hearings; he then wrote a fuller draft of the testimonies, and finally a fair copy of the draft. To avoid unnecessary replication of texts, the notes, which contain information not in subsequent drafts, and the final fair copy (with the exception of the first two statements, which are from the previous draft), are included here. The confessions are on irregular sizes of paper. The first, that of Oliver Warner, is on a rectangular piece of coarse, gray paper, like the type Edwards used on occasion to make notebook covers. The second, on a smaller piece of regular paper, is actually two confessions in one. One sentence is bracketed, which Timothy Root read but Simeon Root did not, to allow for the fact that the latter was not a full member of the church. Finally, Edwards' defense of his actions are on three pieces of fan paper, each folded in half, and the list of questions is on a single piece of fan paper.

Transcriptions of the list of suspects and witnesses, the letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam, the notes on Oliver Warner, the fair copy of the testimonies, and Oliver Warner's confession were previously published in Thomas H. Johnson, "Jonathan Edwards and the Young Folks' Bible," *New England Quarterly* 5 (1932), 41-51.

Letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam Northampton March 26, [1744] To Eleazar Hannam's wife:

It has been testified by one before [the] church, that she was once at the [widow] Macklin's house, where were Oliver Warner and Medad Lyman reading in [a book] about womenkind, that which was [very] unclean to read; and that they made sport of what they read before some womenkind; and that Thankful Parsons [said] you were there. This therefore is [to] desire you, as you are a member of the Christian church and would maintain communion of churches, whereby they [are] obliged to assist one another as occasion [arises], to send an exact and full testimony [of all] that you know relating to this matter particularly as near as you can, how [unclean] it was; which of these two young [men] read in the book, whether one or [both] of them; what the book was about, whether it was about womenkind; [whether] it was about women's having children; whether they seemed to make sport [and] diversion of what was read; whether [they] took occasion from it to run upon the [girls]; and the like. In thus giving your testimony, I think you will do what [you] are obliged to do as a Christian. Jonathan Edwards.

Timothy Root and Simeon [Root] and Moses Sheldon, often talking in a private way about a book, said something about a bible in a laughing way, that she concluded was that. When he (Moses Sheldon) inquired about the book, he asked me if it was not such an one, and I told him it was. Sarah Baker found it, and gave it to her mother. Moses Sheldon and those two Roots and Charles Wright. Timothy Root and Charles Wright chiefly used to talk much about such things as I suppose such books relate to.

Rachel Clap. Oliver Warner, Deacon Parsons' son, Medad Lyman wanted Aristotle, intended to find it. Simeon Root noised that he was sorry so that he had not read more. He told what was pictured out in the book. There the child is, lies all pictured out.

John Miller's wife. Delivered by younger sister. Isaac Parsons promises reformation. Never thought of the harm. He would totally and finally have done with it.

Mindwell. Moses Sheldon: "I know a great deal more than you can think about the works of nature." Not above two months ago. Turned away and laughed.

Martha Clark. Ebenezer Bartlet: "What if I should say I sat 'till midnight reading one of them books." When he was talking about such books. "Don't you think I would sit up again if I had opportunity?"

Bathsheba. At David Burt's, Noah Baker, Timothy Root and Elkanah Burt, reading in a book that they called "the bible" in a laughing way. All read in it. Timothy Root read most. Called it "young folks' bible." The book was exceeding unclean to the top of baseness. The book what they read was about women. Samuel Burt's book, they said. It [was] about women's having children.

Had seen the book at Samuel Burt's once before. Timothy Root said at that time at Burt's, after the child is born they burn guts and garbages.

Hannah Clark with her in the street, and Oliver Warner met them. Said, "When will the moon change girls? I believe you can tell. I believe you have circles 'round your eyes. I believe it runs."

Medad Lyman and Oliver Warner knew what the girls was, what nasty creatures they was.

At another time, Oliver Warner and Medad Lyman reading in a base book, nasty book, about womenkind. At Moll Macklin's talk. John Macklin was there; he was worse than others that was there. Thankful Parsons was there. Dorothy Danks was there.

Joanna Clark. Oliver Warner, about a year ago: "When does the moon change girls? Come, I'll look at your [face] and see whether there be a blue circle 'round your eyes." Bathsheba Negro was with her. "I believe it runs."

Ebenezer Pomeroy, last lecture day night, when she groaned, said, "I believe you need the old granny. I have read in a book about that." John Lancton was with him.

Shadrach Bedartha. Oliver Warner asked me if I did not want a book that there had been talk about. I asked him who there be had such a book. He answered, "That is no matter to you." Elizabeth Pomeroy. Found a book in their house up the chimney, on the backside of the chimney on the press. No reason to think it was mother brought it in. The title, "The Midwife Rightly Instructed." Katherine Wright was with her. A new book.

Katherine Wright to the same. See Betty took it out. "Midwife Rightly Instructed," etc.

Experience Strong. A year or year and one half [ago], Ebenezer Bartlet at Jonathan Strong's house: "It might be necessary for young men to know such things." He owned that if he had had such a sense as he once had, he should have had no inclination to read such books.

Isaac Searl. Ephraim Wright talked to him and Oliver Warner too, that told him they would not have him tell more than he need, that they would not have him enlarge much upon it. Ephraim Wright told him he need not say a word, if he had not a mind to.

Mary Downing. Two years ago this summer at her mother's. Oliver Warner there. There was reading amongst them in a book. They made sport of what they read in the book. One or two more there; she could not certainly say who they was. They all did so. What they laughed and made sport of was about girls, things concerning girls that it is unclean to speak of. They seemed to boast as if they knew about girls, knew what belonged to girls as well as girls themselves. She took it that the book was about the same things that they talked of. They seemed by their talk to apprehend that they got what they knew about girls out of that book. They run upon the girls at that time, boasting how much they knew about them. Many other times here, talking after the same manner about what he knew about girls. He has talked to me himself about what he knew.

John Lancton, a fortnight ago last Friday, was at the farm where I was and was talking of such things, and he boasted that he had read Aristotle. He talked about his reading that book more than once; talked about the things that was in that book in a most unclean manner a long time. Betty Danks and Moll Waters there. He spoke of the book as a granny book. When I checked him, he laughed. He talked exceeding uncleanly and lasciviously, so that I never heard any fellow go so far. After he was gone, we--the young women that were there--agreed that we never heard any such talk come out of any man's mouth whatsoever. It seemed to me to be almost as bad as tongue could express.

Complaint.

To deny powers to a church to meddle without a complaint is to deny a power of self-preservation common to all societies.

Shall the master of a ship not inquire when he fears the ship is running on rocks.

All societies have this power.

The Scripture directs to making a complaint only in the two instances of personal injury and private offenses.

To deny any power without a complaint is to deny any power of authoritative inquiry. To deny this is to deny the power of self-preservation common to all societies.

Watchmen are set chiefly for preservation of the body, as watchmen in the time of war are for the preservation of a town or land. Ezek. 33.

Mr. Durham is express that no particular formal accusation necessary.

*Obj*. I. The rule in the eighteenth [chapter] of Matthew.

1. The rule don't require it, because it was not a private offense. 'Tis the way that offenses used to become public, viz. by the fame of them, not by being a great many witnesses.

2. It could not be done privately for, First, search must have been made to know who they were. Secondly, witnesses and they must be heard face to face. Thirdly, they must be dealt with, all of them. Fourthly, if they would not hear one or two more, taken with each one. Whether or no such a usage or practice amongst young people in a town is not scandalous.

[Confession of Timothy and Simeon Root]

I do acknowledge that I have been guilty of scandalously contemptuous behavior towards the authority of this church in their late proceeding in the affair, to inquire into which the church lately chose a committee. Herein I am sensible, I have greatly sinned against God, and injured the authority of this church [and violated the obligations I am under as a member of this church, engaged by solemn covenant to be subject to the authority of this church.] For this my sinful and foolish behavior, I now appear publicly to humble myself and take shame to myself, and do ask forgiveness of God and of this church, desiring their prayers that God would forgive me through Christ; and do now declare that 'tis my resolution, by the help of God, for the time to come, to behave myself more humbly, meekly and decently, treating those that are over me in the Lord with due honor and respect, as becomes a Christian.

The above is the confession of Timothy Root, and also of Simeon Root, excepting that which is enclosed in brackets thus, [ ]; owned June 1744.

## Notes for a Council Meeting on the Case of Elisha Hawley and Martha Root, June 1749 Hawley-Root Case

Jonathan EdwardsNotes for a Council Meeting on Elisha Hawley, June 1749

In Northampton in 1748 and 1749, a case occurred involving Lieut. Elisha Hawley and Martha Root, who gave birth to twins out of wedlock, one of which died. In May 1748, Root received £155 from Hawley as a settlement. However, Edwards brought the issue before the church in an effort to force the two to marry, despite the fact that Root's parents did not want a marriage. Hawley refused to comply, and in August 1748 he was excommunicated. In response, with his older brother Joseph Hawley acting as his lawyer, Elisha called a council of ministers, which met in June 1749. The document was drafted by Edwards for this council.[^002-note1] It is also possible that Edwards resusitated his "Some Reasons, Briefly Hinted At," originally composed for the Eleazar King-Hannah Graves Case of 1731, for the Hawley-Root case.

Despite Edwards' arguments, the council did not demand a marriage and instead left Hawley to proceed according to his conscience. Hawley was admitted back into church fellowship after making a confession. This case, then, is significant as an indication of Edwards' declining influence in the church. Here, two of Edwards' biological cousins and spiritual disciples opposed him. Also, the case dovetailed with the beginning of Edwards' final controversy with his congregation over the qualifications for communion, which no doubt contributed to the determination of Joseph Hawley to defeat Edwards. Edwards had already made his principles known, and his congregation was nervously waiting for his book to issue from the press as the Hawley-Root case was decided.

## Questions for Young People Questions for Young People

Jonathan EdwardsQUESTIONS FOR YOUNG PEOPLE

Handwriting and ink suggest a date of the early to mid-1730s for this list of intricate, even daunting, questions, drawn from the Bible, for "young people," specifically, older boys and young men. Edwards numbered most of the entries, though left others unnumbered, possibly because he did not use them or considered them as part of or subsidiary to a preceding numbered entry. He crossed out entries as well, apparently indicating he had used them. In some cases he provided the answers, simply writing a scripture reference. Nearly all of the numbered entries have appended the name of a particular boy or young man who was assigned the question. Edwards probably met with them as a group, possibly at his house, for catechizing and Bible study. In all, 66 names appear. The 61 who can be identified were born between 1712 and 1728, with the bulk coming in the years from 1716-1721. It is possible that Edwards had begun drawing up this list early in his pastorate and did not write in the names until later, since at least some of them appear to be added later. An issue revolving around this list of questions is how old the boys or young men were when Edwards was tutoring them, before or after their admission into the church, which in Northampton usually took place around age 14. Alternatively, the group may have consisted of members and non-members alike, since at least two young men, Elisha Clark and Amos Negro, joined, respectively, when Stoddard was still keeping records and in the first year of Edwards's pastorate, and at least one boy, Simeon Root, never joined the Northampton church. Given that most of the boys mentioned joined the church during the revival of 1734-35, it seems likely that the names were inscribed, and some of the later questions drawn up, during or shortly following that event.

## "The Theological Questions of President Edwards, Senior, and Dr. Edwards, His Son" 1822 Questions

Jonathan Edwards, Jonathan Edwards, Jr.The Theological Questions of President Edwards, Senior, and Dr. Edwards, His Son

This 1822 pamphlet contains two series of questions for which manuscript no longer exists. We can only speculate, therefore, if Edwards himself or one or more of his students, such as Hopkins, had compiled the list, and why the list existed. Probably they were assigned to students who came to study with the elder and younger Edwards (when the "Questions" were reprinted in *Bibliotheca Sacra* in 1882 [vol. 39], the title indicated they were "for their pupils in theology"). Alternatively, they could have been used when examining ministerial candidates. Starting with the nature of God and ending with the nature of the church, the questions reflect the structure of a systematic theology, as laid down most basically in the Westminster Confession. While Edwards Senior had 90 questions, Edwards Junior's tally rose to 313, incorporating many questions from his father's list but adding others that pertained to late eighteenth-century controversies and issues dear to the New Divinity.

Edwards Sr.'s list may be related in some way to the MS "Questions on Theological Subjects," though none of the questions there correlate exactly to the questions presented here.

## Drafts of Professions of Faith Drafts of Professions

Jonathan EdwardsDrafts of Professions of Faith

During the contentious period of the qualifications controversy, Edwards composed, for the benefit of the Northampton Church Committee, sample professions that he would accept from applicants for full membership. A couple of these, rather brief, he included in the preface to the *Farewell Sermon* (and in his "Narrative of the Communion Controversy"). During this same time or shortly after--perhaps at the beginning of the Stockbridge period--he drew up lengthier drafts, one in its entirety, followed by four revised or added portions, which are presented here. After the first and longest addition Edwards inscribed the names of two Indians, apparently to indicate that this was part of the profession they submitted or agreed to. Whether they had any part in composing the text is a matter of speculation, though the changes in this version are minimal.

The profession and its iterations indicate the type Edwards sought from applicants, including a sincere hope of having received Christ, a desire to live a godly life, and submitting to the governance of the church. The presence of two Indian names shows that he required no less of the Mahicans and Mohawks to whom he ministered. Along with sacrament sermons, the profession also demonstrates that Edwards applied these standards among the Indians as well as the Stockbridge English in admitting them to membership.

## A Copy of the Resolves of a Council of Churches, Met at Northampton, May 11. 1742 Resolves of a Council 5-11-42

Hampshire County MinistersA Copy of the Resolves of a Council of Churches, Met at Northampton"

On May 11, 1742, an impressive array of clerical representatives from nearly every church in Hampshire County met at Northampton to discuss the ill effects of the recent revivals. Both critics and supporters of the revivals were there, though the debates were apparently not rancorous. As Stephen Williams noted in his diary for that day, "Attendd ye councill of the county -- discoursd of things freely - & came to an agreemt[?] - & Adjournd."[^027-note1] As clergy, they could readily agree on the threats to their status. Censuring ministers, lay exhorting, and itinerancy were the "disorders" specifically mentioned. As remedies, the ministers counseled covenant renewal and a day of fasting.

This attempt at clerical consensus would be short-lived; within months, the cleavages in the Hampshire clergy over the nature and manifestations of the revivals would grow. William Rand of Sunderland and Benjamin Doolittle of Northfield would go into print against enthusiasm, while Edwards would publish *Some Thoughts*.

*********

Presented here are first, a manuscript copy of the results of the council, presumably in the hand of Rev. Peter Reynolds of Enfield (MS in Miscellaneous, Deidrich Coll., Clement Library, Univ. of Michigan), and the printed pamphlet, which was published in Boston in 1742 by Samuel Kneeland and Timothy Green in a six-page pamphlet.

good Order, in our Churches, the following Churches being present

by their Elders and Messengers Sd Church in Springfield Northampton

After solemn Prayer to God for direction

the Question was ut whether or no it apears to us that there has been a

very glorious and extraordinary work of the Spirit of God and Revival of Religion of late in New England and particular in this County in Convincing

and Converting Sinners and holding up and Comforting Saints and Causing

a very great and general Reformation. Voted in the Affirmative ------

Further voted that it is the Duty of Ministers and People to be deeply

affected with the Divine Goodness herein, to a Sinfull and Backsliding people

and publickly and frequently to Give Glory & Praise to God therefor,

Further Voted that we apprehend that there are things Prevailing in Some

Places that tend to hinder the and obstruct the Work of God and Cast a

Blemish and reproach on it. Voted that the following things are contrary

to the Rules of Gods Word, and of hurtfull tendency (viz) Depending upon

and following after Impulses and Impressions made on the mind Either with

with texts of Scripture brought to the mind or without as tho’ they were

Immediate Revelations of Some truth or Duty that is not Reveald

in the word of God Considerd together with his Providence Independent of

of that Impression: and persons takeing it upon them to Censure and Condemn

Ministers or others as unconverted persons or as being in a Dead and carnal Frame

that are Professing Christians and have a right by their Profession and Good behaviour

to be receivd as visible Christians in Good Standing, by the Visible Church

and Lay=mens taking upon them to give Publick Exhortations purposly

addressing themselves in a Set Speach and in a Solemn manner to a multitude

Convened either in the meeting hous or elsewhere, or to admonish reprove and

Exhort others as having a message from God, or Speaking in the name of Christ

or assuming the air of a Teacher or Speaking in an athoritative manner to those

that are their Equals or Superiours privately Independent on them. and we

look upon it to be irregular for Women and Children, Young People Servants

and others unless in an humble Manner and by way of intreaty to Exhort their

Superiours privately or at all to exhort those that are very much their

Superiours unless in Some extraordinary Cases Should not exhort otherwise

than in a Christian Conversation. Lay men ought not ordinarily to take

upon them that part of the work of a Minister that Consists in the Guiding and

assisting others in the Judgment they Should make of the State of their

Souls and that it is disorderly for persons to be from home and absent from

## Copy of a Covenant, Entered into and Subscribed, by the People of God at Northampton . . . March 16, 1742 1742Covenant

Jonathan EdwardsThe Northampton Covenant of 1742

As the intensity of the awakening that occurred in Northampton during 1740 and 1741 began to subside, Edwards saw his people returning to their worldly, quarrelsome, and selfish ways. He worked to reverse the tide and keep religious concerns high. In his sermons, he resorted to brow-beating his congregation, the same tactic he had used after the decline of the awakening of 1734-35. Another tactic at his disposal was covenant renewal, which had been used in New England churches since the late seventeenth century as a means of renewing loyalties to the religious and social ideals of the Puritan experiment. Basically, members of the church would re-covenant, or re-dedicate themselves to following the ways of God as a body of believers. Edwards' willingness to use the method of covenant renewal is demonstrated in the following document.

The covenant of 1742, which bears all the marks of Edwards' composition, was consented to in a special ceremony on March 16, 1742. The week before, on March 9, he first preached a sermon justifying the covenant renewal. Seeking to bind the recent converts to the church as well as encourage the preservation of the revival spirit, Edwards told his people that by this act they were "subscribing to the head" and joining the commonwealth of God. [^028-note1] At the close of the sermon, the covenant was read. The following week, just after the renewal, Edwards preached a further sermon on the subject, Josh. 24:15-27. [^028-note2]

Though brief, the covenant resounds with Edwards' insistence on social evidences of Christianity. Roughly the first half of the piece deals with individual moral behavior--particularly economic behavior--that impacts either upon other individuals or upon the community as a whole. The people of Northampton are to deal fairly and openly with one another, render everyone their due in business, make restitution for any wrongs, and avoid unjust reflections upon the characters of others. Those in positions of power are not to use their influence either for personal gain or to further the interests of parties. The youth of the parish are to avoid irreligious pastimes as well as "freedoms and familiarities in company" that lead to sexual promiscuity. Ironically, at this time some young men of the parish were making pornographic use of a book on midwivery to mock the young women. In two years this scandal would come to light, and Edwards and his people would be embroiled in the "bad book" case. The covenant was not agreed to unanimously. A segment of the church was suspicious that Edwards' motive in the covenant was to increase his own power. Some considered the renewal a "superfluity," as Edwards admitted in the sermon delivered shortly after the exercise. While Edwards viewed the covenant as a sign of the people's consent to obey God, many of the congregation viewed it as a sign that they were to obey the minister. [^028-note3] The covenant renewal of 1742 therefore reflected the changing relationship of Edwards and his church. The 1740s would see increased instances of conflict between minister and people, as Edwards struggled against what he perceived as increasing secularity and disrespect in the congregation, and the people against what they perceived as their pastor's efforts to accrue power and to dictate over their lives.

## Advice to Mr. and Mrs. Kingsley, February 17, 1743 Kingsley Case (all)

Jonathan Edwards (with members of Ecclesiastical Council)Advice to Mr. and Mrs. Kingsley

The following set of documents, all in Edwards’ hand, is the advice of an ecclesiastical council that met on Feb. 16-17, 1743, to hear the case of Hains and Bathsheba Kingsley of Westfield, Massachusetts.Hains Kingsley was born c. 1710, fought in the French and Indian War, and died in 1752; Bathsheba Kingsley died in 1748. The council of Feb. 16-17, 1743, was held at the Kingsley’s house. In his Journal for these dates, Westfield pastor John Ballantine wrote: "16. spent ye day at sergeant Kinsleys ye former part of it in prayer ye after part in hearing [and] considering ye Case with mr Edwards & some others. 17. spent ye former part of ye day at ye same place with ye same abt same business. mr Edwards preacht a lecture fro[m] Heb. 5. 7. 8." (See sermon no. 631.) (Journal of the Rev. John Ballantine, 1737-1774, Westfield Athenaeum, Westfield, Massachusetts.) The Westfield church’s attempts to discipline Bathsheba Kingsley first appear in the official record back in 1741, when, on Oct. 18, she publicly confessed to "breaking 4th 5th & 8th Commandmts in stealing a Horse riding away on the sabbath witht her husbands Consent." Apparently the labors of the council were to no avail, for on March 1, 1743, the congregation voted to bar Bathsheba Kingsley from the Lord’s Supper "on the accot of yr dissatisfaction with her." "The Publick Records of the Church at Westfield," Westfield Athenaeum. Joan B. Ackerman, Reference Librarian of the Westfield Athenaeum, graciously located and provided pertinent entries from the Westfield Church Records. The case is an illustration of the excesses that arose during the Great Awakening. Bathsheba Kingsley claimed to be receiving revelations from God, and thereby justified travelling abroad for extended periods of time and censuring individuals, including her husband. While Bathsheba Kingsley did not claim the full authority of a minister, she nonetheless took upon herself "some parts of the work of the ministry" in her public denunciations and prophecies.On Kingsley as an instance of how women during the revivals of the eighteenth century "stretched the boundaries of traditional ’feminine’ religious behavior to the breaking point," see Catherine A. Brekus, Strangers & Pilgrims: Female Preaching in America, 1740-1845 (Chapel Hill, Univ. of North Carolina Press, 1998), 23-26; quote on p. 26.

The council’s advice, for the most part, is conservative. The ministers tell Bathsheba Kingsley that she should "keep chiefly at home" and observe her station and obligations, namely, being subservient to the commands of her husband and the needs of her family. They also enjoin her to be more circumspect in her behavior and readiness to condemn others as unconverted. But they likewise tell Hains Kingsley to be mindful of his wife’s spiritual needs. He is not to hinder her from attending religious meetings and engaging in Christian conversation with her friends. Also, he is to restrain his temper in consideration of what the council interprets as his wife’s unstable emotional state.

The charged atmosphere of the revival, then at its height, influences the ministers’ advice. Mindful of the complex workings of the Spirit at the time, they seek not to stifle Bathsheba Kingsley’s zeal but to channel it. She is warned of the danger of judging the spiritual state of others, and of supposing "dreams" and "sudden impulses" to be revelations from God. Edwards was doubtless chosen to be a member of this council because of his defense of the revivals and his expertise in the "distinguishing nature of the gracious influences of the Spirit of God." Bathsheba Kingsley’s behavior borders on antinomianism, as she throws off traditional strictures and voices her opinions in an often vindictive manner. One group she singles out for her resentment is the clergy: among the people she reproves are her own ministers.

We Judge that the fores’d Mrs Kingsley has for many years been greatly out of the way of her duty. And as to the Cause of it we Judge that, together with a weak vapoury Habit of Body, and want of some proper conduct and advice in the beginning of her difficulty, and Satan’s taking the Advantage, it has been owing chiefly to these following things:

1. Ignorance of the distinguishing nature of the gracious Influences of the spirit of God, which are upon the Heart giving a sense of divine things there, and exciting answerable affections, dispositions & holy Joys, and not by exciting imaginations in the Head. From whence it has come to pass that Mrs Kingsley has for some years been almost wholly under the Govern ment of a series of dreams, imaginations and sudden Impressi-[L. 1v.] ons & Impulses, supposing them to be immediate Revelations from Heaven, and so has been perpetually hurried and driven to and fro, without any Rest.

2. Want of a thorough Knowledge of her self, and being emptied of herself, & want of that Humility that disposes persons to think others better than themselves; whence she has been apt to think her Knowledge to be very eminent & superiour to that of others, and so has been apt to think that she was a proper sense Person to be improved to do some great thing in the Church of God, & that in the Exercise of s doing some parts of the work of the ministry: whence also she has great ly trusted in her self, and ready to look upon it that the Interest of Zion depended on her, and that if she was not improv’d its Cause would sink, at least in this Town; so that she has neglected her proper Business, and gone quite out of her Place to promote Religion: whence also her Behaviour has of late, in general been, exceeding contrary to that Christian Gentleness & meekness that becomes a follower of the Lamb of God, especially in her treatment of her Husband.

3. To a want of having her will subdued to God, and not complying with that great Christian duty of selfdenial, and resigning her self up wholly to God; whence her soul has been for many years in a continual Tumult, like the sea in a storm; being destitute of that Peace and Rest in God that many other Christians enjoy; and also in a great measure destitute of Joy & Thankfullness for the great things God has lately done for his People.

And with regard to Mrs. Kingsley’s particular Conduct, we Judge she has exceedingly departed from the way of her duty in the following things, viz. In very greatly throwing off the Care of her Husbands House and Family, being great part of her time gone from home, almost continually wandring about from House to House, and very frequently to other Towns under a notion of doing Christ’s work, and delivering his Messages; And undertaking sometimes to go and reprove & warn her own ministers, and denounce God’s Judgments against them, under a notion of delivering messages from God to them; and often disobeying her her Husband in going abroad contrary to his commands; and often stealing away privately, and taking her Husband’s Horse to go away to other Towns Contrary to his mind, & sometimes unwarrantably taking Neighbours Horses: And in making almost a Constant Business of talking against others wherever she goes freely, at all times, & before[L. 2r.] all Persons sorts of Persons, Censuring others, declaring her Judgment and suspicions of others, as to the state of their sous. It appears to us that her Conversation has for some years, chiefly consisted in setting forth others vileness & wickedness, and above all other Persons whatsoever, talking and inveighing against her Husband, and also in commonly talking in a very severe, harsh and reflecting manner to her Husband, before the rest of the Family & others, without any restraint, or any manner of Appearance of the duty that a wife owes to her Husband; And also sometimes reflecting on the dead, in a manner that was very p improper, & could tend to no Benefit. And it seems to us that Mrs Kingsley has of late almost wholly cast off that modesty, shamefacedness, sobriety meekness, diligence and submission, that becomes a Christian woman in her Place.

As to her Husband’s behaviour towards his wife, we Judge that under his great provocation he has considerably [-] governed his spirit; But notwithstanding, the Heat of spirit he has sometimes shown and his manner of expressing it towards his wife, we don’t think is to be Justified but Condemned.

Upon the whole we would give the following advice to Mrs Kingsley.

1. That she henceforwards keep chiefly at home, & let her main Business be within doors, within her husbands house, serving God there, and taking Care of the Family & houshold affairs, being diligent therein; And that she go no more abroad as following such impulses & impressions as she has followed hitherto, under a notion of delivering messages from God to one and another, or as being sent by God.

2. That she submit to her Husband in meekness, & Humility, and Honour him as her Head, and be obedient to him, & not go a- broad when he disallows it.

3. That she don’t commonly or frequently talk against her husband. 4. Not to talk to her & reprove him with rough & severe Language, and especially before others; but if she reproves that it be privately in an humble submissive loving manner.

5. That for the time to come, she don’t Judge & censure others that are visible Christians in good standing in the Christian Church, as to their spiritual state, commonly or openly, or so as shall tend to make[L. 2v.] her Censures publick.

6. That her Conversation on religious subjects be not for the future much made up of talk about, & against Opposers, and those that she looks on as the Enemies of Religion, but other things that tend more to her own & others Edification.

7. When she is in distress for the souls of others, to give vent to it chiefly by pouring out her soul before God for them; not that we would prohibit her from taking opportunities as providence shall give them, without prejudice to the Business of her station, prudently & humbly to counsel exhort & intreat others.

8. We advise that she be ready to humble her self before God and men for her foremention’d Errours, & to Particularly to the Church to which she belongs, if they require it, & so with regard to such particulars as they think are scandalous. In doing these things we think she will best fullfill the vow she made to God some years Past.

And to her Husband we give the following advice, viz. We judge that the foresaid Mrs. K[ingsley] has for many years been greatly out of the way of her duty; and as to the cause of it, we judge that, together with a weak, vapory habit of body and want of some proper conduct and advice in the beginning of her difficulty, and Satan’s taking the advantage, it has been owenObscure form of "owing." chiefly to these three things:

2. Want of a thorough knowledge of herself and being sufficiently emptied of herself, and want of that humility that disposes persons to think others better than themselves. Whence she has been apt to think her knowledge to be very eminent and superior to that of others, and so hasMS draft: "as." been apt to think that [she] was a proper person to be improved for some great thing in the church of God, and that in the exercise of some parts of the work of the ministry. Whence also she has greatly trusted in herself, and ready to look upon it that the interest of Zion depended upon her, and that if she was not improved its cause would sink--at least in this town--so that she has neglected her proper business and gone quite out of her place to promote religion. Whence also her behavior has in general been exceeding contrary to that Christian gentleness and meekness that becomes a follower of the Lamb of God, especially in the treatment of her husband.

Whence her soul has been for many years in a continual tumult, like the sea in a storm, being destitute of that peace and rest in God that other Christians enjoy, and also in a great measure destitute of all joy and thankfulness in the great things God has lately done for his people.

And with regard to Mrs. Kingsley’s particular conduct, we judge she has exceedingly departed from the way [of] her duty in the following things: in very greatly throwing offMS draft: "of." the care of her husband’s house and family, being great part of her time gone from home, almost perpetually wandering about from house to house, and very frequently to other towns, under a notion of doing Christ’s work and delivering his messages; and undertaking sometime to go reprove and warn her own ministers and denounce the judgments of God against ’em under a notion of delivering messages from God to ’em, often disobeying her husband’s commands in going abroad and often stealing away privately and taking her husband’s horse to go away to other towns contrary to his mind, and sometimes have probably taken the neighbors’ horses; and in making almost a constant business of talking against others wherever she goes, freely at all times and before all sorts of persons censuring others, declaring her judgment and suspicions of others as to their souls’ state. And it seems to us that Mrs. Kingsley has of late almost wholly cast off that modesty, shamefacedness, and sobriety and meekness, diligence and submission, that becomes a Christian woman in her place.

As to her husband’s behavior towards his wife, we judge that under his great provocations he has much governed himself and restrained his passions; but notwithstanding, the heat of spirit he has sometimes shown, and his manner of expressing it towards his wife, we don’t think it to be justified but condemned.

Upon the whole we would give the following advice to Mrs. K[ingsley]:

1. That she henceforward keep chiefly at home and let her main business be within doors within her husband’s house, serving God there and taking care of the family and household affairs, and being diligent therein; and she go no more abroad, as following such impulses and impressions as she has followed hitherto under a notion of delivering messages from God to one and another, or going because God sent her.

2. That she submit to her husband in meekness and modesty, and honor him as her head, and be obedient to him and not go abroad when he disallows it.

3. That she don’t commonly or frequently talk against her husband. Not that we would restrain her from opening any family difficulties to some wise and judicious persons for their counsel, if there should be a necessity of it; but not to make a business of talking against her husband, nor to do it to many persons and not to speak openly against him.

4. Not to talk to him and reprove him with rough and severe language, and especially before others. But if she reproves him, let it be privately and in an humble, submissive, loving manner, becoming one in so near a relation that, above all others, requires love and endearment.

5. That for the time to come, she don’t judge and censure others that are visible Christians in good standing in the Christian Church as to their spiritual state, commonly or openly, or so as shall tend to make her censures public.

6. That her conversation on religious subjects ben’t for the future much made up of talk about and against opposers and those that she looks on [as] the enemies of religion, but things that more tend to her own and others’ edification.

7. When she hasMS draft: "as." distress for the good of others, to give vent to it chiefly by pouring out her soul before God and crying to the compassionate Savior for his pity to them. Not that we would prohibit her from taking opportunities as providence gives them, without prejudice to the business of her station prudently and humbly to counsel, exhort and intreat others.

And to her husband we give the following advice:

1. That notwithstanding all the provocations his wife has or may offer him, that he govern his spirit and don’t suffer his passions to rise, and treat her with patience and gentleness, without hard words or blows, or any kind of violence used, without further advice of ministers that may judge the extremity of the case hereafter to requite it.

That he treat his wife, notwithstanding all her ill treatment of him, with love and tenderness, providing well for her outward comfort and the comfort of her mind by giving reasonable liberty to go to religious meetings in this town and to lectures to neighbor towns if she desires it, when his circumstances will well allow it; and also all liberty of Christian conversation with Christian friends that may come to converse with her at his own house, and that she ben’t wholly confined from all liberty of visiting Christian neighbors and brethren for mutual edification.

And we advise that all past injuries be forgotten and buried on both sides, and that henceforward both of them take up a resolve with the utmost watchfulness to maintain mutual peace, harmony and love for each one, striving to do the duty of their station and relation and all duties of mutual kindness and benevolence towards each other, crying earnestly to God for his help, that they may be enabled so to do.

And we advise Mr. Kingsley, if his wife should still persist in her former practice notwithstanding her promise and this advice, to endeavor to make their promise and this consequent advice known in the neighboring towns as he shall see occasion, that others may hereafter the better know how to conduct themselves towards her.
