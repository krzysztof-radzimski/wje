### Judas Iscariot Has Been in Hell for More Than Seventeen Hundred Years

### *Judas Iscariot Has Been in Hell for More Than Seventeen Hundred Years*

As evidenced by the title of this sermon, Jonathan Edwards was not ambiguous in his estimation of Judas' final end. While Edwards' other writings are consistent with this conclusion that "Judas Iscariot has been in Hell for more than seventeen hundred years," they reveal more of Edwards' view of Judas as a person than does this piece. In them, we see how he analyzed Judas' faith, what examples of that kind of faith he saw in modern life, how he interpreted Jesus' interaction with Judas, and how that interaction informed his view of how he and others should deal with the Judases of the eighteenth century. These features from Edwards' other works that discuss Judas give us a fuller picture of his view of the apostate disciple.

Because Edwards was primarily interested in internal disposition rather than external appearance, he analyzed Judas' faith. Interestingly, Edwards' contention is not that Judas was completely faithless. Rather, his faith was corrupt. Edwards says "apostates never have the same kind of faith with true saints."[^002-note1] In other words, Judas had a kind of faith in Christ, but not true faith in him. Edwards says Judas followed Jesus for the worldly wealth and glory it would bring him.[^002-note2] When it became apparent that his expectations would not be fulfilled, he began to feel indignant and bitter towards Jesus. Despite this false faith, however, Edwards tells us that Judas was "visibly a Godly man,

<!-- p. pagenum -->

visibly a true disciple of Christ."[^002-note3] The problem was that, beneath this, he had no moral sincerity.[^002-note4] Accordingly, Edwards exhorts in the present sermon, "Don't rest in being externally a disciple."[^002-note5] However, despite all of this emphasis on Judas' externality, there is one instance in which we see Judas motivated by internal concerns, his confession in Matthew 27.3-5. This confession is certainly internally motivated since Judas says he has sinned by betraying innocent blood. The passage even uses the word "repented" to describe the action (in the KJV, the bible Edwards used). However, Edwards says in a Miscellany on justification that this confession is not valid because it is joined with despair.[^002-note6] We can conclude from this that Edwards wants Judas to follow Jesus because of a peaceful, inward pull towards Being, not because of distress from having committed a great evil.

With this profile of Judas' faith in mind, Edwards addresses the subject of modern-day Judases in the world around him. In "Betrayers of Christ," he bifurcates this group into two categories: 1) Those who put on a show of words by claiming to be Christians, declaring Christian doctrine, or professing repentance and trust of Christ; and 2) Those who put on a show of actions or behavior by doing what is not commanded (liturgical ritual) or even what is commanded. Edwards objects to people who say and do

<!-- p. pagenum -->

these things while holding principles or committing works Christ hates. He says these people "sell their Heaven, sell their God and their Saviour."[^002-note7]

They never sold all for Christ, and when it comes to a trial, they show that they had rather sell Christ than sell other things: they had rather part with Christ than part with their ease; they had rather sell Christ than part with their money; they had rather let go Christ than part with the good will of their neighbor; they had rather sell Christ than be laughed at by their companions.[^002-note8]

Edwards wants a purity of devotion that does not sell God for the master of mammon, but, just the opposite, sells all it has to follow Christ. Thus, in the present sermon, Edwards warns against following the way of the Rich man in the Gospels[^002-note9] and he warns against following Judas: "Don't do as [Judas] did, sell your Lord and your [deleted and undecipherable] for a moment's enjoyment of a few pieces of silver."[^002-note10] Elsewhere, Edwards says the modern-day betrayers are happy to accept God to help them out of temporal difficulties and keep them out of Hell, but they refuse to take God as the portion Christ died to give them.[^002-note11] The irony, of course, is that Edwards says it is precisely this perspective that will likely earn them the Judasian reward of eternal difficulty in Hell.[^002-note12] In the Miscellany on free grace and justification by faith, he says that Judas is a prototype

<!-- p. pagenum -->

for the professing Christian who enjoys the gospel, has been visibly grafted into Christ, and admitted a disciple, yet "thrust out in wrath as a punishment of his vile treatment of Christ."[^002-note13]

Ever pastoral, Edwards takes up the question of what should be done about this tragedy of people going the way of Judas. Obviously, we can say that one way he deals with the problem is to exhort people to change in the present sermon, the works being quoted here, and other works like these. Within this more obvious solution is Edward's strategy of looking to Christ for the answer. How do Jesus and Judas relate? In a Miscellany on the visible Church, Edwards points out that Jesus received Judas as a disciple and treated him as such, even making him an officer, a minister of the Gospel.[^002-note14] As he says elsewhere, "He sent him forth to Preach and work miracles and in other respects treated him as one of his disciples. He was highly priviledged and had recieved abundance of kindness from Christ."[^002-note15] In other words, even though Jesus knew that Judas would betray him, he treated him well and accorded him the same status that he accorded the rest of the disciples. The tone here is somewhat sad. Edwards muses about what it would be like if any of us had "one of our own Children or some person that we had been intimate with" betray us in this fashion.[^002-note16] He quotes Psalm 41, "yea, mine own

<!-- p. pagenum -->

familiar friend in whom I trusted which did eat of my bread hath lifted up his heel against me."[^002-note17] He quotes Pslam 55, "it was not an enemy that reproached me. . .but it was thou a man mine equal my guide and mine acquaintance we took sweet counsel together and walked to the house of God in company."[^002-note18] This sadness of Edwards on behalf of Christ and the situation in general provides a nuance to the picture we get in "Betrayers," where we see more anger. In this softer approach, Edwards is attempting to highlight the fact that Judas treated Christ so badly when he had treated Judas so well. Since Jesus knew Judas' disposition and motives from the beginning, the fact that he treated him well is all the more significant for Edwards.

Edwards uses the way Christ acts towards Judas as a model for how he and others should respond to the modern-day betrayers. He advocates that ministers "treat professours according to what is visible."[^002-note19] Jesus knew that Judas' faith was corrupt, but he sent him out to preach with the rest of the disciples because he was treating him according to what was visible in Judas. Edwards says ministers in his day should do likewise with people who profess Christ. They are to be taken as faithful disciples if they speak and behave as faithful disciples. However, Jesus' interaction with Judas changed once Judas had met with the Chief priests to betray him. At the Passover supper, Jesus predicted that the one to whom he handed the bread would betray him, gave the bread to Judas, and told him to go and quickly do what he must do. Edwards says "[Christ] knew that Satan had entered into [Judas] and filled his heart, and that he was then cherishing in himself a malignant malicious spirit against him master, excited by the reproof X had

<!-- p. pagenum -->

lately given him, and that he had already formed a traitorous murderous design against him."[^002-note20] Since this occurs just before the Lord's supper, Edwards concludes that when individuals hold principles or commit acts that Christ hates, they should not be allowed to participate in communion.

In the present sermon, Edwards is only concerned with Judas' actions and how the sermon hearers are modern-day versions of him; there is no exploration of the interaction between Jesus and Judas and no hermeneutical exegesis of the biblical text addressing how modern-day Judases should be treated by others. However, the addition of these latter two dimensions from Edwards' other writings sets this sermon in context by showing a more round picture of how Edwards viewed Judas and what he thought we should do about those who go Judas' way. *Judas Iscariot Has Been in Hell for More Than Seventeen Hundred Years* is written on two duodecimo leaves, two columns per side. It has an outline form, intended to be delivered extemporaneously, a practice Edwards adopted in the early 1740s in favor of his prior method of memorizing manuscript sermons.
