Valedictory Oration Valedictory Oration Jonathan Edwards

Valedictory Oration (introduced and translated by George Levesque)

Although Jonathan Edwards is undoubtedly one of Yale's most distinguished graduates, the fledgling "Collegiate School" that welcomed him in the fall of 1716 was itself hardly distinguished. The college, founded in 1701, was scarcely older than the thirteen-year-old, and given its fragile condition it was not clear whether it would survive long enough to graduate him. The Class of 1716 numbered only three students, and despite constant efforts from the trustees, the college still had no building, no fixed location, and no full-time rector.1 The rector pro tempore was one of the trustees, Samuel Andrew, but he was a busy minister who continued his pastoral duties in Milford, while the students and tutors met in various homes in Saybrook, some forty miles east along the shoreline. This arrangement had lasted for almost ten years, since the death of the first rector, Abraham Pierson, but students and parents complained often about the inconsistent instruction and minimal oversight, and they talked more than once about withdrawing from the school. Complaints about the tutors had grown so loud, in fact, that the trustees had to "quiet the Schollars" by temporarily allowing them to "repair to such instructors as they might be satisfied in."2 Some students went to their homes, while a group of about ten gathered in Wethersfield, just south of Hartford, under the direction of independent tutors.

To make matters worse for the fragile college, the trustees themselves became increasingly divided. The main point of dispute was the permanent location of the college. Although the trustees had originally selected Saybrook as the site for the college, the first two rectors never lived there, so the school had followed a nomadic existence for its first fifteen years, meeting in various homes and towns along the shoreline. Given their lack of success in recruiting a rector for Saybrook, trustees with strong connections to either Hartford or New Haven, then co-capitals of the Connecticut Colony, lobbied to move the college, with each camp supporting its own community. Meanwhile, other trustees maintained that Saybrook, at the mouth of the Connecticut River and free from the political maneuverings of Hartford or New Haven, was still the ideal location.

As the new academic year began in the fall of 1716, the matter remained unresolved. Unable to reach consensus on a single location, the trustees compromised and allowed students to choose among three locations: Saybrook, Wethersfield, or New Haven. Before young Jonathan could set off for college, therefore, his father, the Rev. Timothy Edwards, first had to decide which branch of the Collegiate School his son would attend. For the Edwards family, it was an easy choice. Wethersfield was just on the other side of Hartford from their home in East Windsor, only ten miles down the Connecticut River, and Jonathan's father liked the idea of keeping his only son nearby. Moreover, the primary tutor for the Wethersfield students, Elisha Williams, and the town's minister, Stephen Mix, were both Edwards relatives on the distinguished Stoddard side of the family. Although Jonathan could not be sure where, how, or if he would complete his collegiate education, he nevertheless had good reason to hope that Wethersfield would provide a safe and solid start.

Throughout the 1716-1717 academic year, students remained in their respective locations as the trustees continued to debate the permanent location of the college. By the fall of 1717, it finally appeared that a resolution was on the horizon, and the advocates for New Haven had slowly emerged as the victors. The September Commencement exercises were held for the first time in the meetinghouse on the New Haven Green, and a few weeks later the New Haven trustees were able to purchase, with the assistance of local donations, a tract of land across from the Green as a site for the college. In their haste to secure New Haven as a permanent location, they immediately began construction on a building. The Hartford trustees continued to dissent and appealed their cause to the Connecticut General Assembly, but after many rounds of debate, the Assembly ruled on behalf of the majority New Haven trustees. Refusing to give up their fight, the dissenting trustees nevertheless persuaded the Wethersfield students, including Edwards, to remain separate in hope that they might persuade the Assembly to reconsider their ruling.3

Despite the obstinacy of the upriver students and trustees, the New Haven trustees nevertheless moved forward with their plans, and as they watched the new college building take shape, they grew ever more confident of their ultimate victory. The new building would not only secure New Haven as the permanent site for the college, but it would also finally provide a home for an extensive library that Jeremiah Dummer, a patron of the college and colonial agent in London, had collected and donated almost five years earlier. In a letter to Dummer, the trustees exulted in their progress:

The affair of or. School hath been in a Condition of Pregnancy. Painfull
with a witness have been the Throwes thereof in this General Assembly.
But we just now hear, that after the Violent Pangs threatening the Very
life of the Babe, Divine Providence as a kind Obstetrix hath mercifully
brought forth the Babe into the World, & behold a Man-child is born,
whereat We all Rejoyce.4

Encouraged by this news, Dummer and Cotton Mather, the disaffected former president of Harvard and early supporter of the Connecticut college, secured a generous benefaction from a wealthy Welsh merchant, Elihu Yale, to help the struggling Collegiate School. Yale's grandmother, father, and uncle were among the earliest settlers of New Haven, so the college's new location undoubtedly made the pitch from Dummer and Mather more compelling. And with a building going up, Mather suggested, Yale could finance the completion of the structure that would commemorate him "better than an Egyptian pyramid."5 Yale apparently liked the idea and soon shipped some books and a large box of valuable goods from East India, which the trustees sold in Boston for a handsome profit. In grateful recognition of the gift, the trustees voted at the Commencement of 1718 to call the new building "Yale Colledge," a name that soon became synonymous for the whole school.6

As the trustees and townspeople in New Haven joyously celebrated Commencement and the commemoration of the college's first building, Edwards and the other students in Wethersfield carried on separately. The Hartford trustees even held a rival commencement in Wethersfield, awarding degrees to five students. But the trustees soon realized that they could not continue their independence for much longer and compete with New Haven's growing resources and support. Moreover, the General Assembly had grown weary of the dissension, and they again intervened. They passed "An Act for Encouragement of Yale College" that granted Hartford £500 for a new courthouse if the town gave up its fight to relocate the college. The Hartford trustees realized they could fight no longer. By late November, Jonathan and the other Wethersfield students had packed up their belongings and at last dutifully reported to New Haven.7 Finally, all the students were united in one location.

One significant problem remained, however: the college still lacked a resident rector. The trustees were nevertheless confident that, with a permanent location and building, they could quickly recruit one. For the present, however, the students remained under the charge of tutors, an arrangement that soon caused familiar problems. Shortly after their arrival in New Haven, the students grew dissatisfied and drew up a "Collection of faults" they found with "the public Expositions & Disputations & managemts. of the Tutrs.."8 As Jonathan later explained to his sister, Mary Edwards, the chief complaint was against tutor Samuel Johnson.9 The stated reasons for their dissatisfaction concerned Johnson's teaching, though it is doubtful that their complaints were with his intellectual abilities. He was one of the college's brightest graduates (Class of 1714) and later became the founding president of King's College (Columbia). A more probable cause for their discontent was that Johnson's rationalistic theological leanings, which eventually led him to Anglicanism, differed from the evangelical "experimental religion" of the upriver students. It is also likely that political forces were still at work as the Hartford trustees continued to exert their influence over the Wethersfield students. Whatever the precise reasons, Jonathan and the other Wethersfield students again packed up their belongings after only one month's stay in New Haven and resumed their studies with Elisha Williams in January 1719. The "violent pangs" of birth were obviously not yet over. 10

With the college again divided, colonial officials tried once more to mediate. Instead of the General Assembly, however, this time Governor Saltonstall and his council met privately with the trustees, that "having a right understanding of the management of the said deserters, which had occasioned so much disorder, proper measures might be taken to redress what should be found amiss, and prevent the like for the future."11 Rector Andrew explained that the only stated cause of the desertion was "the insufficiency of one of the tutors," but he did not doubt that the complaints against Johnson's services "would be found, upon enquiry, most unreasonable."12 Other trustees confirmed this assessment, and the council members were satisfied. The real problem, the trustees and the council agreed, was that the college still lacked a resident rector. The trustees were happy to report, however, that they had recently found a promising candidate: Timothy Cutler, pastor of the Canterbury parish in Stratford. The governor and council were relieved by the news and hoped a permanent solution was finally in sight. If the new rector proved satisfactory, the Wethersfield contingent would have no more cause to complain, and the Assembly could therefore order them to return to New Haven.

Cutler arrived in New Haven a few weeks later, and when the news of his appointment reached Wethersfield in March, the students there were indeed pleased and willing to return to New Haven. Jonathan wrote to his sister Mary that "the council and trustees…have put in Mr. Cutler, Pastor of Canterbury, President who (as we hear) intends very speedily to be resident at Yale College, so that all the scholars belonging to our school expect to return there, as soon as our vacancy after the election is over."13 At last, the long labor to "erect a collegiate school" appeared complete. After almost two decades of uncertainty and nomadic existence, the college had an established location, a permanent building, and a resident rector. And after three years of uncertainty, Jonathan could finally begin his senior year, no longer a pawn in political debates.

Despite the frequent interruptions, Jonathan's studies did not appear to suffer; in fact, he seemed to thrive even under the challenging circumstances. Although the upriver trustees failed in their attempt to move the college, they found an excellent leader and tutor in Elisha Williams, later president of the college, and were able to employ two other tutors, providing the Wethersfield students with more tutors than either Saybrook or New Haven. Now in New Haven, Jonathan and all the seniors came under the direct oversight of the new rector, Timothy Cutler. Cutler already knew Timothy Edwards, his fellow Connecticut clergyman, and wrote him shortly after Jonathan arrived in New Haven, commending his son's "promising ability and advances in learning" and assuring him that Jonathan would be under the rector's watchful care.14 Timothy Edwards later wrote Cutler, hoping that his son would behave "discreetly and soberly" and make the most of "his prsent Opportunity at ye College… Considering the time that he hath Lost and how Near it is to its end."15 Jonathan, for his part, was apparently happy in his new environment. After a month in New Haven, he wrote his father, "I take very great content under my present tuition as all the rest of the scholars seem to do under theirs. Mr. Cutler is extraordinarily courteous to us, has a very good spirit of government, keeps the school in excellent order, seems to increase in learning, and is loved and feared by all that are under him."16 As evidence of Jonathan's success, the trustees appointed him as the college butler for his senior year, a paid position that involved serving bread and beer to the students at meals and assisting the college steward with the financial accounts.17

In addition to the benefits of a resident rector and a new building, the students in New Haven now enjoyed the long-awaited fruits of the college library. For almost five years, since Dummer's first donation of books in 1714, the library had remained in boxed storage at the home of Justice Daniel Buckingham in Saybrook, where the volumes served as valuable bargaining chips in the political debates over the location of the college. After the college house was built in New Haven, the Governor's Council ordered that the books be moved to the new location, but the county sheriff still had to remove the books forcibly from Buckingham's house. Even as the caravan proceeded to New Haven, an unruly mob of town residents overturned the carts, turned loose the oxen, and damaged the bridges from Saybrook to New Haven to prevent the delivery. It took three days to make the thirty-mile journey, and over 250 books were lost, but the remaining one thousand books eventually arrived safely.18

The books were well worth the wait and the fight. Among the volumes in the Dummer collection were not only the major theological writings of the day but also the latest scientific and philosophical works from abroad, including works of Isaac Newton and John Locke, which surely thrilled a preternaturally inquisitive student like Jonathan. Although Edwards scholars continue to debate when he first encountered some of these works, and how much they influenced his later thought, Edwards almost surely had some access to them through the college library by his senior year in New Haven. Inspired perhaps by some of Newton's works, Jonathan likely began to compose some of his precocious scientific observations during this time, including his famous essay "Of Insects," the first of Edwards's "Spider" papers.19 Jonathan's most legendary, though not necessarily most important, intellectual encounter, however, was with Locke. Edwards's first biographer, Samuel Hopkins, famously described Jonathan's reading Essay Concerning Human Understanding "with great delight and profit" in college and reported that Edwards took the book in his hands shortly before his death, commenting that he had "more Satisfaction and Pleasure in studying it, than the most greedy Miser in gathering up handsful of Silver and Gold from some new discover'd Treasure." 20

Jonathan's reading of modern thinkers supplemented the traditional curriculum of the college, still strongly steeped in the medieval educational formula of the trivium (grammar, logic, and rhetoric) and quadrivium (algebra, geometry, astronomy, and music), which was itself a Christian reformulation of ancient Greek and Roman education. Following the pattern of Harvard College and its English ancestors, the instructors at Yale inoculated the largely pagan curriculum with regular doses of prayer and theological study, chiefly through William Ames's Medulla Theologiae and Cases of Conscience, Johann Wollebius's Compendium Theologiae Christianae, and the Westminster Assembly's Shorter Catechism. Inasmuch as Yale's stated mission was to produce a "succession of Learned & Orthodox men," solid learning and sound theology were the daily diet, enforced through a strict daily schedule of study, scripture reading, and public worship.21

The intense regimen seems to have suited Jonathan's highly introspective and disciplined nature. As his graduation in the fall of 1720 approached, he was at the head of his class and was therefore selected to deliver an oration at Commencement.22 This honor surely elicited equal amounts of anxiety and excitement in the habitually nervous and ambitious young scholar, for though college commencements were festive occasions, they were also public examinations, designed ostensibly to allow the trustees and the general educated public to verify the learning of the new graduates. This "Public Act" required each of the degree candidates to prepare answers to questions, called Theses, in various branches of knowledge (Logicae, Grammaticae, Mathematicae, etc.), which they then "manfully defended" (pro virili defendendae) during the commencement exercises. The Theses and the names of the graduates were printed in advance on a broadsheet and distributed to the audience so that they could follow the proceedings and interject if so desired. Jonathan, not yet even seventeen years old, and on public display for the first time, would therefore be expected to prepare both his assigned Theses and a valedictory address. As a further challenge, he would of course need to compose his disputations and address in Latin, the language of instruction in the college.23

Details of the Commencement exercises at Yale during this time are sketchy, but as with many things, the college seemed to follow the pattern of Harvard. Cotton Mather, president of Harvard from 1692 to 1701, described Commencement Days in Cambridge "whither the Magistrates and Ministers, and other Gentlemen then came, to put Respect upon their Exercises." The exercises included "Orations both Salutary and Valedictory, made by some or other of the Commencers…. But the main Exercises were Disputations upon Questions, wherein the Respondents first made their Theses."24 Thomas Clap, president of Yale from 1740-1766, later described Yale commencements during his presidency, and little seems to have changed from Mather's day. Clap writes:

The publick Commencement is ordinarily on the second Wednesday in
September annually: at which there is a large Assembly, consisting of
the President and Fellows, a great Number of Ministers, and other
learned and superior Gentlemen. The President begins the Solemnity with
Prayer, one of the Candidates, for the first Degree, makes a salutary
Oration to the Governor and Council, the Officers of the College, and
the whole Assembly: the others give a Specimen of their Learning, by
disputing Syllogistically on the Questions, printed in their theses;
which are then distributed. The like is done in the Afternoon by the
Candidates for the Degree Master of Arts. Then the President, with the
Consent of the Fellows, gives them their Degrees…. Then one of the
Masters makes a Valedictory Oration: and the President concludes the
whole Solemnity with Prayer."25

According to Clap's description, and from what we know of contemporary practices at Harvard, the "Valedictory Oration" was ordinarily reserved for one of the M.A. candidates, so it is not clear if Edwards delivered the valedictory address for some special reason, or if in fact he delivered one of several valedictory addresses, what Clap called the "salutary Oration."26 In any case, it was a great honor to speak at Commencement, and Jonathan seems to recognize both the privilege and burden of delivering the address. It is, however, difficult to distinguish his true sentiments from the flowery formality of his language. On the one hand, he possesses "a mind endowed with fortunate productiveness, furnished with eloquence and greatly equipped with expert knowledge," yet on the other hand he is "gripped by an inability to speak and by the greatest sense of wonder." He is tempted to flee from this duty in fear that he will disgrace himself, dishonor the school, and fail to express adequately the gratitude and good-byes of his fellow graduates. He nevertheless entrusts himself to the audience's generosity and "with trembling tongue and palpitating heart" undertakes his task, "doubtful of success."

The rest of his address is largely a panegyric to the faculty and friends of the college. He begins with Timothy Cutler, the long-awaited "Rector of highest distinction," whom Edwards describes as "the perennial fountain of the body, the life and motion of this School, the source of its living spirits, for which we have constantly been waiting and to which we have everyday been disposed." With Cutler at last in place, he says, the college has been "brought so suddenly from its infancy to its perfection." He next praises Warham Mather, Justice of the Peace in New Haven, who helped to oversee the construction of college's first building, and the Rev. Joseph Noyes, an alumnus (Class of 1709), former tutor, and present minister of the Church of Christ in New Haven. Lastly, he thanks Daniel Brown (Class of 1714), "outstanding tutor of this Academy," whose labors "unceasingly brought benefits" to his students. He concludes finally with fond farewells to his fellow students, "for that pleasing fellowship by which we have been refreshed," whose faces will always remain in everyone's mind "as if carved from marble or sculpted from bronze." As his parting valediction, he proclaims, "Farewell to you celebrated guides and farewell to our fellow students, lovers of learning. May all those always present in their scholarship fare well." 27

Following his graduation, Edwards decided to remain in New Haven to read for his M.A. degree, an undertaking that ordinarily required three years. Unlike the course of study for the B.A., however, the trustees did not prescribe a set curriculum for the M.A. Instead, the candidates were simply expected to spend time in their own scholarly pursuits. After this period of study, the candidate would then present "a written synopsis either of logick or naturall phylosophy or metaphysicks as also a Common place on some Divinity theses and ye solution of two or three problems such as ye Rector shall propose to him and be approved by ye trustees att Commencement."28 Without a fixed curriculum, no residency was required, so only a few bachelors remained in New Haven for their studies.29 Those who did remain at college, like Edwards, were usually preparing for the ministry and often combined their study with a pastoral apprenticeship and regular pulpit supply.

During his first year of graduate study, Edwards continued his duties as the college butler and lived on campus with his cousin Elisha Mix, apparently at the urging of his father and uncle who wanted Jonathan to look after his younger cousin who was just beginning at Yale. Elisha was the son of the Rev. Stephen Mix from Wethersfield, so they knew each other well from Jonathan's earlier days there, but it seems that the new chambermates soon resented this arrangement. Elisha grumbled at Jonathan's constant oversight and nagging, and Jonathan objected to Elisha's indolence and recalcitrance. It was not a happy union, and Jonathan wrote his uncle after just a few weeks to complain. It seems that Elisha was not an earnest student and resisted his chores because he did not "Love to be Called from play to Draw Cyder." Jonathan could not comprehend such absence of discipline and disrespect of authority, and he quickly lost patience. Given Jonathan's personality, one could imagine that he was an exacting and exasperating guardian, but Elisha was also more than a little unruly, and Jonathan persuaded his uncle to release him from his obligations.30

Later in that academic year, in March 1721, we again see Jonathan's precocious and priggish side when a student rebellion erupted concerning food in the "college Commons." The disruption had apparently gained some negative publicity in the colony, and his father inquired about it. Jonathan replied and explained that the undergraduates "all on a sudden, Before Mr Cutler, or (I Believe) any Body Knew yt they were Discontented, entered into a Bond of 15s never to have any More Commons of the Steward." Their boycott brought swift action from the "exceedingly Vex'd and Displeased" Rector Cutler and several of the trustees who immediately demanded the students to return. The "affrighted" students complied, but they "so lost Mr Cutler's favour that they scarce ever will Regain it." Jonathan was quick to assure his father that he lent no credence to "this Cabal." "I must needs say for my Own part," he wrote, "that although the Commons at sometimes have not been sufficient as to quality, yet I think there has been very little Occasion for such an insurrection as this." Jonathan went on to report other disturbances, "Much Worse, and Greater, and I believe Greater than Ever were in the Colledge before." Among the "Monstrous impieties, and acts of Immorality Lately Committed," he writes, were "stealing of Hens, Geese, turkies, piggs, Meat Wood &c-, Unseasonable Nightwalking, Breaking People's windows, playing at Cards, Cursing, Swearing, and Damning, and Using all manner of Ill language." Again, Jonathan was quick to report that he had nothing to do with these actions-"Through the goodness of God I am perfectly free of all their janglings"-and was hopeful that the trustees would properly respond to these kinds of actions.31

As a resident graduate student and as the college butler, Edwards was distinct from the rest of the students and was in some position of authority, but he was the same age as many of the undergraduates, if not younger. Such moral indignation from a peer was undoubtedly hard to receive, and it is not surprising that Edwards struggled with many of his personal relationships in college. As George Marsden suggests, however, Jonathan's difficulties with fellow students and his harsh judgment of others may reflect more of his own spiritual journey, which was then at its most intense stage, than his lack of charity. According to his "Personal Narrative," Edwards was at this time, "in the midst of many uneasy thoughts" about his spiritual state. The son of a minister, he had since childhood wanted desperately to experience the "sweet delight in God and divine things," but he could not overcome "objections against the doctrine of God's sovereignty, " which remained a "horrible doctrine" to him.32 Meanwhile, the inconsistencies of his own obedience to God's commands and the immoral behavior of his fellow students at college, presumably unlike anything he had seen before, deeply troubled him. He was, as Marsden explains, "living like a young monk seeking sainthood in a school of rowdy boys."33

Sometime during the early spring of 1721, however, Edwards had a spiritual breakthrough. As he again explains in the "Personal Narrative," he began to have new "apprehensions and ideas of Christ, and the work of redemption, and the glorious way of salvation by him." As he read and meditated upon Scripture, he says, "The sense I had of divine things, would often of a sudden as it were, kindle up a sweet burning in my heart; an ardor of my soul, that I know not how to express." Shortly thereafter, while home for a brief vacation, Jonathan had a long conversation with his father in which he "gave an account" of his recent contemplations and was "pretty much affected by the discourse." Later, as he walked alone in his father's pasture, he was again enraptured by "a sweet sense of the glorious majesty and grace of God." For the first time, he recounted, he was able to see God's "majesty and meekness joined together: it was a sweet and gentle, and holy majesty; and also a majestic meekness; an awful sweetness; a high, and great, and holy gentleness." At last, the "horrible doctrine" of God's sovereignty had become "an exceedingly pleasant, bright and sweet doctrine."34

The following fall, in August 1722, still glowing in his new "sense of divine things," Edwards accepted an invitation to supply the pulpit for a small Presbyterian congregation in New York City. Not yet ordained, this was an occasion for Jonathan to test his preaching skills and confirm his call to the ministry. One member of the congregation, John Smith, had graduated a year ahead of him at Yale, and Edwards lived in the city with Smith and his "pious mother." He spent eight months of "sweet and pleasant days" in New York, apparently some of the happiest days of his life, but by spring, it was time to leave. Timothy Edwards was eager to have his son back in Connecticut and had arranged a potential pastoral call for Jonathan in the town of Bolton, only fifteen miles from East Windsor. After a "most bitter parting with Madam Smith and her son," Jonathan obediently returned home to pursue his candidacy for the Bolton pulpit and complete the requirements for his M.A. degree.35

Back in East Windsor, Edwards spent the summer preparing for the Bolton pastorate and completing his "written synopsis" for public delivery at the upcoming Yale Commencement of 1723, scheduled for September 20. Similar to the bachelor's Theses, the master's disputations, called Quaestiones, were more complicated and required a substantial discourse. The oral delivery of a Quaestio was largely a formality, however, and there was little doubt that Edwards would pass this test, yet he still confesses much anxiety in his diary entries over the summer. He mentions, for example, concerns about performing something for "public acceptance" surrounding a future "event" with potentially "adverse" circumstances.36 We cannot know for sure what was on his mind, but it seems likely that his upcoming Commencement address somehow contributed to his anxieties. He was apparently not eagerly looking forward to the pastoral call in Bolton that his father had arranged, so perhaps he saw his M.A. disputation as one last opportunity to impress either the college trustees who might be looking for a tutor or church representatives from a more prestigious congregation looking for a young pastor.37

Whatever Jonathan's concerns were that summer, he was conscious at least of one thing as he prepared his disputation: more eyes than usual would be watching this particular Commencement, for the whole colony was still talking about what had happened a year earlier at the Commencement of 1722. Just as the young college seemed at last to be thriving, it faced its greatest crisis yet. Rector Timothy Cutler, the long-awaited leader expected finally to establish the young college, turned out to be a heretic. At the close of the Commencement exercises, he announced his conversion to Anglicanism, made plain by the conspicuous conclusion of his final prayer straight from the Book of Common Prayer-"and let all the people say, Amen."38 With these words, six other men in the audience, including one of the Yale tutors, joined Cutler in his announcement, apparently by previous agreement.39

The seriousness of this defection may be lost to modern readers, but for Puritan New England, their actions were tantamount to high treason. It was, of course, corruption in the Church of England that the Dissenters had hoped to "purify" by their removal to New England, so the thought that the enemy was in their own backyard was a devastating blow. Among their strongest objections to Anglicanism, particularly by the eighteenth century, was the growing influence of Arminianism, which held a relatively positive view of human reason and will, including the ability to receive or reject God's grace. For strict Calvinists who believed that God was completely sovereign in salvation, Arminianism was the first step toward rationalistic religion and Unitarianism. Such theological pollution had long been evident up at Harvard, but now many feared that Yale was in even greater danger.

News of this shocking announcement spread throughout New England, and it soon became known as "The Great Apostacie." The defection was all the more surprising and disturbing because Cutler had been widely regarded as a model defender of Puritan convictions. In fact, he was originally recruited to Connecticut to counteract Anglicanism, which was gaining a foothold along the southwest shoreline of the state. This was the man whom Edwards described only two years earlier in his valedictory address as the college's "perennial fountain…the source of its living spirits, for which we have constantly been waiting and to which we have everyday been disposed." The fountain, it turned out, was poisoned. Equally alarming, the six other men who simultaneously announced their conversion were all Yale graduates, and three had taught there in significant capacities.40 Had the very nursery they established to promote orthodoxy become a cradle of heresy? Major James Fitch, one of the college's benefactors, lamented the defection in verse:

And is it not to our Sorrow a Great Aggravation,
That such in our College had their Education?
That out of that Mother of Learning did spring
That should her Sons and Churches mortally sting.41

As Edwards prepared his disputation, therefore, he was surely conscious that the audience would scrutinize his words, carefully listening for any hint of heterodoxy. In no small measure, the reputation of Yale, and with it the hopes and fears of the Connecticut colony, was at stake. Edwards, characteristically, did not back down from the challenge. Although he was qualified to "dispute" on a wide range of topics, including those related to his detailed scientific observations, Edwards boldly selected for his Quaestio a theological topic at the center of the recent controversies. He defended the classic Reformation doctrine of justification by faith in Christ alone, phrasing his Quaestio this way: "A sinner is not justified in the sight of God, except through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith." Following the standard methods of scholastic disputation, Edwards did not rely upon Scripture-proofs but argued exclusively from the "mutual consistency of God's attributes" and the laws of non-contradiction. Edwards's main point was to demonstrate that his opponents, in suggesting that sinners are justified through "sincere obedience," were setting up a new form of works righteousness and a false system of justification. The logical implication of their position, Edwards claimed, was that a "sinner is his own redeemer." Everyone in the audience doubtless recognized his address as a frontal assault on the Arminian components of the recent "Apostacie." They also quickly perceived that the young Yale graduate had a bright future as a defender of the faith.42

Following Commencement, Edwards returned home to make final preparations for his move to Bolton to begin his new pastorate. Yet leaving New Haven behind was hardly easy. In addition to the intellectual stimulation of the college, Edwards would also have to part with a young lady whom he fancied, Sarah Pierpont, his future wife. She was the daughter of the late James Pierpont, long-time minister of the First Church in New Haven, where Edwards attended services, and the brother of James Pierpont, Jr., one of the Yale tutors. Edwards had likely known her for several years and presumably saw her again on his trip to New Haven for Commencement. She was still young, just thirteen years old, but to Edwards she was the epitome of Christian virtue and beauty, as his famous ode to her, written at about this time, demonstrates. "They say there is young lady in [New Haven]," he marveled, "who is beloved of that almighty Being, who made and rules the world, and that there are certain seasons in which this great Being, in some way or other invisible, comes to her and fills her mind with exceeding sweet delight, and that she hardly cares for anything, except to meditate on him."43 With thoughts of Sarah and New Haven in his mind, Edwards nevertheless duly reported to Bolton in early November and took up his pastoral responsibilities.

After only a few months in Bolton, however, Edwards was rejoicing in a turn of providence that would send him back to New Haven. In the spring of 1724, Sarah's brother resigned his tutorship at Yale, creating a vacancy at the college. Edwards had already expressed strong interest in the position, and the trustees knew that he was highly qualified, so in May they offered him an appointment.44 Just a few weeks later, Edwards eagerly returned to New Haven and began his new duties. His enthusiasm for the job quickly turned to discouragement, however, for the college was even less orderly than when he last resided on campus two years earlier as a graduate student. Following Cutler's defection, Yale had difficulty recruiting a resident rector, and the day-to-day administration of the college fell once again to the tutors. Edwards and another new tutor were therefore responsible for running the college as the trustees continued their search for a permanent rector. In addition to providing instruction in every subject, he and his fellow tutor were in charge of the welfare and discipline of all the students.45 Given Jonathan's earlier impatience with his cousin, we can only imagine how overwhelmed he was by the more than fifty students under their charge. After only one week in his post, and perhaps seeing in his students the effects of the Fall in new light, he confessed in his diary, "I have now abundant reason to be convinced of the troublesomeness and vexation of the world, and that it never will be another kind of world."46

Edwards's tenure as a tutor at Yale was difficult in almost every way. By the end of his second year, he was physically exhausted, emotionally drained, and spiritually starved. On one trip back home to East Windsor for vacation, in fact, he was so tired and ill that he collapsed on the way and spent three months recuperating in North Haven before he returned to Yale. Most troubling for him, characteristically, was his spiritual state. He later recounted in his diary, reflecting on his time as a tutor, that he had been "for the most part in a low, sunk estate and condition, miserably senseless to what I used to be, about spiritual things."47

Despite the many challenges and heavy demands of being a tutor, at least his duties did not lack intellectual stimulation. Ever the polymath, Edwards no doubt enjoyed teaching a wide range of subjects to those students who were eager to learn. He also likely formed a close friendship with one of the M.A. students in residence at the time, Gilbert Tennent, later a prominent revivalist and trusted ally of Edwards. Even one of his most tedious tasks, sorting and cataloguing all the books of the library, surely brought some satisfaction and welcome relief from his daily grind.48 He nevertheless realized it was time to move on.

As Edwards pondered his next step, providence again opened a door. In the spring of 1726, it was announced that the legendary Solomon Stoddard, Jonathan's grandfather and the long-time pastor of the church in Northampton, Massachusetts, needed an assistant. Now in his eighties, Stoddard could no longer meet single-handedly the demands of the congregation, which was the largest in New England outside of Boston. The church hoped to recruit an able associate to help Stoddard as needed, and the obvious implication was that their aging pastor would soon retire and pass on the reigns of this prominent parish. Stoddard no doubt recommended his bright grandson, and the congregation subsequently invited him to preach. Being favorably impressed with Edwards, they voted to extend him a call in the fall of 1726, and he accepted. With his formal education and training at Yale completed, despite the many twists and turns, he was now equipped and ready to begin his remarkable public career as preacher, pastor, philosopher, and theologian.

[1] When, most senior gentlemen of the audience, I find myself here about to address you in the midst of a gathering, at the heart of which the greatest teaching abounds and the pinnacle of knowledge resides and from which virtue unblemished shines forth; when I find myself about to deliver this address to you, because I am a man possessed solely of a mind endowed with a fortunate productiveness, furnished with eloquence and greatly equipped with expert knowledge, it is right for me to go on.Yet suddenly I am gripped by an inability to speak and by the greatest sense of wonder.I am held fast in awe, and indeed as I turn my terrified gaze upon myself (though I hardly dare to do so), my spirit shudders greatly at my words, and so too my thoughts are everywhere filled with you; I return to them, going over them again and again (and they terrify me too), and now indeed I have lost all confidence.Alas!What a state I am in!I am enveloped by trembling wherever I turn, now by immobility and inability too.I am a man condemned.I wish I could leave this place and there be a man standing upon this platform, who would not be a disgrace with his scholarship to him and to the school (and we take special pride that the seat of this school is as powerful as can be).What orator, indeed, could there be who would be worthy to stand before you, whose keenness of speech would not grow blunt with displays of eloquence, who could have expressed our gratitude and the reason for our gratitude?Finally, who could have spoken of our hopes so completely, of our departure from this community, of all our thoughts of farewell?But for me, destitute of all these qualities, spurred on by my contemplations of your generosity and relying on them entirely, it is fitting that with trembling tongue and palpitating heart I undertake this task doubtful of success.Therefore I beseech you, even if this state cannot be avoided, show your support for me, as I have begged you to do, since you are well disposed towards me.The presence of men such as you makes me want to reflect on the truth, but I am forbidden to speak.Whenever I look upon you and your excellent qualities, where I see Learning shining out with true radiance, embracing supreme virtues, where I see all your skills acting in response to each other mutually and in friendship, immediately my wonder at your teaching is stirred anew.O! It [2] adorns men so and its presence elevates them above mortals, and lays bare the deepest secrets.On sublime wings, by which we are carried above this terrestrial little ball, we seek in flight those vast regions across the deep, and by them those spheres shining forth in everlasting circles, in an eternal orbit are turned in perpetual motion, where we walk all over the Heavenly plains as they gleam all around-both you blessed scholars who are in such a way versed in these things and we poor men abandoning them.Now it seems to me there are pleasures of that sort for us and for everyone else, and also for you, while we all take part in these seemly Commencement exercises, as you must bid us farewell at this gathering.And so, to you first, Reverend Sir, TIMOTHY CUTLER-outstanding in scholarship among the best, most famous head of this School, and our honored Rector of highest distinction-humbly and with deep deference may I be allowed to address you:How we deem you always the perennial fountain of the body, the life and motion of this School, the source of its living spirits, for which we have constantly been waiting and to which we have everyday been disposed.And now we are elated at how with your beneficial influence we see clearly the Academy brought so suddenly from its infancy to its perfection; how far we have been nurtured by those roots spread all around by you at the heart of this system of learning, as if warmed by the sun in its core, and is in the same place as that faint teaching, shining out at us glimmering [. . .]

[. . .] we have enjoyed it for so long at the right time, nor do we even hold back from you our sense of attachment to it.And for that pleasing fellowship by which we have been refreshed, for your refined company-and we have found it hard to contemplate leaving it-and for your outstanding support for our studies, and also for the aid given to us most efficiently and ably, we offer our sincere thanks.A profound happiness fills us as we gaze upon you, to see such swift progress from when our learning first began.As you journey, like plants germinating and then flowering, across from our mother's soil, we are bound to call her blessed, and in the same way and much more than when you began, we desire that this progress be pursued.You are on a journey to the absolute peak of learning, and you will take great pleasure from the satisfactions that are to be enjoyed from there.[5] Although we will now be separated from you and no longer in contact, yet however in our minds your faces and your expressions, as if carved from marble or sculpted from bronze, will remain for as long as we are alive.Moreover, we desire strongly that you remain free from all care.You have reached the greatest honors, the greatest heavenly gifts, and indeed total health.Still, in everything and at all times fare you well.Fare you well our friends (I say).May you be safe, may you flourish, may you be blessed.May all your teaching and virtue-which is your own teacher-and prosperity last forever.

And finally with all of you present, allowing yourselves to listen to my voice and being schooled by Minerva-as much in the best of the highest liberal arts as in these individual pursuits of yours-to you, (I say) who are allowing yourselves to be interested in this undertaking and now showing respect, although it is surely little merited:it would not have been a bore for you to listen to our farewells.Moreover, as we have amassed every sort of kindness from you while we have been here, we are brought together in gratitude.Now clearly we are experienced in the truth of what has been said by everyone.But what is not indeed in the experience of all about the sweetness of the college life, whether the reason may lie with you, is that this community is filled with delights from all sides and that there is a breeze so healthful and utterly vivifying.Yet the thoughts that distress us intolerably about leaving these delights do not allow me to say my final words, so that even as we are about to leave we simply cannot express ourselves adequately as we look forward to the school flourishing with the greatest splendor and the ultimate glory.It becomes finally a home to Apollo, a Palladian palace of the Palladian god.And those highest points and foundations that it has now, it will always have throughout this whole earth, throughout all nations and the generations of the world:may it be famous, and may it stand in the eternal beauty of our motherland.And I wish the same for you, whom it has in its heart, by your names and those of your descendants.You have impressed upon our hearts the honor in the walls of these halls, a place sacred to Minerva, such that your titles and your reputation will remain [6] impressed in letters on imperishable certificates.You will be supported, you and those who could be called its limbs, at times in pleasant tranquillity, always in tranquil pleasure.

Now, however, may everyone fare well.Farewell to you celebrated guides, and farewell to our fellow students, lovers of learning.May all those always present in their scholarship fare well.Farewell all, and farewell everyone. :I HAVE SPOKEN:

Delivered before a Gathering.
