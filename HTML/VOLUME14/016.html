None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness

None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness is an important statement on distinguishing true striving for grace from self-righteousness. The main point Edwards pursues is the danger of assuming that God is laid under an obligation to show mercy because of a person's good works. Edwards warns his congregation that expecting a reward for personal holiness is self-righteousness. To allow for any kind of natural or moral excellency, as new schools of thought were doing, is to Edwards as much as saying that some people are better than others and not equally depraved, and therefore not equally in need of grace. Edwards here makes points also found in a recently begun notebook on "Faith," particularly in entry nos. 34 and 36, written shortly before or concurrent with this sermon, which was delivered in late February or March 1729. He states that if God is inclined by good works, then his offended justice and majesty are of no account, and that the eternal "rule of righteousness," which sets death as the wage of sin, is abrogated.

Edwards had said as much in his Quæstio of 1723, but his sophistication six years later reflects how he has applied himself to the complexities of the issue of justification. He is at pains to distinguish precisely what is and what is not trusting in one's own righteousness. In the first objection and answer, which comes at the end of the doctrinal section, Edwards addresses the question of how God "rewards" good works. Here he draws almost verbatim on "Miscellanies" No. 367, entitled "Degrees of Glory." If, as Scripture states, God does indeed reward good works in heaven, then how could it be said that God does not regard works at all in bestowing saving grace? Edwards explains this seeming contradiction by pointing to the degrees of glory that will exist in heaven: some shall be happier than others simply because God has given them more of a "capacity" for happiness. But if Christ's righteousness is given to all the saints equally, how can heaven be happier for some than for others? Edwards answers that the imputation of Christ's righteousness has no bearing on the degrees of happiness.

-- 330 --

Christ's death only fulfills the covenant of works for humankind, and it is God's sovereign pleasure that determines individual capacity.

But Edwards goes on to make finer distinctions, in the process co-opting the language of sentimentalism while adapting the notion of a wholly benevolent God to his covenantal Calvinism. Having said that God does not reward good works, he argues that it is just as incorrect to believe that God does not take "delight" in the holiness and good works of the saints. While it is self-righteous to think that God is obliged to reward holiness, it is not self-righteous to hope that God is pleased with works done from a sincere desire for godliness. Indeed, a person should "take encouragement from his own striving and diligence." In the covenant, God promises salvation to those who strive, and so saints should lay claim to that promise. For Edwards, the impetus to good works is not that God looks at the efficacy of good works—for they have none—but rather that he looks at the effort. God ordinarily does not convert those who do not strive to do good works. "Taking encouragement" from personal striving is vastly different from believing that that striving "makes God willing to convert them."

Edwards concludes with a series of six signs of trusting in one's own righteousness that apparently draws on the "Signs of Godliness" notebook and anticipates the later works on the signs of grace. It is the inner disposition, he argues, that makes the whole difference in obtaining grace. A "murmuring and quarreling" spirit is the opposite of the true Christian spirit of meekness and resignedness. Impatience is the opposite of a persevering holiness. Hoping for mercy because one is a great sinner, Edwards teaches, actually implies a perverse self-righteousness in that it assumes that the attainment of personal holiness would make a person more acceptable to God. Giving way to thoughts of atheism because one has not been converted is likewise self-righteous. "Partial obedience," following only some of God's commands and omitting others, shows that one believes that the few things that are done should suffice. Finally, "supposing anything of grace" reveals a contentedness with the measure of grace one has, and a desire not to have more.

The manuscript is composed of twenty-four leaves, the first twenty-two duodecimo and the last two octavo. Edwards' shorthand notations indicate that the sermon was preached a second time on an unspecified

-- 331 --

date. Minor insertions and brackets were added in revising for repreaching. The brackets enclose the "definition" and the series of four "favors" that describe what unconverted persons are apt to hope for from their righteousness. Edwards folded the final two octavo leaves to make them the same size as the duodecimo, resulting in damage, mostly in the form of words rubbed away along the folds.

This sermon lacks the formal separations between preaching units that Edwards normally used, namely, repetition of the text, doctrine, and, on occasion, a brief summary of the propositions. Thus, it is not certain how many occasions Edwards took to preach this sermon, though in all probability it was two. This lack of formal separation may be a function of the structure of the sermon, which is uncommonly varied, containing two series of objections and answers, a "definition," a "motive," "cautions," and "signs." All but one series of objections and answers appears in the Application, making that section of the sermon by far the longest.

The absence of preaching divisions may also reflect the possible purpose of the sermon, which may have been delivered, at least in part, as a lecture or lecture-series. The Application is very analytical and, in many respects, is virtually indistinguishable from the discussion of the Doctrine.

None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness Titus 3:5.1 Not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved us, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost.

The Apostle is charging Timothy to put those that he preached to in mind "to speak evil of no man, to be no brawlers, showing all meekness unto all men" [Titus 3:2], as being a very unbecoming thing for Christians, for those that are the redeemed of Christ; for this reason, because those that are become Christians, that are free from the vices of other men, yet are naturally of the same corrupt hearts, the same wicked principle as they, and once lived a wicked life as well as they. Titus 3:3, "For we ourselves also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, and hating one another." And therefore, instead of talking against others and reproaching them, we ought to remember our own sins and consider how vile we are.

And especially because 'tis mere grace has us otherwise. Titus 3:4–5, "But after that the kindness and love of God our Savior towards man appeared, not by works of righteousness which we have done, but according to his mercy he saved, by the washing of regeneration, and renewing of the Holy Ghost."

It was not because we were anything better than they in ourselves, that we ben't guilty of those sins and follies that others [are]. We once were as bad as they, as God only by his mere grace has made the difference. And therefore surely we ought [not] to speak evil of others, reproach and rail against them: this is exceeding unsuitable for them

-- 333 --

that are redeemed and saved from sin by the mere mercy and grace of God.

The Apostle in this verse is showing of us how we are saved.

[1.] In the first place, he shows upon what motive it is that we [are saved].

(1) Negatively proposed. "Not by works of righteousness which we have done." That is, not upon the account of any of our works of righteousness.

(2) Positively. "But according to his mercy he saved us."

2. Here is the inward work that is wrought by which our salvation is accomplished; that is, by the washing of regeneration, and regeneration and renewing of the Holy Ghost. The washing of regeneration seems to include two things:

(1) The washing from guilt which attends regeneration, and so is the same with justification.

(2) The washing from the pollution of sin, which is regeneration itself, and is the same with the renewing of the Holy Ghost, spoken of in the last clause.

Doctrine.
There are none saved by their own righteousness.

That is, there are none saved upon the account of their own moral or religious excellency or goodness, or any qualification of the person, any good disposition of the heart, or any good actions, either sincere or not sincere.

There are none saved upon the account of any habitual excellency, either the excellency of the natural temper or any good qualification obtained by education, or any moral or religious habit obtained by frequent acts or any truly gracious habit. Nor upon the account of any labor, diligence, devotion2 or affection in religion, or any exactness or brightness of morality, any justice or charity, though they should give all their goods to feed the poor. Men are not saved at all upon the account of any such habits or works of righteousness.

There are these three things principally meant by being saved:

1. Conversion. There are some that God is pleased to make sensible of their miserable condition, of the great need they stand in of a savior, and to open their eyes, to discover the glory and fullness of Jesus Christ to them; to draw their hearts to him and persuade to give up

-- 334 --

themselves to God; to be devoted to his service; to choose him for their portion, and to sell all for Jesus Christ. And [God] quite changes their nature: takes away the heart of stone out of their flesh and gives them a heart of flesh, and makes them to live a new life.

And hereby God redeems them from bondage unto sin and captivity by Satan. He sets them at liberty from their cruel slavery,3 and gives them the liberty of the sons of God.

This is not at all upon the account of our own righteousness. God don't bestow this great mercy upon some and not upon others because some are more deserving of it than others, because they are of a better disposition naturally, or because they done more good, han't been so wicked4 in their lives as others. Those that are converted, before are as other men, under the same power of sin and living a sinful life. As the Apostle says in the third verse of the context, "We also were sometimes foolish, disobedient, deceived, serving divers lusts and pleasures, living in malice and envy, hateful, and hating one another." God takes them out of the midst of their sins and makes them partakers of the divine nature. Romans 9:16, "'Tis not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy."

2. Justification. Those that God is pleased to convert and call unto Jesus Christ, them he justified. Romans 8:30, "Whom he called, them he also justified." As soon as ever they accept of Jesus Christ and come to him, their sins are all blotted out. Whatsoever wickedness they have been guilty of, God remembers it no more, and they are released from punishment. And not only so, but they are taken into favor, and are adjudged to eternal life; they have a right given them to all those glorious blessings which Christ purchased.

Neither is this upon the account of any righteousness of ours. This is the very end of Christ's righteous obedience and death, to justify us. If we were justified by our own righteousness, Christ would have been dead in vain [Galatians 2:21].

3. The actual bestowment, called "redemption" (1 Corinthians 1:30).

But we will briefly give the reasons why we can't be saved by our own righteousness.

I. 'Tis not consistent with the honor of God's majesty and authority that we should be saved in such a way. We have been guilty of sin, whereby we have affronted and despised God's infinite majesty. We have violated the law and despised the authority of an infinite God.

-- 335 --

Now if this contempt of divine authority and majesty may be allowed of, if he that governs the world tolerates [it] without some suitable manifestation of the evil of it by his opposition to it, and in his providence showing how dreadful a thing it is to affront such a majesty by punishing of it, it would be an obscuring of God's majesty, and it would not appear such a dreadful thing to cast contempt upon it as it is. There would be no proper manifestation of the evil of the contempt of his majesty, and consequently no proper vindication or defense of God's majesty itself.

So the honor of God's authority would not be preserved if, when his authority was disregarded, and his law, enforced with threatening, should be violated, the violation should be tolerated and let pass without any manner of notice or manifestation of resentment. It would surely make his authority not appear so sacred.

A law without sanctions, that is, without being enforced with threatenings of punishment and promises of rewards, is no law at all; and therefore, if when God's law thus enforced is violated and the violation suffered to pass unregarded and without any compensation, that would be the way to make God's authority appear no authority at all, or at least his authority would not be sufficiently guarded. It is proper that when God doth exercise his authority, that he should make it to appear to be the authority of a God.

But men's own righteousness can be no compensation at all, both because that all the works of unconverted, unjustified men have no goodness at all in them, being done from no good principle, and because those works that have some goodness in them, being done from a gracious principle, they can bear no proportion at all to the sin they are guilty of; yea, though they had been guilty but of one sin, because every sin is an infinitely aggravated evil. Much more when they have been guilty of so much sin.

II. That men should be saved for their righteousness is not consistent with the honor of God's holiness and justice. 'Tis the honor of God's holiness that it should be manifested in its genuine effects. Now if God be infinitely holy, he infinitely abhors sin and wickedness; and therefore it is for the honor of that holiness that he should manifest that hatred of sin, not only by saying that he hates it, but by making of it appear in his providence. But it would not appear if God suffered sin to be in the world without taking any notice of it, by way of resentment of it, except there were some suitable compensation made.

Now in Jesus Christ God does manifest his resentment to the full, as

-- 336 --

much as if the sinner was eternally damned; he suffered in our stead, and the dignity of his person makes amends for the temporality of his sufferings. And Christ also offers compensation by his meritorious righteousness. But our own righteousness, as we said before, makes no compensation.

The honor of God's justice won't suffer sin to go unpunished. 'Tis a dispute whether God's absolute justice obliges him to punish sin; but his rectoral justice, as they call it, that is, as judge and governor of the world, requires it, and as it belongs to him to see to the order of it.

III. That eternal rule of righteousness which God has established will not allow of it. The rule of righteousness, which God never altered, which shall never pass away—the heaven and earth passes away, which is the law—fixes death as the wages of sin and perfect obedience as the only price of eternal life. And therefore, unless men could both die and satisfy for their past sins, and also perfectly obey the law of God, it will be in vain for them to seek to be saved by their own righteousness. Both which are impossible: he can't die and satisfy and then have eternal life after that, because the death spoken of is eternal death or its equivalent. And then there is no man can perfectly obey the law of God in this world; yea, so far from that, that without being born again, he can't perform one act of sincere obedience. But 'tis the obedience of heart that the law requires.

These things equally argue against the possibility of man's being converted upon the account of our own righteousness, as of being justified or glorified. For if we suppose that God's heart is inclined by the seeing of men's goodness to convert them that they may be justified, supposes that they are justified already, at least in part; for unless God's anger for sin was at least partly appeased, and he began to be inclined to their favor by their good works, he could not be inclined by them to convert them.5 If God were as angry as ever he was still, why then his heart would not be at all the more inclined to bring them out of a state of damnation.

IV. Lastly, this is contrary to God's design of glorifying his free grace by Jesus Christ. That is the design which God has had upon his heart from all eternity, and which he early manifested, and for which indeed

-- 337 --

he made this world and in subordination to which he orders and disposes all things. In this way, God always intended to glorify himself and his Son. They therefore that think to be saved by their own righteousness, they what in them lies do [to] overthrow this whole design. Romans 4:16, "Therefore it is of faith, that it might be by grace."6 1 Corinthians 1:29, "That no flesh should glory in his presence."

Obj. How can it be that our salvation should not be at all upon the account of our own righteousness, when we are taught in the Word of God that the saints' good works shall be all rewarded in heaven? And that if we give a cup of cold water to a disciple in the name of a disciple [Matthew 10:42], we shall in no case lose our reward? So that those that do fewer good works will have a less reward, and they that do more good works will have a greater reward. 2 Corinthians 9:6, "He that sows sparingly shall reap sparingly; and he that sows bountifully shall reap also bountifully."

Now the objection lies here: If it be so that eternal happiness in heaven is given in reward of good works, so much that if they do perform such and such good works they will have more happiness, how can it be otherwise than that salvation should be given partly at least upon the account of our own works? The saints all equally have Christ's righteousness imputed to them. Now if salvation be given only upon the account of Christ's righteousness, why is not their happiness equal?

Ans. To this objection, I answer, that Christ by his righteousness purchased that every believer7 should have perfect happiness, or as much as he was capable of, but the degree of capacity was not determined by his righteousness.

We are saved wholly and entirely by Christ's righteousness. Now by "salvation" is meant a perfect deliverance from all misery and the bestowment of eternal life; by "eternal life" is meant happiness that is perfect or that fills the capacity of the creature. But this hinders not but that the saints' being of various capacities may have various degrees of happiness, and yet the perfect happiness of every one be the fruit of Christ's purchase.

Indeed, it can't be properly said that Christ purchased any particular degree of happiness; but in the general he purchased perfect happiness,

-- 338 --

or which is the same thing, he purchased that everyone's capacity should be filled. The saints are like so many vessels of different sizes cast into a sea of happiness, where every vessel is full. This is eternal life for a man, forever to have his capacity filled. This Christ purchased. But after all, 'tis left to God's sovereign pleasure; 'tis still his prerogative to determine the largeness of the vessel, and he may determine how he pleases. Christ's death and righteousness did not meddle with it, but left it to God's pleasure to determine; and therefore he may dispense in this matter, according to what rule he pleases, not the less for what Christ has done. He may either dispense without condition or upon condition; and if he does dispense upon condition, he may do it upon what condition he pleases to fix. Christ's righteousness don't meddle with this matter.8

The covenant of works did not meddle with this matter. If Adam had perfectly obeyed the law, he and his posterity would have had eternal life; that is, they would have been completely blessed, or would have had so much happiness everyone as to fill his capacity. But God would have been at liberty still to have made some of one capacity, and others of another, as he pleased.

The angels had eternal life by a covenant of works upon condition of perfect obedience. They all of them performed the same condition, and they all thereby obtained complete blessedness, that everyone should be filled. But yet we are made acquainted that there are degrees amongst the angels because God gave them their capacities as he pleased; their perfect obedience did not meddle with this matter. And for the same reason, Adam's perfect obedience would not. And if Adam's perfect obedience would not have been concerned in this matter, then it follows that Christ's perfect obedience is not: for Christ only fulfilled the covenant of works for us, and performed that obedience which Adam should have performed.

And therefore God is still at liberty to dispense as to this matter in what method and upon what terms he pleases, or, if he pleases, without terms. But he has been pleased to fix the degree of capacity, and so of glory, to the proportionable degree of grace and fruitfulness. 'Tis his free and sovereign act that he doth so: he gives higher degrees of glory as a reward to the higher degrees of good works, not because it deserves it, but because it pleases him.

-- 339 --

Thus it appears that all the happiness of every saint is purchased by Christ in the same sense as the angels have all their happiness in reward of their perfect obedience, although they have different degrees of happiness and all performed but the same condition, the same perfect obedience.

If it had been but one man that Christ died for, if it had pleased God to make him of a very large capacity of happiness, Christ's perfect obedience would have purchased that his capacity should be filled, and then all his happiness might properly be said to be the purchase of Christ's perfect obedience. Although if he had been of a less capacity, he would have had so much less happiness by the same obedience of Christ, and yet he would have had as much as Christ merited for him. And yet Christ's righteousness don't properly meddle with the degrees of happiness any otherwise than as perfect happiness may be said to be a degree with respect to imperfect. But it was not concerned with degrees of perfect happiness.

Application.

And let us make this use of the doctrine, to beware that we don't trust in our righteousness.

And here, we shall endeavor to show distinctly what is and what is not trusting to our own righteousness, that you may know whether you trust in your own righteousness or no, and that you may [know] wherein you do and wherein you do not. It is an inquiry of very great concern because it is a matter that very immediately concerns our soul's salvation, that we should not trust in our own righteousness. If we do do it, it is a fatal disease. It therefore concerns [us] to know the disease, that we may be the better directed in seeking a cure. It behooves us to know what is trusting in our own righteousness, that we know what to condemn ourselves in and what not, and what to allow ourselves and what not.

[I.] I shall therefore show what is not trusting in our own righteousness.

First. A person may think that God takes delight in his services and in him for them, and yet that [may] not be trusting in his own righteousness. Because some persons have good ground to think so: those that have good ground to think that what they do, they do in sincerity and from a gracious respect to God, they have good ground from the Word of God to think that God delights in what they do. Thus God takes

-- 340 --

delight in those prayers that are made in sincerity. Proverbs 15:8, "The prayer of the upright is his delight." The spiritual sacrifices that the saints offer up, their charity and their good works, are acceptable sacrifices; they are like a sweet savor unto God. Philippians 4:18, "I have received the things that were sent from you, an odor of a sweet smell, a sacrifice acceptable and well-pleasing to God."

And God takes delight in them for their holiness and good works. The holiness and good works of the saints are God's image. God takes delight in his people for his own image which he sees in them: they are God's beauty which he has put upon them, and God takes delight in his saints because he has made them beautiful with his own beauty. Canticles 4:9, "Thou hast ravished my heart, my sister, my spouse; thou hast ravished my heart with one of thine eyes, with one chain of thy neck." These chains upon the spouse's neck are the graces of God's Spirit, so that Christ's heart is as it were ravished with the graces and holy exercises of his saints. And there Christ proceeds to enumerate the graces particularly: "Thy lips, O my spouse, drop as the honeycomb: honey and milk are under thy tongue" [Canticles 4:11]; that is, "Thy speech, thy prayers and praises, are most delightful to me." And Christ everywhere in that Song is setting forth how amiable that church was to him for her graces.

And it must needs be so, for God necessarily takes delight in his own image and in his own beauty wherever he sees it. Indeed, the best saints in this world, they have more deformity than they have of this spiritual beauty, but all their deformity is overlooked; it is put upon Christ's account, so that although there be so much filthiness and deformity in the saints, yet God sees none. "He hath not beheld iniquity in Jacob" [Numbers 23:21], he sees nothing but beauty in them. Therefore Christ says to his church, "Thou art all fair, my love; there is no spot in thee" (Canticles 4:7). Not that there is no spot really in the church here upon earth, for how exceedingly defiled are the hearts of the saints, and how defiled and spotted are their services: but God beholds it not; he imputes it not to them; he sees nothing but beauty.

There is a two-fold righteousness that the saints have: an imputed righteousness, and 'tis this only that avails anything to justification; and an inherent righteousness, that is, that holiness and grace which is in the hearts and lives of the saints. This is Christ's righteousness as well as imputed righteousness: imputed righteousness is Christ's righteousness accepted for them, inherent holiness is Christ's righteousness communicated to them. They derive their holiness from Christ as the fountain of it. He gives it by his Spirit, so that 'tis Christ's holiness

-- 341 --

communicated, 'tis the light of the sun reflected. Now God takes delight in the saints for both these: both for Christ's righteousness imputed and for Christ's holiness communicated, though 'tis the former only that avails anything to justification.

Therefore merely for a saint to think that God takes delight in him for his holiness and good works is not self-righteousness, though there is commonly much self-righteous spirit exercised in such thoughts. And that these ways:

1. Persons, and even saints themselves, are apt to think God takes delight in their services sometimes, when he does not. They are ready to set much by some services. It may be that [they] are not sincere. They have stirrings of affections, but they are from some other cause than gracious principle. And most commonly when there are affections even in the saints themselves, 'tis not all grace, but a great deal of it is from natural principles.

Now this is self-righteousness: 'tis a trusting in their own works. Their works are wholly their own; they are from themselves, and not from God.

2. When persons think that God takes delight in them for their holiness. Supposing their holiness from themselves, they don't keep in mind that 'tis God that gives them their good works and that makes them to differ from others. They are too apt to think that it was they that did [them]. They are excellent, and have made themselves so, and are ready to glory that God delights in them, that they should do so excellently of themselves: not enough considering that they have nothing to boast of, seeing that 'tis God that makes them to differ, and that God has put his beauty upon them, that he might delight in them, and that their beauty is not original to themselves.

3. When they think that God delights in them for their holiness for a false reason, not considering that it is only through Christ. If God should behold them as they are, with all their unworthiness, their filthiness and deformity, and did not overlook their deformity upon Christ's account, God would have no delight in them at all for that little holiness that they have, because their sin is of infinite demerit and their holiness is nothing put in the scale against it. Therefore the good works of the saints are pleasant and delightful to God only through Jesus Christ.9 1 Peter 2:5, "Ye are a holy priesthood, to offer up spiritual sacrifices, acceptable to God by Jesus Christ." There is so much filthiness in the heart and so much defilement in the best duties of the

-- 342 --

saints, that they could not be acceptable to God, only as Christ takes away the defilement.

Therefore when persons are not so sensible of their unworthiness, but that they are ready to look of God as being greatly pleased with their holy services, without considering that they are so unworthy that they could not be accepted any other way but in Christ, as Christ takes away the defilement: when persons seem to consider themselves as being upon the account of their good works worthy that God should delight in them, this is self-righteousness. But a mere thinking that God takes delight in their sincere holiness, and in them for it, is not self-righteousness.

Second. Persons may have delight in the reflection on their own holiness and good works, and yet not therein trust in their own righteousness. A man may delight in holiness wheresoever he sees it, though it be in himself. Though a godly man may think he sees but very little holiness in himself, and may be greatly humbled that he has so little, but yet he may have delight in that little he has. A godly man delights in God's image, and he delights to see it [in] himself; and the more he loves God, the more pleasant will it be to him to see himself made like him. A godly man necessarily takes delight in his own holiness. If he knows that he has any holiness, the very nature of godliness itself infers a necessity of delighting in holiness in general; and natural self-love makes it necessary that if he does delight in holiness at all, that it should delight especially to see it in himself. He delights in it upon a double account, both because God is glorified and because God enables him to glorify him.

'Tis great part of the happiness of the saints in heaven that they are made perfectly holy, that they shine bright with the beauty of Jesus Christ upon them, but it would be no happiness at all if they did not take any pleasure in it. It is doubtless for their happiness to be holy, and more for their happiness, than for one of their fellow saints, to be made perfectly holy. But yet there is no self-righteousness in heaven.

Not but that very often the pleasure that men have in reflecting upon their own goodness is nothing but self-righteousness. And very commonly, there is a great mixture of self-righteousness in the pleasure that true saints have in the reflecting on their holiness. There is a mixture of spiritual pride with it.

There is a great difference between a self-righteous pleasure that that man takes in the reflections on their own goodness and the holy pleasure in God's image, such as the saints have in heaven. This is a

-- 343 --

humble pleasure, that although it rejoices, yet it don't lift up. It is not a delight that works a disposition to boast and glory in themselves; it is with a sense of their own unworthiness.

It is a thankful pleasure. They don't rejoice in it as being any goodness from themselves—though it be in themselves—but as the gift of God to them. The more there is of this pleasure, if it be not a self-righteous pleasure, the more will there be of a disposition to give glory to God.

Third. Persons may expect to be rewarded for their good works and yet not trust in their own righteousness. Yea, 'tis the duty of the saints to expect it because God has promised it, and it is the duty of saints to believe God's promises. 'Tis so far from being self-righteousness, that it is trusting to God's faithfulness. 'Tis our duty by good works to strive for higher degrees of glory, to lay up treasure in heaven. We are directed to make Jesus Christ our example, "who for the joy that was set before him endured the cross" [Hebrews 12:2].

Fourth. 'Tis not all sort of encouragement that an unconverted man can take from what he himself does is trusting to his own righteousness. A person may take encouragement from his own striving and diligence in seeking salvation. That is, he may think that there is more hopes, more probability, of his being converted because God enables him to strive, and with steadiness and constancy and earnestness to labor for salvation, than if he was dull and remiss and inconstant, and committed known sins and neglected known duties. For there is really a greater probability—though there be no certainty, yet there is a greater probability—that one that with great earnestness and diligence seeks salvation will obtain than one that is a negligent seeker. And if there be really a greater probability, then it is no self-righteousness in a man to think so.

'Tis no self-righteousness for men to take encouragement where God gives encouragement. But God gives encouragement in his Word to those that strive for salvation. Though there be no promise, yet there is encouragement. There is a great deal of encouragement in those words, "Strive to enter in at the strait gate: for many, I say unto you, [will seek to enter in,] and shall not be able" [Luke 13:34].1 In the first place, there is encouragement in it because we are directed to this striving as being the best way we can take; and then again, it is plainly implied in the words that this is a more likely way than barely seeking.

-- 344 --

Which makes good what we said before, that there is a greater probability that they will obtain salvation that seek it earnestly than they that seek it negligently. And therefore, he that strives may think so.

And as God gives encouragement in his Word, so he does in his providence to them that strive for salvation. This has been found by experience to be the ordinary way wherein God meets with men and converts them. It is found by experience that a constant persevering striving for salvation is generally attended with success; and therefore he that strives may, yea, ought to take the encouragement that God gives, and it is the duty of ministers to encourage such with these encouragements.

Not but that there is generally a great deal of self-righteousness in the encouragement which natural men take from their own striving. The foundation of their encouragement is not only the encouragement that is given in God's Word, as we shall show presently. And when they do take encouragement from God's Word, they do [it] in a wrong way: they take other encouragement than is given; they are ready to look upon God [as] obliged by the encouragements he has given in his Word. Whereas the encouragement God has given is only thus far: that when God does bestow converting grace, he will ordinarily bestow it on such as strive.

Obj. It may be objected that if God has given any encouragement that he will do a thing, why is it not equivalent to a promise? Why is he not in fullness obliged to do it?

Ans. We can't fairly understand God's Word so that he so much as gives encouragement that every particular person that strives shall be converted. God has neither promised nor given encouragement so far as that, but only that when God does convert any, he will ordinarily convert those that he finds in this way, still reserving to himself his sovereign liberty of showing mercy to whom he will show mercy. The command to strive infers no more than this. God gives encouragement in his Word to them that strive no otherwise than in his providence. God in his providence shows us that 'tis his manner, when he converts any, to convert him in this way; but yet this encouragement of providence brings God under no obligation to convert all such. No more does the encouragement of his Word.

Indeed, the encouragement to those that seek God are sometimes in positive terms, as if they were absolute promises. Proverbs 8:17, they "that seek me early shall find me." Proverbs 2:2–4, "If thou criest after knowledge, and liftest up thy voice for understanding; if thou seekest her as silver, and searchest for her as for hid treasures."

-- 345 --

But these and other like places may be understood two ways. The seeking spoken of may be understood of a sincere, true, gracious seeking, as that place, "Seek and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be opened to you" (Matthew 7:7). And so they are absolute promises. For indeed, ungodly men can't really seek God or seek wisdom: for to seek to be delivered from hell by God is not to seek God, and to seek salvation from hell by wisdom is not to seek wisdom.

Or else those places may be understood of the unconverted seeking God. And then they are no other than indefinite promises, and imply no more than that it is the likeliest way to find God to seek him early, and it is the likeliest way to find wisdom to cry after it and "lift up thy voice for understanding" [Proverbs 2:3].

[II.] We now proceed to show positively what it is for men to trust in their own righteousness. And you may take this for a definition of it:

Def. It is to conceive hopes of some favor of God from a false notion of the relation our own goodness or excellency bears to that favor.

When men compare themselves with their own moral goodness, either of their hearts or actions, with some favor of God which they desire, and have a wrong notion of the proportion or relation which their goodness bears to that divine favor, and thence to conceive hopes of it, that is to trust in their own righteousness.2

Whether this goodness of theirs be only supposed as real and true spiritual goodness, yet if they have hopes of God's favor from it from a wrong notion of the relation it bears to that favor—they make it the foundation of their hopes—it is self-righteousness. For men may make a righteousness of true and saving grace, and even of the grace of faith itself.

And let this goodness be a positive goodness and excellency, or only a negative one, that they are not so bad as others, it alters not the case. A man may be self-righteous and yet think he has no true excellency at all; he may make a righteousness of his negative excellency, that he is not so bad as such and such.

'Tis plainly the design of the gospel to exalt God's free grace and love, and the excellency and fullness of Christ Jesus the Savior; and in order to that, to show us our own great and entire unworthiness and emptiness of all excellency in ourselves, and that it is not any proportion or any manner of relation between our excellency that moves God to bestow upon us those great blessings of the gospel, but mere grace. And therefore those that think there is any proportion between any of

-- 346 --

their excellency and any of these blessings, they are far from closing with the design of the gospel.

But this definition of self-righteousness may possibly be better understood by showing particularly the ways men are wont to trust in their own righteousness by conceiving wrong notions of the relation there is between their own goodness and God's favor.

First. When persons imagine that any of their goodness or righteousness lays an obligation upon God in mere justice to show them some favor. Or which is the same thing, when they think they, by what they are or by what they do, merit some favor of God.

There are some in the world that do professedly hold the doctrine of merit, and there are many that disclaim do yet secretly in their hearts believe it. There are probably hardly any amongst us but what if they were asked whether they believed the popish doctrine of merit—that we could merit anything of God by our works—would say no, and possibly would readily bring Scripture for it, where we are told that, after we have done all, we should say, "We are unprofitable servants" [Luke 17:10].

But though they disown the doctrine in general, yet when they come to particulars, they plainly show that they do not disown it, but do think that God is obliged injustice to show mercy to them. Perhaps they won't express it in such terms, but they think God won't do fairly if he has no mercy upon them, seeing they attended his rules for so long a time and have taken such pains as they have done. They have done as much as it was in their power to do.

'Tis evident a great many do think thus, or else why do they complain, why have they such hard thoughts of God, why have they such risings of heart against him for his not dealing no more mercifully with them, if they don't believe that he is in justice bound to show them favor? If they do really believe that it is fair for God to do as he does, they could not have such heart risings about it.3

Second. When persons think that God can't answer the character of a merciful and gracious being without showing them some favor for what they do. And so they think they have got some hold of God by the declarations which he makes of his mercifulness and graces and loathness to punish sinners. They think if God has no regard at all to their frequent and earnest prayers and cries and diligent striving, but hies

-- 347 --

himself utterly from them, and at last in anger casts them down into eternal misery, then they think God will not be such a merciful God as he declares himself, but it seems to them that he'll be very hard-hearted. If for all his labor and strivings night and day he can have no pity, can't so much as obtain one favorable look from God, they think this will be very hard. God will not act like one that is full of mercy.

Now this is utterly a wrong notion of the relation their works bear to God's favor. There is no such necessary connection between their labors and prayers and God's mercy, as they imagine. God can be infinitely merciful without showing them any mercy. God may show his mercy to be full answerable to the declarations in his Word, and yet have no regard to their prayers or striving.

Third. When they think it would be no other than a suitable and decent thing for God to show them favor out of respect to their works. Whether they think God is obliged in strict justice or no, yet if they think it would be very proper and becoming in him to have so much respect to their religion or morality as to be favorable to them for it, and to entertain hopes from thence, this is self-righteousness. This is very common amongst natural men: it seems to them so suitable that God should be gracious to them, seeing they have done so much, that they can't but expect it; it seems to them that it would not be at all suitable for them to be rejected and cast into hell, that God would not deal excellently: he would not do that that would be to his honor, if he should so do.

Fourth. To imagine that God has brought himself under some obligation by his Word to have respect to what they do, where indeed he has not. This was the self-righteousness of the Jews: they went about to establish their own righteousness; they did not seek justification by faith, but by the works of the law; they expected to be justified in the sight of God by circumcision and strictly observing the ceremonies of the Levitical law; and they thought they had warrant for it from the Word of God. They judged that this was the way of justification pointed forth by the law of Moses.

And this is a very common thing still. The self-righteous man would feign make it out that he has some warrant from the Word of God for his hopes. They would feign catch hold of some promise when there is none for them to catch hold of. They interpret the Scripture wrong. And they are ready to imagine that God, by commanding of them to do such and such duties, lays him under an obligation to have a gracious respect to them when they obey those commands.

-- 348 --

We speak here of imagining God to have obliged himself by his Word to have respect to what men do where he has not, viz. to the insincere, ungracious works of natural men. God has not obliged himself to have any respect at all to anything that they [do], though he has been pleased to oblige himself to have respect to those good works that are done from a gracious respect to God. Though here also persons may be self-righteous by thinking that God has obliged himself to have such a respect, as he has not, and to bestow such favor as a reward of them, which he has not promised.

[III.] In the next place we shall show what are those favors of God which men are wont to hope for from their own righteousness, particularly unconverted men. They are principally these four:

First. Some think that God, upon the sight of what they do, will or ought to be the more inclined to forget their sins and receive them into favor. And so they directly place themselves in Christ's stead, and make themselves their own mediators, and make Christ Jesus of no use at all. Galatians 2:21, "If righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain."

Man is so foolish a creature that he is ready to imagine that he can make some amends for sinning against God, or sometimes by doing his duty of another. They are ready to think with themselves that, seeing they have forsaken their sins and been diligent in religion so long, God may well enough forget what is past. They plead in excuse for their sins that they were committed through the corruption of nature, that he could not help [it], and from the greatness of temptation and the like. And they think therefore, that seeing they have amended their ways and been sorry for them and have done so well of late, that God need not hold his anger forever.

Some are ready to have a secret thought in their heart that what they do is profitable to God, as if they some way or other did God good by their religion, and therefore that God should be the more ready to be reconciled to them and take them into favor.

There are several degrees of this. Some think that God by their righteousness won't only be inclined to pardon them, but to more than that, to bestow heaven upon them for their righteousness. So do many of the Papists, and the Pharisees of old. They were exceeding strict in many things: they fasted twice a week, and gave tithes of all they possessed and observed the tradition of the elders. And they thought they merited eternal life by it.

-- 349 --

Others, it may be, aim at no more than appeasing God, to make him not angry with them for their sins; and others, though they mayn't think that they can wholly get all their sins forever pardoned by their righteousness, yet hope that God's anger is abated. When they rise up from an earnest and affectionate prayer, they are ready to look upon it that now God is not so angry with them as he was before.

Second. Natural men are ready to think that God has a complacency and delight in them for what they do. They secretly think that [God] is pleased to behold how exact they are in their walk, how well they keep sabbaths, how they'll rise in the night and go to prayer. They imagine God's eye is upon them with pleasure and satisfaction.

This sort of self-righteousness more especially is wont to attend the religious affections of natural men. When they are much affected, it seems to them that God is affected too; and when they go away, they have that sense upon their minds that God likes 'em and is pleased with them for their affectionate duties.

Third. Many think that what they do ought to make God willing to convert them, and give them faith in Jesus Christ. They are abundantly taught that justification is only by faith in Jesus Christ. They say they can't obtain pardon and heaven by their own righteousness, but the way of salvation is by believing in Christ. And so although they don't directly expect to be pardoned for their works, yet they deceive themselves and entertain hopes that their works will make God willing to convert them and give them faith. They do expect that they shall be converted, and that is the foundation of their hopes. They think that their goodness and religion, their affection and diligence, will or ought to make God the more willing to bring them off from their own righteousness and to give them a true sight of Christ.

Now this is a very different thing from that taking the encouragement from their own striving given in God's Word and providence, or thinking that 'tis more likely that they shall be converted for their striving. Because 'tis God's manner, when he is pleased to convert men, to convert them in this way, and not ordinarily any out of this way; which we spoke of before, which is not self-righteousness.

For that is a very different thing from thinking that their striving makes God willing to convert them, or that it ought anything the more to incline God to it, or that God would be anything unfair or hard if he should refuse to convert them after all. For God is not obliged in justice, honor or decency to convert those that strive for conversion,

-- 350 --

nor has he said that he will convert all striving persons; but that he will convert whom he pleases of those that strive, and ordinarily never of those that don't strive. And God is not made at all the more willing to convert men because they strive, but he stirs up his elect to strive because he is willing to convert them. The

Fourth, and last way, is when they hope that their goodness will recommend them to the acceptance of a Savior. They say they don't expect at all to be saved by themselves, that they must come to Christ if ever they are saved; but they seek to get something to recommend them to Christ's acceptance. Many are ready to say they can't think that Christ would be willing to accept such vile creatures as they are now. They own there are gracious invitations in the gospel, but they can't think that Christ meant such wicked creatures as they are; and therefore they think they must get something better to come to Christ, that he may accept of them.

They are now all in filthy garments. They durst not go to Christ so filthy and loathsome. They would put on a better garment, that they may be more fit to come to Christ.

And some think they are so good that they are fit, and so they trust in Christ, but it is upon that encouragement. They think they don't trust in their own righteousness because what they aim at is to come to Christ; but they do as much trust in their own righteousness in reality as if they had no thought of Christ at all, for the whole foundation of their encouragement is their own righteousness.

They are principally, therefore, these four favors of God that self-righteous men conceive hopes of from their own work: either that God should justify them; or that he should have complacency in them; or that he should convert them; or that Christ should accept them. Thus we have shown what it is for men to trust in their own righteousness.

Therefore let us carefully beware of these things. What was said in the explication of the doctrine shows the unreasonableness of them. How vain are men to imagine that their supposed goodness bears some proportion to the favor of God, especially such favor and love as that is that moves God to convert and justify, and Christ to receive them.

How vain and childish is the thought that men entertain, that such a price as they have to offer to God is sufficient to purchase such things. Alas, the great God is not about to set forth his love to sale to the highest bidder, nor will he offer his converting grace or his Holy Spirit or his eternal glory for such poor pelf as men's own goodness, their

-- 351 --

poor prayers and a few tears of carnal affection, and their poor meditations and seekings, which are performed from no respect to anybody but themselves.

And how vain is it in men to imagine that they by these things make some amends for their affronting of the infinite majesty and violating the law of the God of Gods, especially so as to think that God is in justice bound to show them some favor, and that it would be unfair in him not to do it. How great is the vanity of man's heart, that he should think that he by his religion and righteousness has laid some tie upon the God of heaven and earth! God is so far from being bound in justice to have any mercy upon you for what you are or do, that his justice and holiness rather oblige him not to have mercy upon you upon any such account. It would dishonor his justice and holiness if he should show mercy upon any such account, as we have already shown.

And God is so far from being obliged in honor and decency to have so much respect to your works as to pardon you or convert you upon the account of them, that it would be a thing very ill-becoming of his majesty and glory. It would be a thing no way suitable, that one that has disobeyed the law of God should have any mercy at all shown him upon the account of [his] own works.

And God won't act at all cruelly and hardly if, after all your prayers and all your striving, he should let you alone to drop into hell. It would not be any reflection at all upon his mercy, nor would he in the least come short of those declarations of his mercy which he has made in his Word, wherein he has said he hath no delight in the death of a sinner, and that he delights in mercy, and to whom judgment is a strange work.4

God, if he should resolve to damn you for all that you have done, his mercy would not be at all the less glorious. He would not in any measure fail of his design of glorifying his grace because of that, though he should cast all your prayers as dung in your faces, yea, though instead of pitying of you, he should flash hell fire in your faces for the sin of your best prayers. God will glorify his mercy in the salvation of whom he will: some he will glorify his mercy upon, and others his vindictive justice.

And then God lies under no sort of tie from anything he has said in his Word, either directly or consequentially, not to damn you, notwithstanding

-- 352 --

all that you can do. And you may be sure of it, that God never will pardon or convert you upon the account of any of your righteousness. You never will be able to draw God's love by all that is in your power. Those that think to draw God's love, and so incline his heart to convert them, they don't only put themselves in Christ's stead, but they think to do more than Christ himself: for Christ did not purchase that love of God by which he was inclined to save his people.

Therefore let go your hold of your own righteousness; leave off such vanity; labor to see your own unworthiness. Be much in meditating of your sins and the wickedness of your heart and life, and no more harbor such a thought in your breast that God ought to show you mercy upon the account of what you do or have done in morality or religion.

Mot. I shall offer this one motive, and that is the fatalness of it. This is the rock upon which many thousands have split. They that trust in their own righteousness, they will not submit themselves to the righteousness of God. You must be emptied of yourself, and be brought to trust in Christ's sufficiency and come to God only in his name, if ever you are accepted. If your own righteousness is what you build upon, you build your house upon the sand; and when the wind blows and the rain descends and the floods of death come upon you, you'll fall, and great is like to be your fall.

I shall answer an objection here, and that is:

Obj. If I should quit all hopes from my own righteousness, I should immediately go into despair.

I answer, there is no need of it, upon two accounts:

First. There is an encouragement a person may take from his own striving which is not self-righteousness, as we have shown you already, because there is encouragement given in God's Word and providence. By both we are taught thus much, that this is God's ordinary way wherein he bestows saving grace, so that he very often does bestow it upon such, and ordinarily upon no others, though he has not promised that he will bestow it upon all such.

Second. If you were brought to see God's sovereignty, you would see that he might show you mercy if he pleased, as well as that he might refuse if he pleased. And that will raise a hope in you to see that God may show you mercy if he pleases. Self-righteous men under fear of hell ben't fully convinced of neither. They ben't convinced that God may refuse them mercy; it seems to them very hard that he should cast them down into hell, and that he should have no regard to their good

-- 353 --

works. Nor yet be they convinced that God may show them mercy. They sometimes are ready to argue with themselves that they are so great sinners, God never will show mercy to them. They are afraid there is no hopes for them. They have affronted God so much, they fear he is unreconcilable. They think his honor requires that they should be punished. And thus they are tossed between two opinions: their hearts are all in a tumult, sometimes thinking that [God] ought to show them mercy for their works, and that it would be very hard if he don't; and other times arguing that they are such great sinners that there is no hopes.

But if you saw God's sovereignty, then you would come to an conclusion that God might do as he pleased, and you would see plain that he might show you mercy if he pleased. And this would raise a hope and would ease your mind of burthen, and put an end to those tumults which now your wavering, hesitating, doubtful mind is in.

Two Cautions

I. Never shun your duty nor be the less careful to do it for fear you shall make a righteousness of it.

The way that God has appointed for men to seek salvation in, is a way of obedience to all his commandments to the utmost of the ability. And if a person neglect any duty for fear he should make a righteousness of it, he is not in God's way; they don't seek him in this way of his appointment. If a person may neglect one duty, why not another? If he may neglect deeds of charity [for] fear he should make a righteousness of it, why mayn't he neglect prayer too, or reading the Scriptures?

Therefore let your mind be intent upon every duty, and don't think that what you have to do is only to pray and read and go to meeting. But you must forgive injuries, and do good to them that do evil to you, and be liberal and charitable to the poor [and] be of a meek, patient, Christian behavior. If you intend to obtain conversion, you ought to be as much afraid to neglect these things as to neglect prayer or meditation or reading the Scripture; yea, God prefers the former to the latter, for he says he will have mercy, and not sacrifice [Hosea 6:6].

II. Never in the least give way to any sinful thoughts, that you may see your heart and be brought off from your own righteousness. It is what you have many a time heard, that if you would ever be converted, you must first thoroughly reform and must tolerate in yourself no known sin; and you may as well curse and swear that you may see your own wickedness as give way to wicked, blasphemous thoughts that you may see your heart. Those that do thus have as much reason to fear

-- 354 --

that God will be provoked to take away his Spirit from them as those [that] give way to profane or lascivious language.

'Tis not the way to be aright sensible of our own vileness, to allow ourselves in sin that we may see ourselves to be wicked. 'Tis not the way, for men to go and commit adultery, that they may see their own hearts; nor is it the way, for men to commit blasphemy, that they may see their heart. They are the most likely to see the wickedness of their hearts who strive most against it and are most ashamed of it. Sin, if it be allowed, hardens and blinds. 'Tis striving against it that is the way to be sensible of the evil of it.

'Tis a contradiction to suppose that it is lawful to give way to sinful thoughts, that we may see our wickedness in it. For if men have that notion that it is lawful to give way to [sinful thoughts] because 'tis a necessary part of experience, and yet to see one's wickedness [in it]:5 for if it be lawful to give way to [them], then it is not wicked.6 And how can they see their wickedness in [that] that they think is lawful to do or allow?

Persons should be ashamed to publish their blasphemy and envy and wishing that persons was dead because they were converted before they, though they have been since converted and repented of it. If a man before he was converted had committed adultery, and God had made it a means of showing of him his heart, yet it would be a dishonor to God for him to publish it to the7 world. And so it is a dishonor to God for persons to publish their blasphemy or murderous envy. Commonly to publish such things makes them seem the less evil to others, and others are more apt to allow themselves in it for it. If a man should wish his neighbor dead, and have a design of killing of him from a private quarrel, he would be ashamed to tell of it; and he has as much reason when he wishes him dead out of hatred to him for his goodness, and not the less because God made a means of showing of it.

[IV.] I shall conclude by mentioning some things that are certain signs of men's trusting in their own righteousness.

First. All those who are of a murmuring and quarrelling spirit towards God, it is a certain sign they are under the prevailing of a self-righteous spirit. They would not murmur and quarrel with God for not showing them mercy if they were sensible that all that they can do lays no obligation at all upon God to show them mercy. They quarrel

-- 355 --

with God because they think he don't do as he ought to do, because they think he has not so much respect to their goodness as he ought.

Second. When persons have been seeking salvation a while and grow discouraged because they don't find [it], 'tis a certain sign of self-righteousness. If that was not their hope that they should obtain it of themselves through their own works, but were sensible that it was at God's pleasure to bestow or not if he pleased, and if he did bestow it, to bestow it when he pleased, they would not think that there is no hopes of ever obtaining it because they han't obtained it already. But they intended to work it out, and they find that method don't do, and so they are discouraged. If it was by works that men obtained conversion, if they had tried a great while and could not [make]8 it do, they would have reason to be discouraged; but if it only depends on God's pleasure, a man has no more reason to be discouraged after he has been striving ten years than when he first began.

Third. When persons are ready to despair of ever finding mercy because they have been great sinners, that is a sign {of self-righteousness}. That shows that they have an opinion that if they were not such great sinners, that is, if they had more righteousness, they should have some dependence upon it. If they think that God won't be willing to convert them because they are great sinners, that shows that they think if they were righteous, God would by that account be willing, and that is direct trusting to their own righteousness.

Fourth. When persons have been seeking a long time and question whether there be any truth in religion because they don't find [conversion], that shows that they think that if there be a God he would have had more regard to their works before this time and would have shown them mercy, seeing they have done so much for it. And because God has no more respect to what they do as stilt to let them alone and not convert them, they think there is no God.

Fifth. All those that rest in a partial obedience, it is a certain sign that they are hypocrites. They that forsake some sins and keep others, that do some duties and rest contented in that, and leave other duties, it is a sign that they think their doing some duties will make amends for their neglecting others. If they did not think so, they couldn't rest without doing all duties, or as if he shall trust in his own righteousness.

Sixth. Lastly, all those that trust [in a past day]9 of grace, and don't strive to get further, they rest in their own righteousness. It shows that

-- 356 --

the end that they propose in what righteousness they have is to get heaven, and nothing else; and they think they have got enough to carry 'em to heaven, and so they are contented. Which shows that what they do, they do from a legal spirit: for if they did what they did from love to Christ, the same love would make them desire and seek to be yet more conformed to him.
