Sermons and Discourses, 1723–1729
Quæstio: Peccator Non Iustificatur Coram Deo Nisi Per Iustitiam Christi Fide Apprehensam

Introduced by George G. Levesque

Edwards prepared the following oration in partial fulfillment of the M.A. degree requirements for the Yale College Commencement of September 20, 1723. This "public act," three years after his B.A. degree, would consummate his academic training, announce his proficiency in the arts, and signal his readiness to assume a pulpit as a fully qualified minister. It would also be Edwards' first formal theological discourse outside of the pulpit and here represents his academic career.

In applying for the M.A., Edwards was following the expected pattern outlined by the college trustees in 1701 and again in 1718. They required four years of study to become a Bachelor and three more years to become a Master.1 In contrast with the B.A. course, however, the trustees did not prescribe a curriculum for the M.A., and there was no residency requirement. The only official stipulations for the degree were that the candidate was to present "a written synopsis either of logick or naturall phylosophy or metaphysicks as also a Common place on some Divinity theses and ye solution of two or three problems such as ye Rector shall propose to him and be approved by ye trustees att Commencement."2 Following the medieval scholastic tradition still in force at Oxford and Cambridge, the M.A. candidate would deliver his "synopsis" in the form of a public disputation on graduation day.3

-- 048 --

It is likely that Edwards prepared his disputation shortly before Commencement, during the summer of 1723. He had recently completed his nine months of pulpit supply in New York City, and was staying at the family parsonage in East Windsor. Here, Edwards "spent the Summer in close study" and, with the guidance of his father, candidated for a pulpit.4 During these productive months, he added 22 new titles to his "Catalogue of Books" and wrote "Miscellanies" nos. 1 to 65.5

The "Miscellanies" of this period and entries in the "Diary" indicate that Edwards thought about his Commencement discourse frequently over the summer. Thomas A. Schafer points out that "Miscellanies" nos. 2, 27b, 36, 41, 44, in particular, closely parallel themes in the Commencement disputation, and parts of the discourse were likely translated into Latin directly from these entries.6 In the "Diary," we find scattered confessions of anxiety, apparently connected with having something "prepared to public acceptance." Edwards particularly mentions an upcoming "event" with potentially "adverse" circumstances, forcing him to trust "that God will grant success, or make the contrary to my advantage."7 The entry for August 9, 1723, mentions some "important business… now in hand," which biographers as early as Sereno Dwight have believed was in reference to his master's examination.8 If so, the entry suggests that Edwards had completed the disputation by early August, and at that point could only wait for the day of his "public act" in New Haven.

The annual Commencement exercises at both Harvard and Yale were grand affairs in colonial New England, attracting visitors from throughout the region eager to view the latest crop of graduates. At Yale, the proceedings took place in September and were held in the meeting house on the public green, across the street from the college's first building. On hand for the big event every year would be a large assembly, consisting of colonial officials, college trustees, many area ministers, and other "learned and superior Gentlemen." The ceremony was conducted entirely in Latin, and included several eloquent

-- 049 --

speeches by distinguished guests. The main event, however, was the public disputations by the candidates for each degree.9

In the morning, after an opening prayer by the Rector and a salutatory oration, the baccalaureates went first. The disputations were based on Latin propositions, called Theses, representing many common axioms of the Liberal Arts. Each graduating class selected their own Theses list in consultation with the faculty, and printed them in various categories (e.g., Logicae, Grammaticae, Rhetoricae, Metaphysicae) on a broadside for distribution among the audience. Several Theses, indicated by special notation, were "manfully defended" by pre-selected members of the class, although any candidate could be called upon to defend any given thesis. Taken together, the Theses served as a summary to others of what the commencers had studied and learned.

After lunch, the crowds reassembled, this time for the master's disputations, called Quæstiones. Though similar in purpose and style to the Bachelor's Theses, the Quæstiones were more complicated and were assigned in advance, one per candidate, requiring a more formal discourse. The Quæstiones were propositions phrased in such a way that the candidate had to respond either in the negative or in the affirmative, and then defend his position syllogistically. Once the rounds of "disputes" were satisfactorily completed, the Rector presented the candidates to the trustees, saying, "Placente vobis [Are you pleased?]," to which they answered, "Placet, placet [It pleases, it pleases]."1 After the whole affair, one of the graduates would deliver a valedictory oration and the Rector would close the day in prayer.2

While the annual graduation exercises were normally festive holidays, the Yale Commencement of 1723 brought more cause for concern than it did for celebration, for everyone was talking about what had happened a year ago. At the Commencement of 1722, just when it seemed that the college had overcome its turbulent birth pains,3 the trustees were faced with their greatest crisis. The Rector of Yale at the time, Timothy Cutler, along with one of the two tutors and five local

-- 050 --

ministers, had revealed their conversion to Anglicanism, made plain by Cutler's conspicuous conclusion of the Commencement prayer from the Book of Common Prayer—"and let all the people say, Amen."4 The seriousness of this defection may be lost to modern readers, but for Puritan New England, it was tantamount to high treason. It was, of course, the corruption in the Church of England which the Dissenters were seeking to "purify," and the thought that the enemy was in their own nursery of learning sounded a great alarm.5

News of this shocking event spread throughout New England and it soon became known as "The Great Apostacie." The defection was all the more surprising because Cutler, prior to coming to Yale, was widely regarded as a model defender of Puritan convictions. Moreover, of the seven men who announced their conversion, six were Yale College graduates, and five had taught there in significant capacities. The Congregationalist ministers and townspeople were devastated as they considered the implications. Had the very institution they established to promote orthodoxy become a cradle of heresy?6 This could have been expected at Harvard where signs of theological pollution were long evident, but now many feared that Yale was in even greater danger.7

-- 051 --

Such was the atmosphere in September 1723 in New Haven as the young M.A. candidate from East Windsor rode into town. Edwards would have been fully aware of the controversy which had erupted at the previous Commencement, and it no doubt added to the anxiety reflected in the "Diary." More than ever, ministers and local residents were prepared to scrutinize every candidate's statement, carefully listening for any hint of heterodoxy. In no small measure, the reputation of Yale, and with it the hopes and fears of the Connecticut colony, were resting on Edwards and his fellow graduates. And yet, as became the pattern of his life, the young Edwards did not back down from the challenge in front of him.

While Edwards was qualified to "dispute" on a wide range of propositions, including those related to his detailed scientific observations, he boldly selected for his Quæstio a controversial theological topic. In a climate in which free will and self-determination were gaining favor, Edwards defended the Calvinist proposition that a sinner is completely dependent upon God for salvation. Everyone in the audience doubtless recognized this as a frontal assault on the Arminian tendencies of the recent "Apostacie."

Edwards phrased his Quæstio this way, responding in the affirmative: "A sinner is not justified in the sight of God, except through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith." On one level, Edwards was simply defending the Reformation hallmark tenet of justification by faith alone. Yet he used this familiar idea to attack his Arminian opponents, claiming that they were undermining this "assuredly central" doctrine. It was a clever rhetorical strategy, for very few people in New England at this time, including self-professed Arminians, would have wanted to be caught on the wrong side of the Reformation.

Throughout the discourse, Edwards follows the method of scholastic disputation. Rather than relying on Scripture-proofs, he argues exclusively from the "mutual consistency of God's attributes" and the logical laws of non-contradiction. Edwards' point is to demonstrate that his opponents, in suggesting that sinners are justified through "sincere obedience," are setting up a new law and a false system of justification. This "neonomianism," Edwards argues, makes sincere human obedience a "condition of justification," and thereby becomes another form of works-righteousness. The logical implication of the Arminian position, Edwards claims, is that a "sinner is his own redeemer." This, however, is an impossibility, for the offense made against the holiness of God is of "infinite seriousness" and renders all

-- 052 --

human repentance "infinitely inadequate." Such "imperfect justification," Edwards concludes, is "altogether no justification at all, but is assuredly condemnation."

By all accounts, Edwards' discourse was well-received.8 The characteristic themes, methodology, and language utilized in this early discourse would later establish Edwards' reputation and serve him throughout his career. His first published sermon, God Glorified in the Work of Redemption, by the Greatness of Man's Dependence upon Him (Boston, 1731), elaborated many of the themes of the Commencement discourse. It was delivered at the prestigious Boston Thursday lecture in July 1731, where Edwards again confronted his Arminian opponents on the topic of justification, this time at their own doorstep. Edwards' belief in the unity of scriptural and rational arguments, also indicated in the discourse, was supported in his second published sermon, A Divine and Supernatural Light, Immediately Imparted to the Soul by the Spirit of God, Shown to Be Both a Scriptural and Rational Doctrine (Boston, 1734). His emphasis on the "reception" of Christ by the "entire soul" was elaborated subsequently in his notebook on "Faith" and later in Religious Affections (Boston, 1746). Lastly, the entire subject of justification is one which Edwards revisited regularly; it was a series of sermons on that very topic that launched the 1734 revival in Northampton. In these lectures, Edwards developed several of the themes introduced in his Commencement discourse such as the infinite heinousness of sin, the covenants of works and grace, and the need for the imputed righteousness of Christ's active obedience for justification rather than mere forgiveness.

The manuscript is four octavo leaves paper stitched into a stiff brown paper cover. The text of the Quæstio, written neatly and in a large hand for public reading, takes up the first seven pages. The final copy was discovered among manuscripts donated to Jonathan Edwards College at Yale University, and was subsequently placed in the Beinecke Library in 1958. Long labeled his "Master's Thesis," it is more accurately described as his M.A. Quæstio.

-- 053 --

Page eight and the inside back cover of the MS contain notes that may or may not be connected with his Commencement exercises. These notes, which are presented in an appendix, are syllogistic arguments relating to the issue of the power of church councils to make laws "binding on the conscience." Behind this issue is limited conformity in ecclesiastical affairs, specifically, conformity to the Church of England in matters of worship. Edwards was writing "Miscellanies" entries on this and similar topics at the same time he was writing his Quæstio.9 However, the notes at the end of the discourse are very elliptical and hastily written, making it difficult to ascertain their purpose. Possibly they were written during the Commencement examinations in anticipation of a likely question.

Some general principles apply to the presentation of Edwards' Latin text and to its translation. Edwards wrote in a late medieval Latin style. Rather than transcribing the discourse into a classical form, an effort has been made to present the text as originally written by Edwards, including errant capitalization and punctuation, with the exception of a few obvious cases of dittography and slips of the pen. In composing several passages of the Quæstio, Edwards apparently translated his own English from "Miscellanies" entries, using his own cognates; the translation presented here relies in part on those corresponding entries. In addition, a draft of the discourse is in the Trask Library at Andover Newton Theological School (f. 1720–1729A.5). Prof. Schafer pointed out some helpful similarities between the draft and final copy where the reading of the final copy was in doubt.

Quæstio: Peccator Non Iustificatur Coram Deo Nisi Per Iustitiam Christi Fide Apprehensam

Opus maxime quidem insigne minime quamvis Difficile nobis hodie Contigit: reformatæ scilicet Religionis Protestantibus, et Christianæ Christianis veritatem vindicare. Nec tenuem esse Gloriam habemus tueri id quod Profecto est et semper Christianis Primis et universis recentioribus Reformatam Christianitatis puritatem profitentibus. Evangelii summam Gloriam scripturarumque delicias Concorditer Existimatum Est, hanc scilicet Doctrinam, Iustificationis per Iustitiam Christi fide Apprehensam. Et ni forte nihil sub theologicæ theorematis prætextu sit subiectum Præsentis Disputationis, distincte limites idearum his verbis nobis exhibitarum designemus.

Ac primo per Iustificationem intendimus, Divinæ actum favoris Erga Peccatorem, peccata Remittentem et illum qua Iustum Approbantem. Non solum volumus peccatorum remissionem, hæc enim sola, non aliter Iustum conspectu Dei hominem Reddit ac ipsas arbores aut Rupes. Nam lapides sunt eque reatu peccati et iracundia Dei immunes ac quilibet homo ita Iustificatus. Sed Proculdubio Iustificatio est quidam actus positivi favoris et hominis non solum ut peccato immunis, sed quoque Revera Iusti approbatio Per Iustitiam Christi et activam et passivam et obedientiæ et satisfactionis intellegitur. Nam Omnimodo Eadem est ratio quare Iustitia Positiva a nobis Deo nunc Exigatur ut ad vitam Eternam Recipiamur, quæ fuit ut requiratur ab Adamo et quare ille non immutabili felicitate primo Creationis momento sine aliqua Omnino probatione immediate figeretur.

Per fidem hac thesi volumus totius Animæ Receptionem Christi suorumque beneficiorum: non solum Receptionem intellectus in assensu, nec solum Receptionem voluntatis eum eligendo, nec tantum affectuum Receptionem in Amore, nec solum facultatum Executivarum in Obedientia, sed totius Animae horum omnium implicantis, illam animæ totius erga Christum motionem, quae scriptura varie

-- 056 --

adveniendo, Credendo, Confidendo, Recipiendo, Obsequendo &c. Exprimitur.

Proinde quando asseritur hac fide solum peccatorem Iustificari, hoc volumus, nempe quod Deus Peccatorem in Gratiam et amicitiam suam Recipit, propter hoc duntaxat, utpote ita Christum anima sua tota Recipit, Quod Iustitia et vita Eterna prorsus Gratuito offerentur, et solum propter Receptionem præbentur. Nec quidem quærimus annon Iustificamur Evangelica Obedientia, sed An Iustificamur hac Obedientia Evangelica propter eius intrinsicam bonitatem vel solum quippe Per Obedientiam Evangelicam Christus Receptus est. Omnis Enim Pars Evangelicæ obedientiæ est implicita Christi Receptio et actus fidei Iustificantis. Nos igitur Asserimus peccatorem neque in totum neque partim propter talis Obedientiæ aut quorumvis Operum bonitatem, sed solum propter id quod fecit et Passus est Christus fide Receptum Coram Deo Iustificari. Christum esse salvatorem Completum et non partim tantum nostræ salutis Eternæ esse authorem Contendimus. Peccatorem his Respectibus sibimet Redemptorem et mediatorem esse negamus.

Dicitur hanc Doctrinam Opponentibus propter Recipiscentiam & Reformationem peccatorem Iustificari, id est Quod Deus, suis Peccatis iratus et offensus, adspiciens illius Resipiscentiam sinceram eâ satisficatus et Placatus est. Asserimus autem, & stricte Demonstrabile non solum scripturis vi autem quoque nudæ Rationis tenemus, inconsistens esse Dei Attributis ut ille peccato offensus vel totaliter vel Partim satisfiet Resipiscentiâ quantumlibet sincera. Et primum hoc ponimus, quodque Proculdubio Omnibus erit Concessum, quod non attributis Divinis Consistit, nec Obstante immensitate misericordiæ Dei, ut Peccator Iustificatur et in Dei Gratiam Recipiatur sine aliqua Resipiscentia: Primo Enim aspectu Patet. Quod sanctitas et infinita Dei Puritas in Eternum necessario obstabit ut Peccator odiosus, obstinatus et absque minima Resipiscentia Pertinax in amicitiam Dei sit Receptus, hoc nemo negabit. Deinde secundo hoc ponimus, quod et hinc necessario, et eadem Completa Ratione Consequetur: neque Perfectionibus Divini stare propter eam Resipiscentiam Iustificare quæ est tam Exigua ut nullam Prorsus Proportionem Delicto teneat, sed ut Culpæ Revera sit infinite inadequata. Et Proinde sit Penitus instar nullius Omnino Resipiscentia, si Peccato Comparetur. Nam Omnifariam Idem est Peccatorem Iustificare sine ulla Resipiscentia ac peccatorem Iustificare propter Resipiscentiam quæ nullam prorsus

-- 057 --

proportionem Crimini tenet, et ita profecto nulla Resipiscentia est Comparatione Delicto huius igitur non Dubitare Possumus, Divinis nempe attributis Repugnare pro tali Resipiscentia peccatorem Iustificare.

Hoc Autem assumimus, quod maxima et sincerissima Resipiscentia cuius Capax est natura humana, profecto nullam proportionem tenet minimæ iniuriæ Deo factæ, ad maximam cuius certitudinem exhibendam, non opus est ut Disputemus annon iniuria Dei sua natura est infinita, si hoc mittemus, tamen si iniuria Deo habet infinitam pravitatem. Respectu obiecti, aut alio quocumque Respectu, veritas huius Positionis Certissima in Eternam stabit. Si Peccatum tantum unam quodammodo aggravationem infinitam habet, tantidem Refert, eque ac si Omnibus aliis modis malum infinitam esset. Sed iniuria Dei unam aggravationem infinitam habet, nam Certum est iniuria Dei ceteris Paribus esse maioris Pravitatis quam iniuria cuiuscumque entis finitæ. Porro atque Certum est quod iniuria entis finitæ potest esse quantivis Gradûs finitæ pravitatis, in quantum quod deficit pravitate iniuriæ aliis Respectibus suppleri Queat Dignitate Personæ offensæ. Unde certo sequitur iniuriam Dei esse maiorem quam quavis finita pravitate, et Certum est quod ea pravitas quae maior est qualibet possibili finitâ est infinita, ac inde iniuriam Dei esse uno Respectu infinitæ pravitatis, et ita quod maxima Resipiscentia finita nullam omnino ei proportionem tenet, et ut Iam Demonstratum est pugnare Divinis attributis propter Eam peccatorem Iustificare, quod Erat Demonstrandum.

Et idem dici potest De Omnibus bonis operibus, scilicet infinite inadequata esse peccato et proinde attributis Divinis esse Repugnans ut peccator quodammodo ad minimum Iustificari propter sinceram Resipiscentiam aut Obediantiam ergo solum per Iustitiam Christi fide Apprehensam. Deinde si ponitur sinceram obedientiam esse conditionem Iustificationis, necessario supponitur immense maior Dignitas in imperfecta Obedientia sub Evangelio, quam fuerit in perfecta Obedientia Adami. Nam primum fœdus ne amplius bonitatis in Perfecta Obedientia supposuit, quam satis Congruam Conditionem vitae eam Reddere. Sed hoc supponit tantam bonitatem et dignitatem in imperfecta obedientia ut ea non solum sit Congrua Conditio vitæ primo fœdere Promissæ, sed etiam Remissionis Omnium Peccatorum et liberationis ab Eterna miseria iis merita, et collationis Gloriæ multo præstantioris quam ecquando a primo fœdere promissa est. Unum Ergo

-- 058 --

horum duorum necessarium est ut teneamus: aut infinite maius bonitatis et dignitatis esse in imperfecta Obedientia sub Evangelio, quam fuerit in Obedientia perfecta sub fœdere primo aut peccatorem iustificari solum Per Iustitiam Christi fide Apprehensam. Aiunt autem neonomiani Christum mortuum esse ad huiusmodi fœdus nobis Procurandum, ut sinceritas sit Conditio Iustificationis vice Obedientiæ perfectæ, et quod nova dignitas a morte Christi in obedientiam sinceram Confertur ita ut id quod antea non fuit Congrua Conditio Iustificationis nunc Congrua Redditur collatione nova dignitatis per Christi merita. Sed Plane Contradictio est dicere quod quamvis merita Christi nullo modo ipsam naturam Obedientiæ sinceræ mutaverunt, nec in se meliorem fecere, tamen eandem Obedientiam Congruam Iustificationi Reddere, quæ antea ei non Congrua fuit; indubitanter Enim Constat aut esse quoddam Dignitatis vel bonitatis sincera obedientia in se et a Christo abstracte Considerata qua Congrua Iustificationi Redditur, aut nihil in se talis dignitatis habet. Si aliquod in se habet dignitatis tum ea dignitas non Est a meritis Christi, si nihil in se habet tum totum dignitatis est in Christi meritis et nihil in Obedientia sincera, quod est idem cui hoc tempore Contendimus.

Porro atque sibi aliter asserentes peccatorem Obedientia imperfecta Iustificari hoc modo Contradicunt: nam si Iustificamur Obedientia imperfecta, necessario sit ut Iustificatio etiam sit imperfecta. Iustificatio Enim sine dubio est Iustitiæ qua Iustificamur Proportionata. Constat hoc ipsa natura Iustificationis quæ est receptio hominis qua iusti, et certe si a Deo qua Iusti Recepti sumus propter imperfectam Iustitiam, Recipimur qua imperfecte Iusti, sed Receptio qua imperfecte iusti est imperfecta Iustificatio, immo nulla omnino Iustificatio, sed profecto Condemnatio. Nam Recipi qua imperfecte Iustus est Recipi qua indigens legi Dei Conformitate, sed Recipi quâ indigens Conformitate legi Dei est Recipi qua Peccator, quod idem est ac Retineri qua obnoxius legis damnationi quod est Condemnatio. Quamobrem Iustificatio neonomianorum in Condemnationem vertitur.

Ac Praeterea nobis hanc doctrinam exhibet universus sacrarum scripturarum tenor potissimumque, novi testamenti, quo Eandem habemus declaratam omnibus clarissimis et maxime Positivis Dictionibus quæ excogitari possunt id quod asserimus exprimendum.

Quapropter nunc impavide asseramus, quod quam Certa est veritas

A Sinner Is Not Justified in the Sight of God Except Through the Righteousness of Christ Obtained by Faith

The task which concerns us today is of the very highest importance, although of the least difficulty, that is, to defend the truth of the Reformed religion to Protestants and of the Christian religion to Christians. Nor do we consider it to be a slight glory to guard that which is assuredly central, both always for the first Christians and for those more recent who everywhere profess the purity of Reformed Christianity, all of whom agree that the highest glory of the gospel and the delight of the Scriptures is this very doctrine of justification through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith. And lest anything under the pretext of theological inquiry inadvertently be the subject of the present dissertation, let us clearly outline the boundaries of the ideas revealed to us by these words.

And first, by justification we mean an act of divine favor towards the sinner which forgives sins and approves him as righteous. For we desire not merely the forgiveness of sins, since that by itself renders a man righteous in the sight of God no differently than it does the very trees and rocks. Indeed, stones are equally subject to the accusation of sin and may be free from the wrath of God just as anyone who has been justified on these terms. But there can be no doubt that justification is a certain act of positive favor that not only frees a person from sin but is also understood in fact as the approval of him as righteous through the righteousness of Christ, both active and passive in both obedience and satisfaction. For in all respects the reason why positive righteousness is now required from us by God so that we may be received into eternal life is the same reason such righteousness was required of Adam and why he was not immediately secured into immutable happiness at the first moment of creation without any testing at all.

In this thesis we wish to argue that receiving Christ and his benefits takes place by the faith of the entire soul; it is not merely the intellect's reception by assent, not merely the will's reception by choosing him,

-- 061 --

not only the affections' reception in love, nor merely the capacities we have for action receiving him in obedience. Rather, it is a reception of the entire soul, which includes all of these. It is that motion of the entire soul towards Christ that is described in various ways in Scripture as coming, believing, trusting, receiving, submitting, etc.

Accordingly, when it is asserted that a sinner is justified by this faith alone, we mean, of course, that God receives the sinner into his grace and friendship for this reason alone, that his entire soul receives Christ in such a way that righteousness and eternal life are offered in an absolutely gratuitous fashion and are provided only because of his reception of Christ. We are not even asking whether or not we are justified by this evangelical obedience, but whether we are justified by this evangelical obedience because of its intrinsic goodness, or merely because it is only by evangelical obedience that Christ is received. For every part of evangelical obedience is an implicit reception of Christ and an act of justifying faith. We assert, therefore, that a sinner is justified in the sight of God neither totally nor in part because of the goodness of such obedience, or of any works at all, but only on account of what Christ did and suffered, received by faith. We maintain that Christ is the complete Savior and not merely the partial author of our eternal salvation. Because of these considerations we deny that a sinner is his own redeemer and mediator.

Those who oppose this doctrine say that the sinner is justified by repentance and reformation, that is, that God, angered and offended by his sins, is satisfied and appeased by beholding the sinner's sincere repentance. But we assert and hold it to be strictly demonstrable, not only by the Scriptures, but also by the force of bare reason, that it is inconsistent with God's attributes that he, offended by sin, should be either totally or partially satisfied by repentance, however sincere. And first, we assume what everyone will doubtless admit that it is not consistent with the divine attributes, notwithstanding the immensity of God's mercy, that a sinner should be justified and received into God's grace without any repentance, for this is plain at first glance. No one will deny that God's holiness and infinite purity for eternity necessarily prevents a sinner who is hateful, stubborn, and recalcitrant, and without the slightest repentance, from being received into God's friendship. Next, our second assumption, which follows necessarily from this and will command agreement by the same complete argument, is that it is not consistent with the perfections of the divine to justify on account of this repentance, which is so slight that it bears absolutely no

-- 062 --

proportion to the offense but is in reality infinitely inadequate to the fault, and hence is a repentence utterly worthless if it is compared to the sin. For it is in every respect the same to justify a sinner without any repentance as it is to justify a sinner because of a repentance that bears absolutely no proportion to the crime, and so it is actually no repentance in comparison with his offense. Therefore, we cannot doubt that it is surely repugnant to the divine attributes to justify a sinner in return for such repentance.

Moreover, we make the assumption that the greatest and most sincere repentance of which human nature is capable undoubtedly bears no proportion to the slightest injury done to God. Demonstrating the greatest certainty of this does not require us to dispute whether the injury done to God is in its own nature infinite. Even if we set this aside, if the injury done to God has an infinite wickedness with respect to its object or in whatever other respect, the truth of this position will nevertheless remain eternally certain. If a sin has only in some way a single infinite aggravation, it has as much weight as if in all other ways it were an infinite evil. But an injury done to God has a single infinite aggravation, for, all other things being equal, it is certain that an injury done to God is of greater wickedness than an injury done to any finite being whatsoever. Moreover, it is also certain that an injury to a finite being can be of whatever degree of finite wickedness you wish, insofar as what is lacking because of the wickedness of the harm can in other respects still be recompensed unto the dignity of the person offended. It certainly follows from this consideration that an injury done to God is greater than one of any finite wickedness, and therefore that an injury to God is in one respect of infinite wickedness; and accordingly, that the greatest finite repentance bears no proportion at all to it. And it has now been demonstrated that it is contradictory to the divine attributes to justify a sinner because of even the greatest finite repentance. This is what was to be demonstrated.

And the same thing can be said of all good works, namely that they are infinitely inadequate for sin and, accordingly, that it is contradictory of the divine attributes that a sinner should somehow in the least degree be justified because of sincere repentance or obedience. Therefore, justification is only through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith. Now, if it is posited that sincere obedience is the condition of justification, it is necessarily presupposed that there is an immensely greater worthiness in imperfect obedience under the gospel than there was in the perfect obedience of Adam. For the first

-- 063 --

covenant presupposed no more goodness in perfect obedience than was sufficient to fulfill the covenant as a condition congruent with life. But this [new] position presupposes so great a goodness and worthiness in imperfect obedience that it is a condition congruent not only with the life promised in the first covenant, but also with the remission of all sins and deliverance from the eternal woe merited by them, and with the bestowal of a glory much more excellent by far than was ever promised by the first covenant. Therefore, it is necessary that we hold one of these two positions: either there is infinitely more goodness and worthiness in imperfect obedience under the Gospel than there was in the perfect obedience under the first covenant, or that the sinner is justified only through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith.

However, the neonomians say that Christ died in order to procure for us this kind of covenant such that sincerity should be the condition of justification instead of perfect obedience, and a new dignity is conferred upon sincere obedience by the death of Christ in such a way that what previously had not been a congruous condition of justification is now rendered congruous by a new bestowal of dignity through the merits of Christ. But it is plainly a contradiction to say that although the merits of Christ have in no way changed the very nature of sincere obedience nor made it better in itself, nevertheless they have rendered the same obedience congruent to justification which before was not congruent to it. For undoubtedly it is established that either there is something of worthiness or goodness in sincere obedience considered in itself and apart from Christ which renders it congruent to justification, or that it has nothing in itself of such worthiness. If it has something of worthiness in itself, then that worthiness is not from the merits of Christ; if it has nothing in itself, then the whole of its worthiness is in the merits of Christ and none in sincere obedience, which is the same contention we are now making.

Furthermore, those who assert otherwise that a sinner is justified by imperfect obedience, they contradict themselves in this way: for if we are in fact justified by imperfect obedience, it would necessarily still be an imperfect justification. For justification is without doubt proportionate to the righteousness by which we are justified. The very nature of justification, which is the acceptance of a person as righteous, is consistent with this. And certainly, if we are accepted by God as righteous because of an imperfect righteousness, we are accepted as imperfectly righteous, yet acceptance as imperfectly righteous is an imperfect justification, and is indeed altogether no justification at all, but is assuredly

-- 064 --

condemnation. For to be accepted as imperfectly righteous is to be accepted as lacking conformity to God's law. What is more, to be accepted as lacking conformity to God's law is to be accepted as a sinner, and this is the same thing as to be held liable to the adverse judgment of the law, which is condemnation. For this reason, the justification of the neonomians is turned into condemnation.

And, moreover, the entire tenor of the sacred Scriptures, especially of the New Testament in which we have the doctrine made explicit, displays this doctrine to us by all the clearest and most positive expressions that can be devised, stating expressly what we assert.

Therefore, we now fearlessly assert that as the truth of the Reformed religion is certain, as the first foundation of the gospel is certain, as the mutual consistency of God's attributes is certain, as the incapacity of what is false to be strictly and absolutely demonstrated is certain, and as it is certain that both parts of a contradiction cannot be true, so it is certain that {a sinner is not justified in the sight of God except through the righteousness of Christ obtained by faith}.
