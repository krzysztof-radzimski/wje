Preface to the Period

The texts presented in this volume constitute a sample of sermons and discourses by Jonathan Edwards from a little-known period of his life. Later, he would deliver the sermons and write the treatises that would earn him the title of colonial America's consummate preacher and most sophisticated defender of Reformed Christianity.1 But that was later. None of the sermons included in this collection was published in his lifetime, and only one was published after his death. Yet, in one way or another, they were all intellectually, spiritually, and professionally formative for the young Edwards and stand on their own as striking specimens of his craft. These texts reflect the development of his homiletic skills from September 1723, when he delivered his Quæstio as part of his graduate examination at Yale College, to the end of 1729, just a few months after he became sole pastor of Northampton following the death of his famous grandfather, Solomon Stoddard.

The most salient feature of Edwards' life and thought during these years was his prodigious intellectual growth. His connections with Yale College, both as a student and tutor, and his access to the new treasures of the Yale library, supplied a rich soil for his burgeoning ideas. During these years his working notebooks expanded geometrically to include studies on scriptural exposition, natural philosophy, typology, and the nature of faith and conversion. In the process, a larger form began taking shape: a theological system which, while grounded in orthodox Calvinism, was peculiarly his own. We will begin with a brief chronological narrative of Edwards' personal life during the years that took him from New York to Northampton, and then isolate some of the more important themes that will recur in the representative sermons.

-- 004 --

From New York to Northampton

"Last night I came home, after my melancholy parting from New York," wrote a pensive nineteen-year-old Jonathan Edwards in his "Diary" for May 1, 1723. He had just completed an eight-month pastorate with an English Presbyterian church in New York City.2 Those months had been crucial for the young preacher for both his pastoral and his devotional developments. He delivered sermons that were meditations on personal holiness and ecstatic apostrophes to God. The "warmth" of his sermons reflected the small, familial nature of his congregation, which lacked official ecclesial stature and met in private homes. For Edwards, already steeped in New Testament exegesis, the experience may have been analogous to that of the early Christians, who also met privately in small, tightly knit groups. Though brief, Edwards' New York sojourn produced lifelong attachments and memories of spiritual intimacy that became a yardstick by which he would measure the congregations he served thereafter.

Though Edwards sorely missed his friends in New York, his spiritual discoveries remained intense and creative. Throughout the spring and summer of 1723, he later recalled, he "continued much in the same Frame in the general, that I had been in at New-York…; having one special Season of uncommon Sweetness: particularly once at Bolton, in a Journey from Boston, walking out alone in the Fields."3 Nature experienced in solitude would remain a powerful vehicle to the supernatural throughout his life.

The latter half of 1723 also marked a period of dramatic intellectual growth for Edwards. During these months, freed from the weekly need to write sermons, he completed the requirements for his master's degree.4 For the commencement exercises he prepared a response to a Quæstio on the nature of justification.

Beside finishing his degree work, Edwards took up other scholarly projects. After a hiatus during the New York pastorate, he resumed his entries in "Natural Philosophy," "Of the Prejudices of the Imagination," and the "Catalogue of Books." Another important composition

-- 005 --

from this period is the Spider Letter, dated October 23, 1723.5 Autumn also saw the beginning of two new series on the "Mind" and the "Apocalypse." Together, these compositions would forever characterize his originality and imagination.

Edwards was probably already preaching at the small town of Bolton, Connecticut, when he was offered a formal settlement and salary. On November 11, 1723, he signed the Bolton church book and officially began his second pastorate. The town had been incorporated only three years before Edwards' arrival. The meeting house was built in 1721—a humble structure measuring forty by thirty-five feet.6 Though small in numbers, the inhabitants proved as susceptible to social ills as any cosmopolitan center. Though the precise nature of the contention is unknown, Edwards' presence itself may have been a contributing cause, which may in turn account in part for the brevity of his pastorate there.7 In Living Peaceably One With Another, preached at Bolton in late 1723, Edwards beseeches his listeners to "bear with each others' infirmities," "give place to wrath," and be "free and open one to another." After New York, Bolton may have been a disappointment for the young, idealistic pastor.

Edwards arrived at Bolton already supplied with a stock of fully written sermons together with additional sermons written since coming home.8 Between these previously prepared sermons and Edwards'

-- 006 --

continuing work on his private notebooks, we have only seven sermons composed specifically for Bolton. Though few in number, they were seminal in Edwards' evolution as a pastor. The importance of this set of sermons is indicated by the publication of four of them in this volume: A Spiritual Understanding of Divine Things Denied to the Unregenerate, The Pleasantness of Religion, Living Peaceably One With Another, and Nothing Upon Earth Can Represent the Glories of Heaven. In these sermons we see the themes that will occupy him intellectually and pastorally throughout his career. Mirroring exciting new philosophical contemplations in notebooks such as "The Mind," Edwards considers excellency, beauty, and harmony and applies them to pastoral issues. While at Bolton he passed another important landmark as a member of the Standing Order by delivering his first fast-day sermon in April 1724, when he as a preacher became the voice of God to the civic community.9

Edwards remained at Bolton until May 1724, all the while gaining further experience as a pastor. Diary entries from this time reflect his activities both in and out of the pulpit. On November 29, 1723, he writes, "As a help to attention in social prayer, to take special care to make a particular remark, at the beginning of every petition, confession, etc." In his December 12 entry, as an alternative to reading "good books," he lists engaging "in the more private duties of a pastor," which could have included anything from personal prayer and meditation to visitation and counseling.1

New preaching and pastoral challenges were accompanied by new intellectual and literary vistas that were opening up before the young Edwards. His personal writings provide an important backdrop for the sermons from this time, which in their philosophical content and literary style reflect his expansive private aspirations. In his case, spiritual humility did not translate itself into intellectual humility. The Spider Letter, an original observation on "flying" spiders, afforded him an opportunity to correspond as a fellow scientist with a member of the Royal Society, Judge Paul Dudley, and to participate in the trans-Atlantic republic of letters. His notes on "Natural Philosophy" and "The Mind" were also written as a dialogue of sorts with the great

-- 007 --

minds of the English Enlightenment—John Locke, Isaac Newton, and Bishop George Berkeley. Throughout, he sought guiding principles. In his "Diary" for March 24, 1723, he wrote: "I intend, if I am ever settled, to concert measures, and study methods, of doing good in the world, and to draw up rules of acting in this matter, in writing, of all the methods I can possibly devise, by which I can in any respect do good."2

During the early months of 1724, Edwards fulfilled his methodological intentions by drawing up a list of stylistic directions plotting his rise to international literary fame. Aware of his superior powers, yet ever mindful of the importance of a deferential carriage, he cautioned himself to adopt a "modest" style both in writing and in attitude: "The world will expect more modesty because of my circumstances—in America, young, etc. Let there then be a superabundance of modesty, and though perhaps 'twill be needless, it will wonderfully make way for its reception in the world. Mankind are by nature proud and exceeding envious, and ever jealous of such upstarts; and it exceedingly irritates and affronts 'em to see 'em appear in print." With no small degree of presumption he continued, "Before I venture to publish in London, to make some experiment in my own country; to play at small games first, that I may gain experience in writing."3 Clearly, this is the prose of one confident at an early stage, calculating his rise to fame.

Edwards' next position gave him a unique opportunity to pursue his ambitions and to keep abreast of the world of ideas. He was elected a tutor of Yale College on May 21, 1724.4 His appointment came in the wake of a tumultuous episode in the infant college's history, however. Just two years earlier, Rector Timothy Cutler and the tutors had converted to Anglicanism. The "Great Apostasy," as it came to be called, resulted in the resignation of Cutler and his assistants. One of the vacant tutorships was given to the twenty-year-old Edwards because of the "very good name" he had already made for himself as a defender of New England's theological heritage.5

Anglicanism was not only an ecclesiastical threat, it was also a doctrinal one. New England clergy accused their Anglican counterparts of

-- 008 --

propagating Arminianism, a refutation of the Calvinist view of salvation. While a student, Edwards had been well educated in the arguments against Arminianism at Yale College, where he studied Increase Mather's call to "dispute down Arminianism." When the specter of Anglican Arminianism raised its head in Connecticut's nursery of learning, therefore, the scandal rocked the colony. Congregational clergy, led by Cotton Mather, mounted a campaign to safeguard New England Calvinism from the taint of Arminianism.6 With the eyes of New England on the college, the young Edwards stepped into this fray. The "Great Apostasy" personally sensitized him to the alleged dangers of the importation of Arminian thought. He was drawn into an ideological and theological civil war that would occupy nearly his entire life.

Over the next twenty-eight months, until he resigned in September 1726, Edwards dedicated himself to a regimen of teaching, reading, and study. He availed himself of the college library, with its recent acquisitions in the "New Learning," and continued his private writings in "Miscellanies," "Natural Philosophy," "Notes on the Apocalypse," "Notes on Scripture," and "The Mind." In 1725 he wrote "Beauty of the World" and began "Christ's Example" as well as a Table to his rapidly expanding "Miscellanies."

Being in Yale College was a boon for Edwards' insatiable mind, yet the tutorship was detrimental in other ways. The press of his duties meant that he rarely preached, wrote very few sermons, and so could not hone his homiletical skills. Only toward the end of the tutorship, when he was supplying the pulpit of Glastonbury, Connecticut, did he resume preaching; but even then, the sermons were for the most part reworked and cannibalized versions of earlier pieces. For example, when Edwards repreached The Duty of Hearkening to God's Voice, a sermon from the New York period, at New Haven in the spring of 1726, he simply added a final use to the Application regarding the tragic deaths of "two of the members of the college."7

Edwards' tutorship was also marked by a decline in his personal religion. His first "Diary" entry upon taking up his duties at New Haven cites " despondencies, fears, perplexities, multitudes of cares, and distraction of mind." "I have now," he concluded, "abundant reason

-- 009 --

to be convinced, of the troublesome and vexation of the world, and that it never will be another kind of world." Looking back on the tutorship both in his "Diary" and "Personal Narrative," Edwards described the period as one of low personal religious affections and increased "temporal concerns."8

Edwards' demands on himself, always prodigious, furthered his distraction. Again, he felt inadequate to the many responsibilities of his new position, and he compensated by pushing himself to exhaustion, practicing "self-denial," and watching for signs of spiritual rebellion against his lot. He restricted his diet and applied himself rigorously to his studies, all the while plagued by chronic indigestion and headaches. Soon he found himself in a seemingly permanent "listless" frame. "It seems to me," he confided in his "Diary," "that whether I am now converted or not I am so settled in the state I am in, that I shall go on in it all my life."9 Words like these, from one so obsessed with salvation, evidenced real trouble. The many strains of the tutorship, his ascetic measures, intense study, and college turmoil led to a "weakness" or breakdown so complete he was left incapacitated for several months. After commencement in September 1725, he attempted to make his way back to Windsor, but only got as far as North Haven, where he was forced to stop at the home of his uncle, Rev. Isaac Stiles. His condition was serious enough that his mother, Esther, came from Windsor to take care of him.1

By the end of the month Edwards was "mending" and in mid-November he was well enough to be thinking about how best to pursue his studies.2 He was in no condition to resume his regular duties, however; and when his health permitted, he traveled to Windsor, where he stayed until December.

Edwards returned to New Haven to finish out the academic year, toward the end of which he received yet another offer—this time to present himself as a candidate for a "settled" ministry that suited his lofty aspirations. On August 29, 1726, the people of Northampton voted to ask Edwards to assist his aging and ailing grandfather, the Rev. Solomon Stoddard, as a result of "what Experience we have had of him by his preaching & conversation as also from his Character from

-- 010 --

other places."3 Accordingly, in September he resigned his tutorship and accepted the invitation. He arrived in Northampton on October 26, preached as a "probationer" for several months, and was ordained on February 15, 1727. At his ordination ceremony he repeated a sermon from the New York period on seeking God. In the Application, revised especially for the occasion, he addressed his new flock on the "affair which is before us," stressing that the success of "the ministry of the Word" depends on the blessing of God and not on the messenger.4 The words were something of a forecast for the next twenty-three years at Northampton—years that would see its minister caught up in acclaim and conflict.

In contrast to Bolton, Northampton was a town with a long and prominent history as the crown jewel of the Connecticut River Valley. The town was primarily agricultural, though its location on the Connecticut River also made it an important commercial center connected to the larger world of English mercantile trade. By the first decade of the eighteenth century, virtually all of the usable land, much of it marginal, had been parceled out. Meanwhile, the once-fertile bottom land had been depleted from long usage, which resulted in declining productivity. Land ownership was increasingly concentrated in the hands of a minority of very affluent individuals and upward mobility for others was becoming difficult. Individuals were likely to live out their lives in the station in which they were born, leading to inter-generational tensions. In contrast to seventeenth-century New England communities, Northampton town politics were carried on more by conflict and majority rule than by consensus. Disputes between two long-standing factions would continue to plague Edwards' pastorate and eventually lead to his dismissal. Northampton was quickly losing whatever Puritan character it had originally possessed and was instead becoming an arena of competing Yankees.5

Edwards' position was rendered still more difficult by the imposing—and controversial—shadow cast by his grandfather. Solomon Stoddard had come to Northampton in 1669 and rapidly established himself as one of the most powerful and influential figures in western

-- 011 --

Massachusetts, not to mention New England.6 In contrast to Boston churches, the Northampton church under Stoddard's leadership had adopted an "open communion" policy and controversial admissions practices that awarded church membership to all those possessing "historical faith" and of "non-scandalous" behavior. He combined this novel approach with a powerful, traditional message of the New Birth. Under his preaching the town experienced some of the most significant "harvests" or awakenings in New England's history. Using his successes as his most powerful argument, Stoddard sought (with mixed results) a "presbyterial" form of church government. Again, in contradistinction to established Congregational practice, decisions were rendered by ecclesiastical councils within the Hampshire Association and were at least theoretically binding on the particular churches. Stoddard was a man who epitomized the frontier ethic, and he was revered by the people over whom he ruled. His failing health in the latter years of his life, however, meant that the church had been idling and was in need of a young and energetic replacement.

Edwards' duties as his grandfather's assistant included dividing responsibility for the sabbath-day services and taking charge of the weekly lecture. As at Bolton, he could draw on his reserve of sermons to help him through the transition period, composing new sermons only as needed. By the end of the summer of 1728 he would exhaust his supply and increase production dramatically.

With his position secure, Edwards married Sarah Pierpont of New Haven on July 28, 1727. The two had first met when he was an undergraduate. Impressed with her spiritual life, he had composed a meditation on her that praised her "wonderful sweetness, calmness and universal benevolence of mind." In Sarah, who seemed to be in a continual conversation with the "great Being," Jonathan found someone whose fervency matched, and even excelled, his own. The first of their eleven children, Sarah, was born "on a Sabbath day," August 25, 1728.

On February 11, 1729, "Pope" Stoddard died, leaving his grandson in full charge of one of New England's most prestigious parishes. No period in Edwards' life would be more decisive. The sermons composed

-- 012 --

in this period, always thorough and often brilliant, reflect a young man with a mission to prove himself. Almost immediately he asserted his own agenda and guided his congregation in the knowledge of his carefully defined "true" religion.

Though no letters or personal writings by Edwards from the pivotal year of 1729 have survived, enough information exists in Edwards' manuscripts, early biographies, and family letters to ascertain changes in his personal writing habits. Increased preaching responsibilities meant that he now had to produce sermons at nearly double the rate he had had to write them earlier in his ministry. From internal analysis of the sermon manuscripts, it is clear that his writing habits changed quickly and radically. During the New York and Bolton periods, Edwards neatly copied his sermons from drafts into prepared booklets; his handwriting was round and easily legible. But sermons composed at Northampton are written in a hand that is more slanted and hurried-looking. Certain letters, particularly vowels, are no longer written as clearly, while deletions, false starts, and repeated or missing words are more frequent. He no longer drafted sermons; rather, he worked from a rough outline and wrote directly onto duodecimo leaves, correcting as he went. To further budget his time efficiently, he began to abbreviate more: "G." for God, "H. Sp." for Holy Spirit, and so forth. To avoid unnecessary use of paper and time, Edwards began drawing dashes in place of repeated scriptural quotations and phrases. With these improvements, he could work faster and still be concise and compelling. New York, Bolton, and New Haven had prepared him well for rapid composition.

As he was perfecting his sermon composition, he was also working to improve his delivery. Contrary to time-honored descriptions of Edwards as a statue-like, inflectionless speaker, his sermon manuscripts indicate that he varied his delivery, sought eye contact with his listeners, and extemporized on occasion. Having succeeded the master preacher Stoddard, who had condemned the common practice of reading sermons from the pulpit, Edwards experimented with ways to free himself from total reliance on his manuscripts. Increasingly he employed special marks or cues, which allowed him to look up from his notes and then easily locate where he had left off. One such mark was the "pick-up" line, which was merely a curved line drawn to separate key points. He even began to compose portions of his sermons in outline, undeveloped introductory statements, and fragmentary phrases for extemporaneous delivery. Switching from octavo-sized

-- 013 --

booklets to duodecimo leaves meant more frugal use of paper, but the smaller format may also have been Edwards' way of forcing himself to memorize his sermons since they were less easily read.

Edwards' changed handwriting, as well as the sketchy nature that sections of some of his sermons assumed, was not solely the result of increased preaching. He was also struggling with another bout of ill health. In the spring of 1729, he experienced acute emotional and physical fatigue, apparently accompanied by a loss of voice, that manifested all the signs of an anxiety disorder that modern psychologists often equate with creativity. Perhaps as a curative measure, Edwards, his wife, and their infant daughter took a seventeen-day trip to New Haven from late April to early May.7 Despite his time away from the pulpit, Edwards suffered a collapse in early June that rendered him incapable of preaching for about a month. During this time he refrained from writing sermons entirely. Instead, he concentrated on his private philosophical and exegetical notebooks, the "Miscellanies," "Notes on the Scripture," and to a lesser extent "Signs of Godliness," perhaps finding in private study the therapy he needed to recover.

By early July, Edwards had resumed his regular preaching duties.8 His gradual return to health was described in two letters from his father, Timothy Edwards, to his sister Anne Edwards in Killingworth, Connecticut,9 On September 12, a relieved Timothy relayed information on Jonathan:

I have Lately heard Directly from yr Brother, and Recd a Letter from [him], and by Both his Letter and by Mr Benj: Pierpont understand to my Great Comfort that he is very much better in health and bears ye Burden of his work (which hath for some time Lain wholly upon him) so well that he is still mending under it, So yt Mr Pierpont tells me he is both in Countenance and voice rather better yn he was before this weakness came upon him; he also tells me that ye People of Northampton seem to have a great Love and respect

-- 014 --

for him, and that they take Great Content in his Ministry. They Continue their usual Kindness to him, and have built him a Good Large Barn & almost finish'd it, since he hath been Laid aside by his weakness from his work.

On October 6 Timothy Edwards again wrote to Anne Edwards, including the latest news conveyed by two of his East Windsor, Connecticut, parishioners:

Mr Russel and Mrs came hither from N:Hampton Last friday Night, and Give us a very Comfortable account of yr Brother, Who now hath for a Considerable [time] preachd both parts of ye day, and Done the whole Work of ye Ministry, and Mr Russel tells me he seems to him to be quite well, or as well as ever he knew him or to that effect.

The fears and evils besetting the young Edwards had vanished. A recovered Edwards, now in good "voice" and resuming all of his duties, confidently claimed Northampton's pulpit entirely as his own. Further strength came from the good wishes of the congregation now fully under his ministry. His father's reference to the "good large Barn" built for Edwards reveals a congregation eager to please their gifted young pastor. To demonstrate further their acceptance of Edwards, the citizens of Northampton more than doubled his salary; soon he would become one of the best-paid pastors in Massachusetts.1

Now freed to explore large and expansive ideas without fear of failure or rejection, Edwards plunged into his duties, particularly preaching. In addition to changing the way he composed individual sermons, he also changed the way his sermons were connected to each other. During his years as Stoddard's assistant, Edwards could resort to a body of sermons that he had composed during and after his first pastorate in New York City. By the end of the summer of 1728, he had repreached all of these and began working on new compositions. But only after Stoddard's death do Edwards' public statements begin consistently to reflect his most current private observations and social context. As a result, his sermons start to build on and relate to one another in a more explicit fashion.

What emerges from the sermons written after Edwards' illness is

-- 015 --

bolder, programmatic preaching. Taken collectively, the sermons display an internal consistency and a sense of argumentative and doctrinal progression that reflect his practice of evenly distributing imprecatory, exhortatory, consolatory, and christological sermons in cycles. Before 1729, Edwards' sermons had generally, though not exclusively, been an outlet for thoughts and observations episodically set down in his private notebooks. Now, in this period of Edwards' rapid maturing, we see the sermon serving as an outlet for the development of more expansive theological themes. Reinforcing this tendency was Edwards' "Miscellanies" notebooks, once largely independent reflections, but now coinciding with general sermon topics.2

Under the creative pressure of weekly sermon composition, Edwards repeated certain themes or images in a more systematic and correlative manner than previously. For example, images relating to children were particularly appealing to Edwards in the latter months of 1729, possibly because his wife was pregnant with their second child.3 An image that also appears in several of the sermons from the autumn is that of the "river of grace," which for Edwards carried specific typological significance and imparted the never-ending nature of God's mercy.4 For the first time since New York, he composed sermons that were meant to be preached in tandem. Normally, such "paired" sermons formed counterpoints to each other. During the summer or fall of 1729 Edwards wrote sermons on trusting in the creature and trusting in God that were preached back-to-back.5

Doctrines flowed from Scripture texts, and here too we see an important transformation. Sometimes Edwards cited a verse that was to be used as the text of the next sermon or one to come in the near future. In the sermon on Job 31:3 (110) preached in the late summer of 1729, he refers to the Scripture phrase, "With the froward thou wilt show thyself froward." Within two or three weeks, this very phrase appeared as the text for his sermon on Psalms 18:26 (111). Perry Miller has written that Edwards used Scripture merely as an embellishment, an afterthought, for his ideas.6 But this example illustrates how Edwards

-- 016 --

used Scripture to shape his ideas even as they appeared with novel clarity. First, it is clear, from both the sermons themselves and his private notebooks, that at any given time Edwards was focusing on certain scriptural "clusters," or collections of verses, which he felt were related. Second, his choice of proof texts was very deliberate, so that they acted to link his sermons together. In referring to verses that had been alluded to or used as the texts of previous sermons, he hoped to make his listeners relate what they had heard earlier to what they were then hearing. During the sermon, preacher and congregation alike were caught up in an exciting moment of mutual growth and learning.

As this short overview indicates, the two events that roughly form the parameters of the sermons and discourses in this volume—Edwards' departure from New York and his replacement of Stoddard at Northampton—define a crucial period in his life. His migration between various pastoral and academic positions taught him to adapt to different circumstances while affording him the opportunity to increase his knowledge, both scholarly and otherwise. Privately, he ranged farther and farther over the philosophical, theological, and religious landscape. In battling "new-fashioned" schemes of divinity in the pulpit and, in the process, describing his own evolving theological vision, he attained a level of achievement and sophistication remarkable for a person of his age. He also began, quite self-consciously, to establish himself as a new religious voice. His sermons from this period reveal, when taken singly, an inner integrity and, when taken collectively, a cohesion that demonstrates his maturation as a preacher, teacher, and theologian. They also reflect his determination to craft the sermon, his primary means of expression, into as effective a pedagogical and awakening device as was in his considerable powers to achieve.

The following sections explore, in roughly chronological fashion, some of the major themes that Edwards chose to address in his sermons. From 1723 to 1729, the earnest personal nature of Edwards' very earliest sermons gave way to more formalized compositions as he adapted to new academic and pastoral positions. In the process, he accustomed himself to the yearly round of civic and sacramental sermons. Also, he increased the range of his sermons, which became doctrinally and systematically more ambitious. The increasing complexity of Edwards' sermons illustrates the broader intellectual range he was achieving.

Justification by Faith and the Arminian Threat

Edwards' generation witnessed the proliferation and toleration of many religious movements in Anglo-American society. Clergy and laity alike evinced a growing inclination to question the authority of the Bible and the church, to distrust enthusiasm and dogmatism, and to place God at a distance. Deism and other forms of "free thought" disturbed Edwards deeply, right from the start of his career. Most disturbing was the challenge of Anglican Arminianism. Arminianism was named after the sixteenth-century Dutch theologian Jacob Arminius. Originally, it was narrowly understood as a repudiation of John Calvin's supralapsarianism, but by the early eighteenth century the term came to encompass a broad spectrum of theologians, including the majority of the Anglican clergy, who emphasized good works over right doctrine and maintained the free will of humankind to accept or reject the grace of God.

The "Great Apostasy" of Rector Cutler and the tutors in 1722 taught Edwards the importance of battling Arminianism. His earliest critique of Arminian notions of justification appears in the Quæstio, or academic disputation, that Edwards gave as part of his master's examinations. In Edwards' first learned oral disquisition, he boldly aligns himself with his Reformed heritage. Mere "sincere repentance," or repentance apart from Christ, is inadequate; salvation is not produced partly by man and partly by God. "We maintain," Edwards declares, "that Christ is the complete Savior and not merely the partial author of our eternal salvation." Since every sin is an infinite affront to God's justice and honor, even "the greatest and most sincere repentance of which human nature is capable undoubtedly bears no proportion to the slightest wrong done to God." Those who assert that justification is rendered by good works are guilty of being "neonomians," or makers of a new law not found in Scripture.

Edwards' polemic reflected the increasing polarization within the New England clergy. Some ministers adopted Latitudinarian attitudes and others propagated a renewed Calvinism based on Puritan tenets. The tone of the Quæstio is heard repeatedly in Edwards' sermons throughout the following years, during which he develops the themes of free grace, the absolute sovereignty of God, and human inability to effect salvation. A prominent example is None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness, preached while Edwards was still an assistant to Solomon Stoddard at Northampton. In this sermon, Edwards elaborates on the

-- 018 --

idea that salvation through works is inconsistent with the "eternal rule of righteousness" and with God's design of glorifying free grace in Christ. Self-righteousness "is to conceive hopes of some favor of God from a false notion of the relation our own goodness or excellency bears to that favor." It is, Edwards warns, "a fatal disease."

In his sermons for the years to come, Edwards would warn his people to beware of entertaining such dangerous notions as Arminianism taught. He challenged them to examine carefully the bases of their hopes for salvation and to see if they actually were "going hoping to hell." He feared that many of his flock, no matter how earnestly they may have said and thought otherwise, did indeed believe that God was somehow obliged by their works to save them.

Spiritual Light and Sight

The characterization of the saints as endowed with a heightened "sense" or awareness of divine things was to become a hallmark of Edwards' preaching. This sense that resulted in knowledge of divine things was communicated by God in the form of spiritual light, which in fact was the Holy Spirit dwelling in the soul.7 Those who possessed the inner illumination of the Spirit were consequently given a "realizing sense" of their relationship to God and their inability to achieve salvation apart from him. On the other hand, those who lacked spiritual light were tragically, even comically, subject to blindness of their true plight through pride and self-righteousness.

A Spiritual Understanding of Divine Things Denied to the Unregenerate is a seminal exploration of the light that is imparted to the soul in conversion. Edwards is still struggling at this early stage to define adequately what the light is, first describing it as "a certain clear apprehension and a lively infixed sensibleness" of divine things and then resorting to analogy. One thing that Edwards is certain about is that it is identifiable by its nature and effects, which consist of a "sight" of divine truths, the knowledge of their excellency, and evidence of the Holy Spirit in the process of this spiritual rebirth. This last category is particularly important for Edwards. Just as human beings alone know what love, grief, anger, and other emotions are because they are unique to "the spirit of man," so only those with a knowledge of divine things can truly know what faith, humiliation, love, peace, and the other attributes

-- 019 --

of the Spirit are. "There is," Edwards writes, drawing on John Locke,

a direct knowledge, and there is a reflex knowledge. The direct knowledge is the knowledge the Christian hath of divine things, without himself, of the truth and excellency of the things of the gospel. The reflex knowledge is that which he obtains by reflecting and looking inward upon his own heart, and seeing the operations and actings of that, and the workings of the Spirit of God therein. By this reflection, the Christian obtains to know what regeneration is; and what are those actings of the Spirit of God which are so frequently spoken of in Scripture; and the whole applicatory part of religion, which is one half of divinity, and which every natural man is ignorant of (p. 80).8

In the reflective act lies "experimental knowledge," because in that act the believer finds a sympathy or likeness between what is in his soul and what he finds in God's Word.

From 1723 to 1729, Edwards regularly incorporated into his sermons his perception of the "divine light" to which true saints were privileged. An instance of this can be seen in the sermon on 2 Peter 1:16 (134), where Edwards attempts to explain this new inner sense by distinguishing a merely "notional" or rational knowledge of God's perfections from an "intuitive" one. The latter, as he describes it, consists of a "clear and lively idea" in the soul.

A sermon from late in the colleagueship at Northampton expands upon themes introduced in A Spiritual Understanding. In Profitable Hearers of the Word, Edwards is intent on describing the qualitatively different understanding of divine things that the regenerate have over the unregenerate and how they act on it. This understanding consists of a "sensible apprehension" of the things of God and in believing the truth of God's Word because of "the intrinsic signatures of divinity which they see in it." Furthermore, this understanding, based on the revealed Word, is the "root" of "fruitfulness." Edwards here makes the essential connection between the internal workings of grace and the outward manifestations of that state. Fruits, or good works, must not only be practiced, but they must be "universal" and "persevering." Though the emphasis on practice is not as strong as it would become,

-- 020 --

the balance between profession and behavior is already normative in Edwards' view of conversion.

Excellency at Bolton

The sermons written in late 1723 relate, in whole or in part, to Edwards' definition of "Excellency" in "The Mind" no. 1. Edwards' original contribution to the contemporary philosophical debate, which featured most prominently the third Earl of Shaftesbury, was to argue that excellency consisted in "similarness" or a consensual relation.9 So important was this concept for Edwards, Wallace E. Anderson has observed, that it was the foundation of "the whole of his subsequent philosophical thought."1 Edwards' private struggle to extend this philosophical category found public expression in four sermons at Bolton. Here the notion of excellency was applied to different issues, including spiritual wisdom, personal holiness, harmony, and the purpose of creation.

In addition to its importance as an early statement on divine light, A Spiritual Understanding of Divine Things Denied to the Unregenerate explores what Edwards considers to be the nature of true wisdom. He contrasts the "notional" knowledge of "natural men" to the "knowledge of the things of the Spirit of God" possessed by the regenerate. The latter's knowledge consists in knowing the excellency of divine things. While the natural man may hear and say that such things are excellent, and may be able to hold forth about God's glory, "it is the savingly enlightened Christian alone that knows and that sees they are excellent." From this true wisdom, imparted by God, also follows a knowledge of "deformities" or dissimilarities. Those without a sense of God's excellency do not follow his ways, and so are not spiritually conformed to his commands and attributes.2

When Edwards finished A Spiritual Understanding of Divine Things, he was, Thomas A. Schafer states, "ready to explore the excellency to

-- 021 --

which the will consents."3 One place he explored the nuances of excellency was in The Pleasantness of Religion. At least at this early stage, Edwards was willing to identify "pleasantness" or pleasure with excellency; real good and real beauty, he argued, were the same thing.4 In this sermon, religion, pleasure, and excellency are synonomous. Excellency also consists in harmony and consent, or likeness between beings. Edwards phrased it another way in "The Mind" no. 1: excellency is the "consent of being to being." In The Pleasantness of Religion that concept is applied to the individual. "Religion sets all to rights in the soul," Edwards states, "so that there is no opposition between one faculty and another." This new order is the result of God's excellency and Christ's likeness communicated to the soul.

Edwards further applies the notion of consent in Living Peaceably One With Another, which describes how Christians are to behave toward fellow believers as well as nonbelievers. "The gospel spirit is a catholic spirit," he observes, "a noble and unconfined benevolence, like unto that of our Creator, not confined to any particular part of mankind exclusive of others; but the Christian's good will is general to all the seed of Adam." The text of the sermon, Romans 12:18, is a summary of "those virtues and graces, amiable actions and heavenly dispositions, which more especially adorn the Christian, and make 'em shine brighter than other men." Christians therefore, as the modern saying goes, must "Love the sinner, but hate the sin." To put it in more Edwardsean language, they must be motivated by pity for transgressors and contempt for their transgressions. "We cannot show friendship more than by hating their worst enemies," their sins, Edwards teaches. Swept up in the fervor of his new concept of excellency, Edwards goes on to make several idealistic statements. For example, even if a person differs from believers in "the substantials and fundamentals of religion… yet we ought, as much as in us lies, [to] endeavor to live peaceably with him." The injunction to live peaceably, as it is an extension of excellency and consent, is absolute: "As there is no sort of men but what we are to endeavor to live peaceably [with], so neither is there any case wherein we are not obliged to endeavor peace with men."

In his essay on "Being," Edwards argued that creation is meaningless without rational creatures to behold it. Nothing Upon Earth Can Represent the Glories of Heaven is an elaboration of this idea.

-- 022 --

The creation of the world is nothing but God's manifestation of his own perfection and excellency. 'Tis certain that God did not thus exert his excellencies for nothing, nor did he give man a capacity of perceiving these manifestations—and the glories and excellencies of God in them—for nothing; but it would be utterly in vain if there were none to behold and admire them. Wherefore it follows that man, or rational beings in general, was created to behold the manifestations of God's excellency, and to view them, and to be delighted with the sight of them (p. 147).

But Edwards also connects his conviction about rational perceiving being to the end for which God created the world and the afterlife. Whereas Arminians and Enlightenment thinkers separated human happiness from glorifying God, Edwards declared that the happiness of man and the glory of God are one. Since humankind was created in God's image, it must be designed for great things, above all to be "inconceivably happy." Therefore humankind can find full happiness only in serving and praising God. Heaven is the place of that ultimate happiness; there God will be perceived and enjoyed to the fullest.5

The Reasonableness of Christianity

The concepts of spiritual light and excellency were part of a larger intellectual endeavor, outlined by Edwards early in his career, to write a treatise showing that the teachings of Christianity were in full accord with human reason. In his "Catalogue of Books," he originally identified the title of this project as "A Rational Account of Christianity, or the Perfect Harmony Between the Doctrines of the Christian Religion and Human Reason Manifested."6 As time went on, his interest in the topic revealed itself in notebook entries (particularly in the "Miscellanies") dealing with "Christian Religion." But during this period it is in his sermons that Edwards most energetically explores the reasonableness of Christianity.

To eighteenth-century thinkers caught up in the Enlightenment, reason was the measure to which everything, including revelation, was to be subjected. For Edwards, following Augustine and Calvin, reason was, along with revelation, one of the pillars of religious truth.

-- 023 --

Though reason could not operate alone because it was flawed and finite, it did provide an indispensable homiletic tool for persuading the unregenerate from their unbelief and confirming the regenerate in their belief. Edwards' very first sermon, Christian Happiness, preached in 1720 when he was just out of college, begins with the assumption that humans are "reasonable beings" whose capacities are best answered by the teachings of Christianity. "Reasonable beings, while they act as such, naturally choose those things which they are convinced are best for them, and will certainly do those things which they know they had better do than leave undone. God always deals with men as reasonable creatures, and every word in the Scriptures speaks to us as such."7 Revealed religion was eminently reasonable and fitted to meet the needs of conscious being. These tenets, owned so early in his life, Edwards was to espouse throughout his career.

A lengthy sermon from 1727, All God's Methods Are Most Reasonable, goes a step farther by seeking to show that God has reason that human reason cannot know. While there are mysteries in religion that cannot and should not be fully explained (such as the Trinity), even seemingly inexplicable things, including the perpetually thorny "divine decrees," are ultimately reasonable. It is only the weakness of the human mind that makes God's disposition of things seem irrational. Thus God allows sin, saves some and damns others, makes promises to some and not others, and allows the wicked to prosper and the godly to suffer in this world. Yet there is a higher rationale. "In all these things, God is most reasonable; that is, there is nothing in them but what is agreeable to perfect justice and the highest wisdom, and nothing contrary to infinite goodness." In the larger scheme of God's operations, everything makes sense. If we could view God's motives and plans in their totality, we could see the final, divine reason behind what Scripture and providence tell us. Edwards cautions, "It shows but very little modesty, that men should dare to determine, because God's ways are out of their sight, that they therefore are crooked. It shows but a very little sense of their own shortsightedness, and of the greatness and glory of God." Divine reason resolves itself, as does everything, into the sovereignty of God.

Edwards also attempts to justify God's ways and their reasonableness in The Day of Judgment. A final day of judgment may not be self-evident by "the light of nature," but within the realm of God's reason it

-- 024 --

is entirely understandable and even necessary. Only a final "public" judgment will allow God to fulfill the divine attributes—honor, omnipotence, righteousness, and power. Simply put, on that day God will act as God. God then will fully and visibly exercise divine authority, vindicate offended holiness, and fulfill the promises made in Scripture.

True Nobleness of Mind shifts the perspective from God to the individual human being's reception of Christianity. Sinners should be ready to "entertain" true religion openly and without prejudice, for only in religion can they hope to find happiness. Human nature is intrinsically rational and so in need of acquiring knowledge or wisdom. "It is requisite in order to a noble mind, that a man should be rational, a person of a good understanding." But human nature realizes the full potential of its own rationality, and so of filling its need for knowledge, only in accepting the gospel. Thus in True Nobleness and in other sermons, such as Warnings of Future Punishment and Profitable Hearers of the Word, Edwards stresses the disposition with which persons should receive the gospel, that is, openly and without prejudice. Epitomizing the spirit of the age, Edwards declares, "The doctrines of Christianity are in themselves most rational, exceeding congruous to man's natural reason." When persons come to "embrace" the gospel, Edwards teaches, they are delivered from "slavery to their passions."

Medieval faculty psychology, which posited a hierarchy of human faculties, still largely prevailed in the eighteenth century. The highest and most noble faculty was reason or understanding, while the lowest was the will or emotion.8 To be ruled by passion was to be a "brute creature"; the proper order within the human psyche was thereby overturned. As Edwards describes it, "Our passions were given us to be our servants, and not our masters. Carnal men are so mean-spirited as voluntarily to become servants to what were given to be their servants." Only the Christian correctly "asserts his own liberty and the dominion that is his right."

The Limits of Language

Invoking the reasonableness of Christianity put Edwards squarely within the philosophical and theological trends that dominated the

-- 025 --

late seventeenth and early eighteenth century, yet he was quick to acknowledge that appealing to intellect alone was inadequate. His attempts to add new affective rhetorical dimensions to his preaching were the result of his increasing awareness of the inability of current homiletical strategies to reach listeners. Indeed, invoking the virtues of human reason lent credence to a moralistic or works-based brand of preaching. Warnings of Future Punishment, an early Northampton sermon, contains hints of his reservations. Here Edwards warns that those fortunate enough to grow up in "a land of light" often grow indifferent to divine truths; after years of religious training and listening to sermons, hearing the same lessons over and over, they are incapable of attaching personal relevance to what they are taught. Repetition makes the extraordinary commonplace. Even more, people merely parrot what their parents and peers say they believe. "'Tis what they have been bred up in and have been taught from their infancy, and their education and example makes 'em say as the rest do." Thus their profession arises not from personal conviction but as a sort of ritual that confirms one's place in the community. "Their belief is not a belief upon any evidence shown to their minds, but only a yielding in some measure to education and example." The idea of future punishment and God's wrath has never been brought into "clear view" so as to be "real" to both their understanding and judgment. They will assent in a complacent way (most of the time) to what they are taught, but they lack the "sensible apprehension" that brings true conviction.

The failure of sinners to realize such things was for Edwards an indictment of the hearers' complacency and of the medium of the spoken Word itself. In The Torments of Hell Are Exceeding Great he makes the important observation that real, present, and "sensible" dangers—of being murdered, of falling into the hands of enemies, of being robbed—will motivate men to action. But warnings about the reality of God's wrath usually fall on deaf ears because the words carry no "sense" with them, "It is only about the danger of hell," Edwards laments, "that infinitely exceeds all, that men are like stocks and stones unmovable, that are talked to and preached to, to make them bestir themselves, in vain." His reservations about the limits of language are most dramatically evident in Living Unconverted Under an Eminent Means of Grace, at once a tribute to his recently deceased grandfather and a statement of the challenge that Edwards, his successor, faces. Edwards points out that "they that have been used to powerful preaching, and are used to hear and stand it out, they grow sermon proof." If

-- 026 --

he was to overcome his congregation's over-familiarity with the Word preached, he recognized that he would need a new way of conceptualizing and employing language.

Although the sermons from this period do not contain Edwards' fully developed view of language—what Perry Miller labeled the "rhetoric of sensation"9—they do show that he was applying himself to the task. Two sermons, Nothing Upon Earth Can Represent the Glories of Heaven and The Torments of Hell, address the inappropriateness of language—even the language of Scripture—to convey a true picture of the opposite states of heaven and hell. Despite the flaws of human language and limitations of nature, God "condescends" to speak to us in the Bible in ways that we can understand. For this reason, Edwards concentrates heavily on Scripture similitude and metaphor in trying to overcome the limits of language.

In Nothing Upon Earth Can Represent the Glories of Heaven, he plainly elucidates his theory of Scripture similitude. God recognizes that "we are most apt to [be] affected by those things which we have seen with our eyes, and heard with our ears, and had experience of. Therefore God has taken his similitudes, by which he would shadow forth heaven to us, from those things which, although they are but faint shadows, have yet an analogy and, in those things wherein they are compared, a likeness; and the thing resembled differs no otherwise from the similitudes, in no more degrees, than as they are more excellent and glorious" (p. 140). Edwards therefore draws Scripture representation into his philosophical ruminations about existence and excellency as well as into his expanded view of typology in which creation is the "shadows of beings." In making such connections, he hoped to impress upon his listeners the unity of sacred texts and religious experience and thereby bring them to a new awareness of the things they had read and heard all their lives.

The Torments of Hell begins with a series of arguments "to prove that the misery of hell is indeed extremely great." The fourth argument considers the metaphors that Scripture uses to describe hell. Edwards distinguishes two ways in which metaphors can be interpreted. If they are used to describe temporal things, they are hyperboles and "express more than is intended because they allude to some spiritual thing that temporal thing was the image of." However, the case is much different if they relate to spiritual things. Then "they fall short of the literal

-- 027 --

truth: for these things are the ultimum, the very highest things that are aimed at by all metaphors and similitudes. And the Scripture uses similitudes about these things for quite another reason, and that is because there is a want of words to express them any other way."

Other sermons from the period amply illustrate Edwards' concern with the multivalence of words in Scripture and their usefulness in bringing his listeners to serious consideration of their eminent danger.1 In Warnings of Future Punishment, Edwards discusses the manner of punishment that sinners will endure. "'Tis a strange punishment that is appointed to the workers of iniquity: it will be a torment inflicted after a new manner, in a way that they never experienced anything like it while they were here in this world." Not one to waste so rich a passage, Edwards wrote an entire sermon on the topic in 1729 drawn from Job 31:3 (110). In the first point under the Doctrine, he dwells on the meaning of the word "strange," which he translates as "monstrous." Human beings, he states, naturally desire and seek happiness. Hell is a "strange" or "monstrous" place because there will be no enjoyment whatsoever, though human craving for happiness will not be at all abated. Extending his definition of spiritual metaphor, he argues that the ignorance of hell-bound sinners is the result of their not being sensible of the nature of God's wrath. The torments and judgments that people inflict on each other in this world cannot prepare the damned for their fate, for the punishments of hell are "incomparably more extreme."

Terror Preaching

Edwards set about to find several solutions to the problems he encountered with language. Reason was an indispensable persuasive and polemical tool, but it alone could not bring a sinner to a saving knowledge of the truth. A rational acceptance of God's sovereignty had to be accompanied by an affective experience of personal unworthiness. Here is where fear entered in as an important weapon in the preacher's campaign to vanquish unrepentant sinners. Though Edwards had tried his hand at what has come to be called "hellfire" preaching in his early sermons, only after his arrival in Northampton do we see serious attempts to perfect this brand of preaching. Previously, Edwards tended to dwell on the pleasantness of religion and the

-- 028 --

beauty of its divine object and human adherents. Wilson H. Kimnach has written that the sermons Edwards delivered at New York "are intended more to lure people into heaven than to frighten them out of hell, for the hortatory dimension is overshadowed by the poetically evocative in most of them, and the auditor is less the subject of instruction or the object of attack than he is witness to an expression of faith."2 Only infrequently do we see effective use of hellfire. After 1726, however, his sermons have a much more prevalent imprecatory tone. This new tone can be attributed directly to the influence of Solomon Stoddard, whose writings and personal example encouraged Edwards to complement the rhetoric of delight with the rhetoric of terror.

In the Puritan preparationist model of conversion, fear was an important emotion for awakening the conscience of the slumbering sinner. But, as Schafer has written, "Stoddard is outstanding in the place that he gives to the dread of damnation as a motive." In his sermon on preaching, The Defects of Preachers Reproved (1724), Stoddard wrote, "When men don't preach much about the danger of Damnation, there is want of good preaching."3 Edwards knew that if he was to succeed at Northampton, he would have to heed the master's advice.

Under Stoddard's guidance, Edwards learned to develop his use of terror preaching. Worthy of particular attention in this regard is The Torments of Hell Are Exceeding Great, one of the "hottest" among Edwards' sermons before Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God. Not only is this an irrepressible denunciation of any who would deny the reality and eternity of hell's torments; it is also an unrelenting psychological attack on sinners' consciences. In a series of eight arguments meant to prove the misery of hell's torments, Edwards asserts that the punishments of hell are "the unmixed wrath of the great God," that the damnation of ungodly men is appointed "to show the greatness of God's power and jealousy," and that their "misery will be the perfect destruction of the creature." "Rebellion against God's authority and contempt of his majesty, which every sin contains, is an infinite evil," and therefore deserves infinite punishment. To back up his points, Edwards copiously cites scriptural texts and images designed to have maximum impact on an audience well-versed in the cadences of the

-- 029 --

Bible. Drawing on a genre of popular literature, he rehearses the exquisite sufferings of Christian martyrs, concluding that they are tame in comparison to what the damned will suffer in hell. The Application goes on to condemn sinners' ignorance and self-delusion, cataloguing particular sins and ending with the ominous warning that the Old Testament sanctions stoning for young people who profane the sabbath.

While Edwards preached the necessity of eternal punishment primarily as a means of bringing secure sinners into a state of conviction, he was also addressing a dogmatic concern. One consequence of the Age of Reason was to render distasteful the contemplation of the torments of the damned. Many religious leaders recognized that such a doctrine served to restrain the "vulgar," but for coffeehouse wits such as Jonathan Swift it only illuminated embarrassing contradictions in Christianity. Prominent theologians, including John Tillotson and William Whiston in England, Pierre Bayle and Jean LeClerc in France, and Gottfried Leibniz in Germany, supported universal salvation, while Locke espoused annihilationism, that is, that the souls of the wicked would not be resurrected.4 Like Augustine, who had little use for "tender-hearted Christians" who could not stomach eternal punishment,5 Edwards saw both universal salvation and annihilationism as irresponsible assertions made by shallow "Freethinkers." Denying the need for punishment was equivalent to denying that Christ need have been incarnated and sacrificed, thereby throwing into disarray the entire scheme of redemption. For this reason, Edwards refers often to God's "offended" majesty, honor, and authority.6 In an age when the doctrines of original sin and eternal damnation were falling into increasing disfavor, Edwards always brought his listeners back to the fundamental truth of a depraved human race, justifiably subject to divine wrath and unable to find salvation except through complete trust in a sovereign, grace-bestowing God.

Not all sermons could sustain the terrifying pitch of The Torments of Hell, nor did it suit the preacher's purposes to attempt to do so. Other sermons sought to achieve a number of goals beside awakening slumbering sinners. The Day of Judgment is one such complex sermon. It is a

-- 030 --

magisterial overview of the final accounting that seeks to justify God's position as supreme judge. At one point Edwards breaks away from his rationalist exposition to give a description of the day of judgment that is by far the most impassioned part of the sermon. What is significant about this description is its narrative form as opposed to the usual numbered arguments. Also, it is located in the fourth head under the Doctrine, and not in the Application, where the most intense awakening rhetoric was usually employed. The sermon exemplifies Edwards' versatility and his willingness to interrupt the traditional sermonic form with unexpected ejaculations.

Living Unconverted is both an example of how well Edwards learned the craft and a tribute to the recently deceased Stoddard, whose memory Edwards exploits as a means of demanding attention. Skillfully, Edwards carries through the sermon the image of the forge, in which Stoddard, Hephaestus-like, is the founder who fanned the coals of God's Word to a red-hot heat. But now the bellows are "burnt out," the coals are cold; for all of his good work, Stoddard left behind much "reprobate silver" and "dross." With Stoddard's passing, those who were not converted under his preaching potentially face a terrible fate. Perhaps God has taken him away because there is no more pure gold to be extracted from Northampton; perhaps what is left will be thrown into the slag heap of hell. Edwards uses this lesson to drive his listeners into action, proclaiming, in an unusually pointed statement, "Woe to them that go to hell out of Northampton and that lived under Mr. Stoddard's ministry!"

Typology

Typology, the ancient discipline of finding Old Testament types or antecedents and New Testament antitypes or fulfillments, offered another way of infusing old words and concepts with new meaning.7 But Edwards was not content with limiting typological correspondence to Scripture; he also argued that creation itself, though fallen, contains "shadows" or veiled clues to divine truths. Edwards distinguished himself by looking into and beyond nature for a moral and cosmic order, an order that emanated from the very being of God. Just as there were laws on which nature depended, so also were there spiritual laws on

-- 031 --

which salvation rested. These laws of nature and grace did not contradict each other, but flowed together in one grand purpose established by God.

As with the perception of excellency, the ability to interpret these divine truths in the world was limited to those with spiritual sight. The problem, from a pastoral as well as a philosophical perspective, was how to open the eyes of the unregenerate to the myriad guideposts to the divine that surrounded them. Fortunately, typology provided a means at least to approach, if not describe fully, the "shadows of beings" found in Scripture, nature, and human experience.

In marshaling arguments for the misery of the damned in Torments of Hell, Edwards drew upon scriptural images and types in the sermon's seventh argument, further elaborating the difference between temporal and spiritual meaning established previously. Earthly death is chilling enough, yet it is only a faint and pale resemblance of the eternal death that the inhabitants of hell must suffer. "If the very shadow be so awful and terrible," Edwards posits, "how dreadful is the substance. As the types of the Old Testament fall infinitely below the substance, the sacrifices below the sacrifice of Christ, the tabernacle and temple below heaven, so does temporal death below eternal."

To prove his point, Edwards in fact explores the typological precursors of The Sacrifice of Christ. In this sermon, Edwards wishes to show what an efficacious sacrifice is. To do this, he must distinguish the Old Testament ceremonial sacrifices, which he interprets as portents of Christ's death, from the death of Christ itself. "The sacrifices that are of God's appointment are either Christ, the true sacrifice, or the typical sacrifices under the Old Testament." As types, the Old Testament sacrifices had no atoning power in them; rather, their significance lay in their representation of the belief that God would someday send the true sacrifice. "A sacrifice is something offered to God, which by its suffering and death makes atonement and procures acceptance for them that it is offered for. The sacrifices under the law did not truly do this, but they did it typically. And therefore Christ, according to the language of Scripture, may be called the true sacrifice."

Edwards did not confine himself to looking for typological meaning merely in Scripture. In notebooks such as "Images of Divine Things" he recorded his keen observations of things in the natural world that struck him as containing some hint of divinity. These observations easily found their way into sermons. God's All-Sufficiency for the Supply of Our Wants is a paean to the wonderful order of creation and God's

-- 032 --

wisdom therein. Edwards concludes that the glory of God "appears both in the works of nature and the works of grace, and the book of nature and the book of grace [and] of revelation both teach us this lesson. We should therefore read in both of them." The lessons and language of nature had the potential to contribute to the education and edification of the saints nearly as much as Scripture.

Earthquake

One event that occurred early in Edwards' pastorate at Northampton was particularly resonant with typological meaning. On the evening of Sunday, October 29, 1727, the inhabitants of New England were shaken awake by a violent earthquake. Through the night and into the early morning hours, aftershocks kept the populace awake and terrified as the tremors demolished stone walls, chimneys, and houses. "The motion of the earth was very great, like the waves of the sea," recalled Rev. Thomas Paine of Weymouth, Massachusetts.8

On the day after the quakes, churches throughout the province observed a day of humiliation and fasting, and people thronged to the meeting-houses. Pulpits rang with the tones of the jeremiad, the time-tested sermonic formula that enumerated and lamented New England's transgressions and demanded repentance. More than two dozen of these sermons were printed, so insatiable was the popular need for interpretations of the event.

Gerald R. McDermott has written that Edwards emerges in his unpublished sermons as a "prophet… of the traditional New England jeremiah."9 One sermon from this period particularly bears out this estimation. Impending Judgments Averted Only by Reformation operates not on a personal but on a corporate level. The sermon was preached on December 21, the day set aside by the governor for a colony-wide fast in response to the earthquake. Such an occasion prompted a consideration of God's "controversy" with New England. Edwards' emphasis here is God's dispensation toward "a sinful people." In classic federal theological language, he lays out the meaning of a national

-- 033 --

covenant. Whereas the judgment of individuals waits until the end of the world, nations are judged by their actions on earth. "There is God's general threatening, that if a people grow corrupt, God will bring judgments upon them…. God is more strict in punishing of a wicked people in this world than a wicked person," Edwards states. "A people as a people are punished only in this world. Therefore God will not suffer a people that grow very corrupt and refuse to be reclaimed to go unpunished in this world, though sometimes he waits long to be gracious."

The providentialist-minded people of New England believed that God oftentimes arranged natural events to serve as warnings to a straying people. Following the earthquake, Benjamin Colman, pastor of the Brattle Street Church in Boston, hurriedly wrote to his daughter: "It remains a loud call to ye whole land to repent, fear, and give glory to God. God sanctify ye rod wch he has shook over us for our humiliation and reformation."1 Colman's interpretation of the event was typical. Such phenomena were viewed as "illustrious providences," signs from God that he was angry with the people of New England. As the creator and sustainer of the universe, God could use natural events to express his displeasure. It was up to the subjects to take heed of the warning and turn from their sinful ways before God visited them with worse calamities.

Edwards' fast sermon adopts this line as well. "God sometimes threatens and warns a people by extraordinary things in providence," he declares, "sometimes by strange sights in the heavens, and sometimes by earthquakes, as it was before the destruction of Jerusalem, and were sent for signs of it." Earthquakes may well have natural causes, "yet they may nevertheless be ordered to fall out so as to be forerunners of great changes and threatenings of judgments—earthquakes more especially, beause they very often are means of great calamities."

If the earthquakes were an occasion to lament, they were also an opportunity for revival. Ministers seized on the earthquakes to impress upon their listeners the importance of immediate conversion. As a result, impressive "harvests" were reported in the months immediately following the earthquake. In a typical published account, John

-- 034 --

Brown of Haverhill reported that "since the Earthquakes, I have admitted and propounded 154 Persons; 87 for the Lords Table, the rest for Baptism, or the Renewing the Baptismal Covenant. The number of Men and Women pretty near equal; mostly younger people, from 15 to 30."2

Ministers all over New England used the earthquake as an occasion to warn their people of impending doom. Edwards was no different. The result was a relatively minor awakening that nevertheless provided him with his first opportunity to oversee such a phenomenon. He later wrote in A Faithful Narrative, "Mr. Stoddard indeed had the comfort before he died of seeing no small moving among some, and a considerable ingathering of souls, even after I was settled with him in the ministry, which was about two years before his death…. There were near twenty that Mr. Stoddard hoped were then savingly converted, but there was nothing of any general awakening."3

Edwards drew not only on the ancient reading of illustrious providences but also on a revivalistic theme heard from New England's pulpits since the Reforming Synod of 1679: the need for "reformation."4 "God has for a long time borne our manners and has been waiting to be gracious to us," Edwards points out to his congregation in Impending Judgments. "This great many years that has been the talk, what need there was of reformation, and to find out some method for the reforming the land, and what danger there is of judgments if the country did not reform; but yet there has been no general reformation." He concludes the sermon by appealing to the youth to abandon their sinful practices—another theme sounded in New England's pulpits at least since the beginning of the century.

When reading Edwards' performances on such occasions, we are tempted to pick out what he did differently, what set him apart. But in his early sermons for civic occasions we see Edwards trying to master a sermon form, trying to conform to an inherited homiletic pattern. These sermons are measures of Edwards' efforts to compose and preach the full gamut of sermon types in an effective and acceptable manner. To hear him preach standard doctrines and draw standard lessons, while possibly disappointing for modern readers, would have been what his listeners expected on such occasions.

Occasional Sermons

Of the more than seventy sermons published during Edwards' life or posthumously, not one was preached for a civic or political event.5 The lack of such sermons in the accepted Edwards canon has led some scholars to conclude prematurely that he was not interested in political affairs.6 Edwards' sermon on the earthquake, however, was preached for an officially declared day of fasting, and so is the first of Edwards' occasional sermons to be published.

Impending Judgments is an example of but one sort of occasional sermon, which can be grouped with sermons delivered on election, fast, and thanksgiving days. These types of sermons are all amply represented in Edwards' sermon corpus, not only for this early period but throughout his career. For instance, there is a fragment of a sermon preached for election day, May 29, 1728 (73). In this pronouncement of New England exceptionalism, Edwards states that God has "distinguished" the "nation," meaning England and her empire, in its "knowledge of the true God," even of those who "profess the Protestant religion," because of religious liberty and the sympathy of those in government for "true" religion. He then goes on to show "how God has distinguished this land" of New England, which enjoys "gospel privileges more freely, plentifully and universally… than in any country in the world"—even more than in England. New England also has "greater civil liberties in this land than most of our neighbors, the other British colonies, if not greater than any of them."

Aside from the regular provincial fast held every April and special fasts called after calamitous events such as earthquakes, epidemics, or drought, towns could also hold days of fast. Living Unconverted Under an Eminent Means of Grace is an example of a town fast, called in Northampton to memorialize the recently "removed" minister, Solomon Stoddard. A fast could also be held locally when the county court was in session. Since Northampton was one of the venues for the Hampshire court, Edwards frequently preached to a congregation that included members of the court. Having leaders of the province collected before him was an auspicious opportunity for Edwards to bemoan the

-- 036 --

present state of morals. In Sin and Wickedness Bring Calamity and Misery on a People, he describes the woeful state of the government in embarrassing detail.

Provincial Affairs

Edwards' sermons in this period grew from the events that surrounded him. The economic and political circumstances of Massachusetts, the complex of interactions that included the personal lives of Edwards and his congregation, the intellectual and social climate of Northampton and environs, and the theological issues gripping New England as a whole all helped to shape his sermons. Though Edwards, like most of the clergy, does not usually refer directly to personal or political affairs in his sermons, the themes he pursues demonstrate a knowledge of and concern for them.

Political issues were especially formative in Edwards' occasional sermons. For many in New England, the Charter of 1691, which replaced self-rule with royal rule, represented an intrusion by the crown upon popular control of the government. The former Puritan haven of Massachusetts—in 1729 quite self-consciously celebrating its centennial—had a history of self-determination that stretched back to the Charter of 1629, and the inhabitants had by no means accommodated themselves to direct royal control. As a result, real or perceived threats to traditional rights and privileges were met with stubborn resistance, resulting in constant friction between the royally appointed governor and the popularly elected deputies who made up the General Court. In Massachusetts, as in her sister colonies, the seeds of revolution were already germinating in the 1720s.7

Economically, early eighteenth-century Massachusetts was an emerging mercantile society characterized by growing social and financial disparity. An unstable economic situation, engendered in part by unchecked speculation, was exacerbated by irresponsible fiscal policy. Yeoman farmers and artisans, lacking specie, were unable to pay their debts to their creditors because the paper money issued by the government rapidly depreciated. Social instability was also on the rise. Rising population made land less available, causing intergenerational tensions and forcing sons to continue to rely on their parents, emigrate or find other trades.8 Consequently, the patterns and values of

-- 037 --

the past no longer entirely governed the lives of the "rising generations."

The towns in which Edwards preached were microcosms of the shift from Puritan communal, agrarian values to the new individualist, capitalist ethos, and his sermons record the clash of these two ways of life. As if fully appreciative of the changes that were occurring, Edwards urgently appealed to Christian principles of morality and mutual obligation from his pulpit as the means of shoring up deteriorating social structures. At Bolton he took a positive approach in Living Peaceably One With Another, noting that virtue among a people brings earthly riches and a good reputation. Shortly into the Northampton pastorate, however, Edwards abandoned such an approach. The Day of Judgment castigates those who indulge in "secret wickedness" and fraudulent business practices.

The tenure of Governor William Burnet, which began in May 1728 and ended in September 1729, as well as that of his successor, Jeremiah Dummer, was characterized by sharp conflict over the limits of executive privilege that reflected economic instability as well as growing opposition between Whigs and Tories both in the mother country and in the colonies. No controversies were more pronounced in Massachusetts than those over the questions of a permanent gubernatorial salary and control of tax money. Traditionally, the governor relied on the lower house to vote him an annual allowance, thereby preventing him from becoming completely independent and arbitrary. On this basis the representatives, protesting the matter to be one of "English Liberty," refused to grant a permanent salary.9 Throughout the 1720s, too, the lower house insisted not only on appropriation of tax revenues but also on confirmation of how it was spent—a power that the governors rightly claimed as theirs.1

By the fall of 1729, the conflict reached serious and disruptive proportions. In response to the lower house's intransigence, the Board of Trade threatened to have Parliament take up the issue of dissolving the charter. This made the inhabitants of Massachusetts very uneasy, as a new charter could have been even more restrictive than the Charter of 1691. Also, in June 1729 Burnet had the General Court meet

-- 038 --

outside of Boston on the grounds that the townspeople were trying to "pervert" the minds of the members with their democratic sentiments. The lower house, disingenuously proclaiming its allegiance to the king, refused to yield.2

Edwards and his congregation may not have known every detail of the political conflict, but having access to newspapers as well as traders and travellers to Boston, and through frequent discussions with the Northampton deputies, they were surely acquainted with the events and what they purported. It is not wholly clear where Edwards' political sympathy lay. Certainly he had an upper-class temperament and outlook, but it does not automatically follow that he was unswervingly elitist or royalist in his beliefs. His final political and social appeal seems to have been to preserve a virtuous commonwealth through law, order, and voluntary obedience. In sermons such as True Nobleness of Mind, he calls upon officials and community leaders to provide godly examples and to take strong measures to regulate the behavior of the people. But Edwards was capable of blanket criticisms of "civil rulers" of any persuasion if they departed from their duty and sought their own interests rather than the public good. Sin and Wickedness, delivered in early December before the county court, denounces the political ethics of the leaders not only of Massachusetts but of the mother country as well. "If law-makers are also law-breakers, their laws will be but of little force. Their examples will have greater influence than their laws or their care to execute them upon others." At such times, he warns, "shepherds will turn into wolves." Lamenting the "contention amongst our rulers," he concludes in Sin and Wickedness that "Some or others of them must be exceedingly to blame."

Sacramental Theology and Conversion

As with occasional sermons, none of Edwards' sermons preached for the celebration of the Lord's Supper has hitherto been available in print, which has led to a failure to appreciate a sacramental theology in Edwards apart from the celebrated controversy over the qualifications for communion in 1749 and 1750. Yet there is an abundance of sacrament sermons in the sermon corpus going back to late 1728. The Spiritual Blessings of the Gospel Represented by a Feast is the earliest extant sacrament sermon by Edwards. It exemplifies the prevailing character of Edwards' sacrament sermons during the period, which is to make

-- 039 --

his listeners appreciate the privilege and joy of the Lord's Supper, to see it not as a formal duty but as a happy event. The celebration of the Lord's Supper is portrayed by Edwards here and in other sacrament-day meditations as the ultimate expression of the "pleasantness" of religion, a theme he visits repeatedly in both sermons and "Miscellanies." Spiritual Blessings uses the allegory of a feast to convey the abundance of God's bounty to believers. In a similar vein is a sermon from the following year, on Canticles 5:1 (117), a rapturous meditation that encourages communicants to put no restraints on their "spiritual appetites" and to "swim in rivers of pleasure."

But behind Edwards' sacrament sermons was a vital and more somber question: what were the prerequisite conditions for approaching the table? His interest in this issue resulted in a prolonged inquiry into the nature of conversion that began in the spring of 1723, when, as a nineteen-year-old about to start graduate studies, he struggled with the state of his soul. He felt that he had humbled himself and had tasted God's "sweetness" but was puzzled over his lack of a conviction of sin. Consequently, he had resolved to read "nicely and diligently" the opinions of the "old divines, concerning conversion."3

Edwards was not alone in raising questions about conversion and related issues. Arguments concerning the nature of conversion and its relationship to the sacraments of baptism and communion had been endemic to New England since the implementation of the Half-Way Covenant of 1662, which extended sacramental privileges to children of parents not in full membership. During the first several decades of the eighteenth century, New England's congregational polity came under renewed attack. Stoddard had a major role in these disputes, arguing for more open admission policies and maintaining that the Lord's Supper was a "converting ordinance."4 But in his sermons and private notes during this period, Edwards was distancing himself from Stoddard's views on conversion and the sacraments.

Edwards suspected that the "dullness" or unresponsiveness of a significant portion of his congregation to the preaching of the new birth was attributable to their willingness to rely on a past identifiable experience, or a "supposed" day of grace, and on their status as unconverted

-- 040 --

full members. The fault for this abuse of the means of grace had to be laid at Stoddard's feet. According to Samuel Hopkins, Edwards had hesitated to come to Northampton because he secretly viewed with suspicion the "lax mode of admission" that Stoddard had established in the church, which allowed anyone of non-scandalous behavior and historical faith to partake of the sacraments.5 Around the time of his grandfather's death, Edwards was busily scrutinizing Stoddard's views on humiliation, conversion, and visible Christianity with a strong degree of disapproval. To his private notebooks he committed his concern that the professions of a number of his people made under Stoddard's way had been wittingly or unwittingly insincere.6

The prevalence of "hypocrisy" had been an issue for Edwards since A Spiritual Understanding, but coming to Northampton fixed the topic in his mind. For Edwards, hypocrisy consisted not so much in saying one thing and doing another but in self-delusion, the erroneous perception that one was saved. Many of the churchgoers of Northampton possessed a great deal of religious knowledge, but it was not the spiritual knowledge of which Edwards spoke. His parishioners all too often believed themselves, by virtue of their insufficient knowledge and profession, to be true saints. In sermons dating from the colleagueship and afterwards, such as Profitable Hearers of the Word, None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness, and The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost, Edwards seeks to alert his listeners to the danger of this form of hypocrisy.

Since the day in 1723 that Edwards had resolved to read all he could about conversion, he had come to recognize the great variety of religious experiences and to espouse an alternate conception of conversion to that of the rigid preparationism advocated by many "old divines," his grandfather among them. Edwards' view was informed by the two schools of preparationism, rooted in Reformed and English Puritan thought, that prevailed in colonial New England. The one derived from its chief American progenitor, Thomas Hooker, who had emphasized that individual spiritual growth should adhere to the conversion process yet had not insisted on stringent tests for admission to the sacraments; the other from John Cotton, who had minimized the process and whose admission policies had been very strict. Stoddard was a proponent of the former, and Edwards' father subscribed

-- 041 --

to the latter. Whereas Stoddard had contended that individuals spend a nominal amount of time in each of the stages and trace their conversion to an epiphanic moment, an "explicit act of faith," Timothy Edwards did not insist on locating conversion at a particular time. Reflecting on his own impressive career as a revivalist—which rivalled that of Stoddard—Timothy allowed for a considerable degree of flexibility from individual to individual and focused instead on the end result of a person's experience.7 Thus Jonathan's efforts to evaluate his grandfather's position revealed the abiding influence of his father's views.

On the basis of his father's and grandfather's combined insights, Edwards began to build a synthetic view of the nature of conversion. He increasingly concentrated on the nature of the religious experience rather than on how intensely individuals hewed their lives to the conversion process. Although Edwards held that there could be no good work before conversion, he refuted the common preparationist notion that sanctification could not occur before an exercise of faith. Just as there could be no effect without a cause, there could be no act of faith without a previous indwelling of sanctifying grace.8

Edwards was walking a fine line. Although not explicitly contradicting his grandfather's practices and beliefs, he was in the process of bluntly questioning them. For many of the inhabitants of Northampton, the implication was that the converting experiences that had gained them access to full membership may have been false. For Edwards, allowing such dissemblers to the table contradicted its true meaning. Though he supported the general effort by the clergy to coerce hesitant laity into covenant, he was not willing to do so at the expense of church purity.9

-- 042 --

Of the two sacrament sermons presented here, Glorying in the Savior is the most harsh in its condemnation of sacramental hypocrisy.1 Here Edwards excoriates the disparity between profession and obedience so evident in many purported believers by attempting to define the true spiritual seed of Israel. Nominal Christians, though they claim to be of God's people and attend the church's ordinances, are really no better than heathens. "But God looks at the heart," Edwards explains, "and they only are indeed the people of Jesus Christ and of his flock that have given their hearts to him and serve him." Other so-called Christians, though they pretend to glory in Christ, actually glory in themselves, in their "outward advantages," in their works, and in their own righteousness. Those who take pride in being "of such and such a party among Christians" do not ultimately put their faith in God but in the flesh. Here Edwards is taking aim at those who emphasize the outward marks of status that attend their religion—in particular, those associated with partaking of the Lord's Supper—rather than the sincere faith that, in his view, merits admission to it.

Edwards had not yet reached a point where he could categorically oppose the established practices of Stoddard, but neither did he feel comfortable with many of grandfather's principles. Edwards' recourse was to use the sermon as an educational tool to persuade his hearers to examine their practices. Henceforth, his formulation of high doctrines—such as the nature of the church and admission to its ordinances—increasingly became linked to his ongoing efforts with his congregation. In this sense, the "qualifications controversy" did not begin a mere four or five years before the actual fact; rather, its origins can be traced to the very outset of Edwards' ministry at Northampton.

Trinitarian Controversy

Always a student of current scholarly "controversies," Edwards preached not only on local and provincial debates regarding New England's experiment in church government. He also brought to the Northampton pulpit the issues occupying the attention of European theologians. In an era when fundamental and long-standing assumptions

-- 043 --

about religion were being questioned, Arminianism was one challenge to Calvinist orthodoxy. But anti-trinitarianism, in the forms of Arianism and Socinianism, was also spreading throughout Europe.2 Arianism, named after the fourth-century theologian Arius, maintained that though Christ existed before the incarnation, he was a created being and therefore not God in the fullest sense. Socinianism, founded by the sixteenth-century Italian Faustus Socinus, denied the divinity of Christ altogether and held that the Holy Spirit was not a separate person of the godhead but merely a power exerted by God.

The seventeenth-century English advocates of these schools of thought instigated a salient and lengthy dispute known as the Trinitarian Controversy.3 By the time that Edwards became full pastor of Northampton, the debate was not as prominent as it had been during the previous decade, but its residual effects were being noticed in the colonies.4 In 1724, Cotton Mather pointed to the threat of Arianism, expressing concern that "so many of our Brethren should so openly declare, that they… can receive no CHRIST but one that is infinitely Inferiour and Posteriour to the Eternal Father."5 And Stephen Williams, Edwards' relative and the minister of nearby Longmeadow, Massachusetts, wrote in July 1729: "I prceive—vice—& Heresie, wickdness and Atheism & deism—& Arianism abound & get head in or nation—oh dark day—ye Ld be pleasd to appear—& for thy own names

-- 044 --

sake—be pleasd to pardon—oh Ld—graciously forgive—& heal thy people."6

Edwards himself was fully acquainted with the efforts in England and New England to refute anti-trinitarianism. His "Catalogue of Reading" lists several of the important works to come out of the debate.7 In the pulpit, Edwards condemned these heresies, cautioned against their spread, and ridiculed people who "have itching" for "new teachers and doctrines." In Sin and Wickedness, for example, he warns that "when false doctrines are very much prevailing amongst a people, then wickedness may be said to prevail."

Edwards' most explicit reactions against anti-trinitarianism did not come until later in his life. Yet his interest in defending trinitarianism, which grew in part from reactions to ideas emanating from Europe, helps explain why in the late 1720s he was grappling with the nature of the Trinity in a sustained way. The "Discourse on the Trinity," started in 1730, was actually a reworking of passages culled from notebook entries and sermons written up to that time, among them The Threefold Work. This sermon, Edwards' lengthiest and arguably most important one to date, is an Augustinian explanation of the relationship between the persons of the Trinity. But it operates on other levels as well: it addresses the long-standing doctrinal controversy; it contains some of Edwards' most detailed pronouncements on the Trinity, the atonement, and the Holy Spirit; and it serves as a benchmark for other sermons composed at this time.8

Edwards' response to the anti-trinitarians, as worked out in these sermons, was to combine a covenantal view of the Trinity with a unique conception of the nature of grace. The dual natures of Christ, as human and divine, were of fundamental importance in Edwards' doctrine of the Trinity. In The Sacrifice of Christ Acceptable, Edwards states that for Christ to become incarnate was essential to fulfilling the terms of the covenant. For this reason, Edwards seeks to demonstrate in this sermon that Christ as the second Adam satisfies both the moral and

-- 045 --

positive law. "Now upon this account," he states, "the price offered was equivalent to the demerit of the sins of all mankind, [and] his sufferings equivalent to the eternal sufferings of the whole world." Exhibiting the influence of the Continental Reformed scholastics Peter van Mastricht and Francis Turretin,9 Edwards maintains that Christ's sacrifice is not merely "sufficient" to satisfy commutatively for the elect, but, as Christ is an infinitely holy person, he has the power to save infinitely. Thus Edwards also asserts in The Threefold Work that "where there is infinity, it is impossible there should be any want; the fountain is so great that it is not lessened at all by communication."

Edwards agreed with Augustine that the Holy Spirit was co-equal, consubstantial, and co-eternal with the Father and Son. But his principal point of contact with Augustine in The Threefold Work comes when he describes the Holy Spirit as the agent of love that was given to the church.1 As all three persons have the "same divine essence," he points out, none has more excellence, dignity, or power than the other.2 Yet for Edwards the Holy Spirit was nothing less than the embodiment of God's love, and it was the Holy Spirit itself that entered into the believer's soul and became the new "principle."3

Thus Edwards' interests had come full circle from the early sermons on spiritual light and excellency at Bolton. Now, however, as a result of six years of intensive study, writing, and preaching, he had established a broader theological and apologetical framework for the distinctive concepts he had worked out hitherto. The coming years would provide him with the opportunity further to expand and elaborate the convictions that he had fixed on in these formative years.

From 1723 to 1729, Jonathan Edwards passed many milestones. At New York he had had the benefit of a small and supportive circle of fellow Christians in which he could give expression to his personal religious experiences following his conversion in the spring of 1721. But upon his return to New Haven in the summer of 1723 to complete his graduate work, Edwards found himself in circumstances that called for more and more responsibility and that challenged his intellect and spirit. In some ways the Bolton congregation was similar to the one at New York, yet Edwards apparently encountered a less harmonious situation there than he had enjoyed previously. Personally, however, Edwards developed significantly at Bolton; his religious life flourished and his intellectual life was marked by the formulation of central tenets in his thought, such as spiritual light and excellency. The two-year tutorship, although important to Edwards' pursuit of scholarship, was a time of trial and difficulty. Beset by the burden of his many duties, Edwards fell ill and complained of his spiritual "dullness."

Edwards' call to Northampton signalled an end to his wandering and gave him an opportunity to become permanently involved with a congregation. Under Stoddard's tutelage, Edwards developed his skill at delivering sermons for civic occasions as well as at using terror preaching. Such skills would be sorely needed, for Edwards found a congregation that was "sermon proof" and that placed too much confidence in previous religious experiences. His efforts were hampered by a further bout of illness, arising possibly from creative anxiety and overwork, soon after Stoddard died. Following his recovery, Edwards sought with renewed determination to infuse old truths with new meanings by using his own conception of conversion and typology, by emphasizing God's sovereignty in his governance of the world and bestowal of grace, and by counselling his listeners to self-examination and a sense of individual unworthiness. Within a short time, Edwards had gone far in defining themes for his tenure at Northampton. But it remained for him in the following years to consolidate the pastoral relation and authority that he had begun to establish and to construct a program of preaching on practical topics.

3. An important stage of the controversy ensued in England when Samuel Clarke, a prominent Anglican theologian, published The Scripture Doctrine of the Trinity in 1712. In this seminal work, Clarke renounced the Athanasian Creed's formulation of three co-equal and co-eternal persons as unscriptural and treated the trinitarian question as non-essential to the faith. Clark's book, indicative of the changing tide of opinion, elicited a mountain of print, with discussion centering on the persons of the Son and Holy Ghost. One of the most prolific of his opponents, Calvinist clergyman John Edwards, lamented that some of the most impressive minds in England were taking up the heretical causes. "But now," he wrote, "our Elephants are turn'd against ourselves. Some of our own Body are gone over to the Enemy, and thereby the state of the Religious War is wretchedly alter'd."
