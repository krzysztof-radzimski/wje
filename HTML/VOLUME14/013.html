Profitable Hearers of the Word

Preached in the period between August 1728 and February 1729, this sermon is based on the parable of the sower (Matthew 13:3–23). Edwards uses the allegory to expound on spiritual knowledge, Christian behavior, and the signs of grace. As such, it is related to Edwards' work in new notebooks begun during the same period, particularly "Signs of Godliness" and "Christ's Example." It also shows Edwards' interest in ideas central to his religious psychology very early on in his career, resulting possibly from the cumulative effects of his father's and grandfather's preaching and counsel, his meditations on his own spiritual experience, and perhaps even lessons learned from the minor awakening following the earthquake of October 1727.

Picking up on a theme that goes back to the Bolton period and A Spiritual Understanding of Divine Things Denied to the Unregenerate, Edwards begins by showing how "profitable hearers" have a qualitatively different understanding of divine things from that of natural men. This understanding, he states, "consists in the apprehension and judgment." Believers have a "sensible apprehension of the main things" revealed in God's Word, including the glory of God, the excellency and fullness of Christ, the way of salvation, the foundation of duty, and an "experimental" knowledge of the nature of holiness. In the second act of the understanding, judgment, profitable hearers "believe the truth of the Word" because of "the intrinsic signatures of divinity which they see in it." When they hear the Word of God, they are assured of its divinity because God has opened their understandings. Edwards then uses his definition of understanding to try to convince unprofitable hearers that this "hidden wisdom" is above all earthly knowledge and what God prizes most. Many people grow up hearing the gospel and yet suffer from a "blindness of the mind." They must realize their ignorance and helplessness and come to God for true understanding.

Profitable hearers also differ in that they do good works, the "fruit" of faith. In this head, Edwards is giving form to categories that will shape his pastoral theology and views on religious affections. In the two inquiries that follow, he draws on the earliest entries in "Signs of

-- 244 --

Godliness," a notebook that he began in order to record signs and Scripture proofs. The first inquiry identifies keeping Christ's commandments as the most important fruit that believers "bring forth." This fruit is either internal, "a principle of spiritual life infused into the soul that exerts itself in holy acts and exercises," or external, meaning good works. The second inquiry seeks to demonstrate that profitable hearers differ from hypocrites in that their obedience is sincere, universal, and persevering.

Knowledge and understanding of God's Word, Edwards states in the second doctrine, is the "root and foundation of fruitfulness." Only the seed cast on good ground will grow and flourish; Edwards likens the root of the plant to "spiritual understanding," which is necessary if a person is to act reasonably. "God has given us a reasonable nature for this end principally, that it might be exercised and improved in spiritual things…. Therefore when man acts spiritually, he acts rationally, according to the reasonable nature." The fruits, or good works, are the result not only of the good ground but also of the seed, which is the Word. Edwards exhorts his listeners to seek this sort of understanding by seeing their own ignorance. Begin by attaining a "notional knowledge" of the principles and doctrines of religion, since "this is the way wherein God reveals spiritual knowledge," and by regularly reading the Scriptures.

Though the godly "agree in essentials," yet there are differing degrees of grace and good works to be found amongst them. They are all "alike justified in the sight of God by the righteousness of Christ," but they do differ in "degrees of inherent righteousness," or the "measure" of the Spirit given to them by God. In some, grace is strong, in others weak. Some abound in good works, though they are not "in proportion to the degree of internal holiness." In other words, there is not necessarily a direct correlation between the degree of inherent righteousness and good works. God himself, and more specifically his good pleasure, is the "foundation" of this difference as well as its end. God sovereignly bestows grace in varying degrees because he establishes different work for different people in the church, because some are bound to suffer more than others, and because some are designed for greater degrees of glory in heaven.

These considerations lead Edwards to conclude by encouraging his listeners to strive for more grace and good works. Being contented with an "imperfect degree" of grace shows a lack of grace, which will issue in "less comfort" because less enjoyment of and communion with

-- 245 --

God. Those who are weak in grace will be tempted more easily into sin. But those who are strong in grace will have "firm peace and comfort upon a deathbed." Those who have been diligent throughout their lives in the pursuit of grace and good works will not only have a peaceful death but also more glory in heaven. A series of final practical directions instructs those seeking grace to "begin early," to engage regularly in examining their lives, to resist favorite sins, and to increase "foundation-graces," the fundamental principles of religion.

The manuscript is thirty-five duodecimo leaves written in a medium brown ink. The sermon has an unusual structure. Edwards begins by stating three doctrines, which are discussed over three preaching units. Each doctrine has its own application, with the exception of the first doctrine, which is split into two heads that are separately applied. Edwards' later notation indicates that he repreached the sermon in June 1756, but he made only minor additions and highlighted some passages in a brighter brown ink.

Profitable Hearers of the Word Matthew 13:23.1 But he that received seed into the good ground is he that heareth the word, and understandeth it; which also beareth fruit, some an hundredfold, some sixty, some thirty.

This parable of the sower and the seed is one of the most notable of Christ's parables that we have account of; and it being so, 'twill not be improper in this place a little to inquire into the reasons why Christ so commonly taught in this way. The following reasons may be given:

In the first place, 'tis an engaging way of instructing. It engages the attention of the hearers, when any doctrine is taught in an allegory and by way of story. It is a most familiar way, as a parent would instruct a child. Christ condescended to the weakness and childishness of the people, and instructed by easy and familiar comparisons and similitudes.

And then it is a very instructive way. The reason of the thing many times will presently be seen by an apt comparison, that otherwise is difficultly explained.

And another reason why we are so often instructed in Scriptures in an allegorical way, in Christ's parables and elsewhere, is that we might have some exercise for our understandings to find out the truth contained in them. Our understandings were given us to be used, and above all to be exercised, in divine things. Therefore God teaches us in such a way that we shall have some exercise of meditation and study. God gives us the gold, but he gives it to us in a mine that we might dig for it and get in a way of our own industry.

And then, when the truth is found out, it makes a greater impression, is much more pleasing. If truth was revealed in such a way that we could understand it as well without study or diligence as with, and fools could understand as much as wise men, truth would be despised.

-- 247 --

If gold were thrown plentifully before every man's face, and everyone could have it without any labor or industry, it would not be prized as now it is. But God gives it to us in mines; we must dig for it, if we get it, and that makes it precious. So God gives us divine truth as it were in mines—in allegories and parables and types—where we must dig for it to come at it.

'Tis for the same reasons that abundance of the Scripture is obscure and difficult to be understood. The prophecies were delivered in dark similitudes. It was not Jesus Christ in the flesh that first brought in this way of teaching. God always used it, and especially under the old testament, where all things are in types and shadows; and these types were not only for the Jews' sakes, but also for the church to the end of the world. This is not only God's method in Scripture, but his method in nature also. The works of God are hard to be understood, that they might be "sought out of all them that have pleasure in them" (Psalms 111:2).

For the same reason, the prophets were often appointed to do such and such things for a sign.

This reason also may be given that Christ spoke so much in parable: that many of that people were not capable of receiving gospel truths plainly declared. It would be like putting new wine into old bottles. For the same reason also, the gospel was preached to the Jews under a veil; they were not able to look upon Moses' face except there was a veil put over it.

But the principle reason seems to be that which Christ himself gives to his disciples when they asked him, beginning with the tenth verse, "And the disciples came, [and said] to him, Why speakest thou unto them in parables? He answered and said unto them, Because it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given. For whosoever hath, to him shall be given, and he shall have more abundance: but whosoever hath not, from him shall be taken away even that he hath. Therefore speak I to them in parables: because they seeing see not; and hearing they hear not, neither do they understand." It was that those that had honest hearts and had pleasure in the truth and sought it out might understand it, but that others might not understand it. Those that had a love to divine truth would be likely to know, but others would be ignorant. Therefore we may observe that Christ seldom speaks to his own disciples in parables, and seldom speaks to the multitude without a parable. This may serve something to enlighten us with respect to the reason of Christ's using so many parables.

-- 248 --

Christ, as we are informed in the former part of the chapter, had been speaking a parable to the people; but in distinguishing favor to his disciples, when they were by themselves, he explains the parable to them. Our text is part of that explanation, wherein he explains what are meant by the good ground wherein the seed was so fruitful. Where I take notice of:

1. The root which the seed of the Word took in this ground. "He heareth the word, and understandeth it." The hearing of the Word answered to the falling of the seed upon the ground, and the understanding it to its taking root in the ground.

2. The fruit that is here. "Which also beareth fruit." This is what none of those other sorts of ground did. The seed in the stony and thorny ground sprung up at first, as if it would be fruitful, but it soon failed and no fruit followed.

3. The different degrees of fruitfulness. "Some an hundred, some sixty, some thirty." It was all reckoned as good ground, that that were but thirtyfold, as well as that which produced sixty and an hundred.

From the words I observe three doctrines:

Doctrine I.
That which distinguishes the profitable hearers of God's Word from all others, is that they understand it and bring forth the fruit of it.
Doctrine II.
Men's knowledge is the root and foundation of their fruitfulness.
Doctrine III.
There is a great difference in the degrees of grace and fruitfulness among the truly godly.

[I.] 'Tis the first of these that we propose to say something to at this time, viz. {That which distinguishes the profitable hearers of God's Word from all others, is that they understand it and bring forth the fruit of it}.

[First.] The first thing is that they understand the Word of God. Natural men and hypocrites may boast of an extensive understanding, and may have natural abilities in a much greater strength than a godly man, and may abound in acquired knowledge, and may be able to reason with great strength about the holy Scriptures and the doctrines of religion; but yet he does not, nor can he, understand the Word of God. He hears it, and he may receive it with joy, and may have such

-- 249 --

knowledge as to be able to penetrate into deep mysteries and to explain in them, but yet it can't be said that they understand the Word of God.

The understanding consists in the apprehension and judgment. And in order to the understanding of God's Word, it is necessary that they should have a sensible and clear apprehension of the main things that are therein revealed, and that they should believe them—in both which the profitable hearers of the Word of God are distinguished from all others.

1. They are distinguished from others in that they have a sensible apprehension of the main things that are therein revealed. Ungodly men are so far from understanding the Word of God, that those things that are the main things of revelation, the principal things of the gospel and what are the very quintessence and end of all, are what they have no notion at all of and which the godly only apprehend; as particularly, such things as these: the glory of God, the excellency and fullness of Jesus Christ, the nature of holiness, the reason and foundation of duty. These things are the very main things of the Scripture. They are the greatest doctrines of God's Word, and they are the very end of revelation and its life and soul; and yet they are such as natural men have no idea or apprehension of. Deuteronomy 29:4, "The Lord hath not given you [an heart to perceive, and eyes to see, and ears to hear, unto this day]." And they are those things that God is pleased to discover to his people, and give them eyes to see.

(1) They are the godly only that have any apprehension of the glory of God. The gloriousness of God is the very principal thing of all that we are taught concerning God in the holy Scriptures. All that we are told concerning the attributes of God, or the works of God, is to this end: to teach us the gloriousness, the majesty and excellency of God. Indeed, it is not only the end of the Scriptures, but 'tis the end of the works of creation and providence to show forth God's glory.

But unregenerate men are wholly ignorant of this; they are blind and see no glory in God. How well soever they can argue about God's power or wisdom, or justice or mercy, and reason about his works, yet [they] see no divine glory appearing in any of these things.

But the profitable hearer of the Word sees the gloriousness of God. He understands what the Word of God declares concerning his glorious greatness, his majesty, his glorious wisdom, his glorious faithfulness, love and grace. Matthew 11:27, "No man knoweth the Father, but the Son and he to whomsoever the Son will [reveal him]."

-- 250 --

(2) 'Tis only the godly that have any understanding of the excellency and fullness of Christ which is taught in the Word. Jesus Christ is the sum and substance of the gospel; he is the main subject of revelation since the Fall. The Apostle said that he desired to know nothing but Jesus Christ, and him crucified [1 Corinthians 2:2]. And the principal thing that is revealed in the gospel is the excellency and sufficiency of Christ. But this the natural man, though he often hears the Word, understands nothing of: he don't see how Christ is so excellent and desirable as he is said to be in the Word of [God]. They don't see that there is such a fountain in him for the supply of poor, needy, sinful creatures. But true Christians do understand this: they have seen the King in his beauty; they understand that Christ is more precious than rubies, and that he is a fountain of wealth and honor and happiness. Whatever temporal knowledge men have, they only know this that have it revealed by the special enlightening of God himself. 2 Corinthians 4:6, "God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." Galatians 1:15–16, "But when it pleased God, […] to reveal his Son in me."

(3) They only understand the way of salvation. This is a great and main subject of revelation, but yet unregenerate persons do not understand it. They are told plainly enough that Christ by his death satisfied the offended justice of God, because his temporal suffering was equivalent to our eternal suffering, being an infinite person; and that his righteousness is meritorious of eternal life, because it is perfect, and the righteousness of one of infinite dignity, and was freely undertaken. Nevertheless they don't see the equalness and the excellency of this way and how it every way answers our needs. This the Spirit of God alone discovers. Psalms 25:14, "The secret of the Lord is with them that fear him; and he will show them his covenant." John 6:45, "Every man therefore that hath heard, and hath learned of the Father, cometh unto me."

(4) The godly are distinguished from others in that they understand the foundation of duty. This is another main thing that the Word of God is conversant about, the instructing us in our duty. 'Tis a revelation of the will of God as directing our actions and behavior, and a revelation of the reason and foundation of it. And therefore, surely they can't be said to understand the Word that don't understand the foundation of duty.

The main foundation and reason of our obligation to the duty that

-- 251 --

is required of us, is God's infinite majesty and excellency and exaltation above us. This is the foundation of our duty more immediately to God, our loving and fearing and worshipping him: it is because he, by his greatness and excellency, is worthy of these things; and this is also the prime foundation of the duty we owe to our neighbor: 'tis that they have the image of God and are made for God's service and glory, and because God requires these and those duties of us towards them.

And therefore the ungodly that don't understand God's greatness and worthiness, don't understand the reason of duty. They don't apprehend wherein the excellency and equity and becomingness of duty does consist; if they did, they would practice it.

But God opens the eyes of his people to see these things; and therefore when they hear the will of God declared, they understand the reasonableness and excellency of it (Psalms 119:33–34).

(5) They have the experimental knowledge of the nature of that holiness which is recommended and required by the Word. He that don't understand what holiness is, can't understand that doctrine which is according to godliness: for the whole drift of the Word of God is to teach and recommend holiness.

Holiness is a thing that has its seat in the heart and is from the secret influence of the Spirit of God there; and therefore the knowledge of what it is can only be had by experience. A man that never had no holiness in his heart can no more have a true conception what it is than a blind man of colors.

The godly have experience, and therefore know what it is: they know what the several graces of the Spirit are, they know what faith is, they know what divine love is, they know what repentance is and what spiritual joy is. And therefore when they read or hear of these things, they understand the Word. And therefore it is said that the godly have God's law "written in their hearts" [Romans 2:15], because they have these things in their hearts which the law recommends; and therefore when they read or hear, they find an agreement as there is between an original [and] an exact copy.

[2.] The second act of the understanding is judgment. In this also the profitable hearers of the Word are distinguished from others, in that they believe the truth of the Word.

Natural men may indeed have a common belief of many things in the Word, as they have of probable histories. But the faith of the godly, whereby they believe the Word of God to be true, is from the intrinsic signatures of divinity which they see in it. They see that excellency and

-- 252 --

that image of God in the Word that constrains the mind to assent to it and embrace it as true and divine. There are signatures of divine majesty to be seen in the Word, and signatures of divine wisdom and of divine holiness, and the evident marks of divine grace that make it evident that the Word of God did proceed from a divine majesty and wisdom, and holiness and grace. There are as proper manifestations of divinity in the speech of God as there are manifestations of humanity in the speech of men. God opens the understandings of profitable hearers to see these signatures and manifestations of divinity, so that they hear it as the Word of God. They do as it were hear God speak, and they are assured of the divinity of his speech, for he speaks like a God. His speech is not like the speech of men, but like the speech of God: divinely excellent, holy, wise, awful and gracious.

And 'tis this sort of faith only that will hold the mind through all temptations. This is a sure foundation. This makes the truth of the gospel in a manner intuitively certain. When all other kinds of belief are wavering and unstable, this is an everlasting foundation.

[Application.]

[Use] I. From hence we learn that [the] meanest and weakest of the saints have a more excellent wisdom given them than the most knowing of unconverted men. The Apostle tells us, 1 Corinthians 1:26–27, "For you see your calling, brethren, how that not many wise men after the flesh, not many mighty, not many noble, are called. But God hath chosen the foolish things of this world to confound the wise."

This wisdom is the most excellent jewel that God bestows. 'Tis that wisdom that is better than gold, or much fine gold; more precious than rubies, or all things that can be desired. And God bestows it on whom he will. He is not moved by fine parts, or learning, or any natural or moral qualification. He bestows it on the mean, obscure and weak, and at once lifts 'em from the dunghill and sets them among princes. He exalts them by his wonderful enlightening grace above the wise, the mighty and the noble.

This is the most excellent knowledge, to understand the Word of God. God's Word teaches things of the highest nature. It teaches those spiritual, heavenly and divine things that earthly things, that other men's knowledge is conversant about, is but as dirt and dung to. They that understand these things, they understand what is most worthy to be understood. And they have the best understanding that understand

-- 253 --

the glory of God and the excellency of Christ, that know the way of salvation, that understand the foundation of duty and the nature of true holiness. These are the greatest and the worthiest things.

Therefore God despises other knowledge and wisdom in comparison of this. He calls wicked men fools very often. This hidden wisdom God sets vastly above the wisdom of the princes of this world. 1 Corinthians 2:6–8, "Howbeit we speak wisdom to them that are perfect: but not the wisdom of this world, nor of the princes of this world, that come to naught: but we speak the wisdom of God in a mystery, even the hidden wisdom, which God ordained before the world unto our glory: which none of the princes of this world knew."

God accounts their wisdom foolishness. 1 Corinthians 3:19, "For the wisdom of this world is foolishness with God."

Use II is of Conviction, to convince natural men of their ignorance, to convince them that they do not understand the Word of God, and so that they are unprofitable hearers of it. There are most of those that live under the preaching and within the hearing of God's Word through the world, are those that don't understand it. They hear it all their lifetime, but never come to know what is meant by it. They are some of those spoken of, 2 Timothy 3:7, that are "ever learning, and are never able to come to the knowledge of the truth."

There are many that are rational, capable, understanding men, that are wholly blind as to spiritual things. They neither do, nor can, understand them. The Word of God is a dead letter to him, as sounds without a meaning. 1 Corinthians 2:14, "But the natural man receiveth not the things of the Spirit of God: for they are foolishness unto him: neither can he know them, because they are spiritually discerned."

What has been said is sufficient matter of conviction to natural men that they do not understand the Word. For it is exceeding evident that the gloriousness and excellency of God and Jesus Christ, the way of salvation, the ground and foundation of duty and the nature of holiness, are the very main things of the Word of God: they [are] the life and soul of all that is revealed; they are the very end of and drift of the whole. God aims at nothing else in his Word but only to teach his own glory and the glory of the Mediator, to teach us the way of salvation and to teach our duty and true holiness, and to make us understand the ground, reason and nature of it. Therefore surely those that don't understand these things, don't understand the Word.

We can't be said to understand the word or speech of men, except we can understand what he that speaks principally means and intends by

-- 254 --

what he says. So neither can we be said properly to understand the speech of God unless we understand that which God principally intends and aims at in what he says.

And how can they be said to understand truth that don't know that is truth? Certainly they are blind with respect to truth that don't know it as truth. If once truth is seen in its proper color, it must of necessity be immediately embraced.

It is again very clear and evident that natural men do not understand the Word of God, in that they are no more influenced by it. The things are of such a nature, of such vast importance and concern, that it is a more sure evidence that they are not understood, that they have no more influence upon men to determine their dispositions and actions. For instance, the gloriousness of God, whereby he infinitely transcends all the excellency which is to be beheld in creatures, if it were understood, would certainly influence men to choose God, to love him and admire him. So the foundation of duty and the nature of holiness, if they were understood, would certainly determine men to the choice and practice of holiness.

The knowledge and understanding of these great things of the Word of God are only given by the illumination of the Spirit of God. Men by study can make no approaches to such an understanding without God's teaching accompanying.

It argues the exceeding great and utter blindness of men, that they should not understand those things that they are so frequently taught, that [they] hear so many explications and illustrations of; and such particular arguments to convince them of such things that they often read of; and that they are taught by their parents when they are little children, and taught in the preaching of the Word every sabbath day; and that they are not only taught by the Word, but by the works of God. The works of God do show his glory, and many arguments are to be shown from them that do most demonstratively show the perfections of God.

This matter of conviction to natural men of their blindness may well,

First, awaken them and make them sensible of their misery in their natural condition. It is a great misery to [be] outwardly blind; we look upon it justly as an exceeding great calamity. But 'tis nothing in comparison of the blindness of the mind, to be blind with respect to those things that respect our eternal welfare. If we are outwardly blind, we may be in danger of falling to our wounding, of falling into the ditch;

-- 255 --

but if we are blind spiritually, we are in danger of falling into pit of hell, of dropping unawares into eternal misery.

Second. It may well humble them and make them sensible that they utterly helpless of themselves, as he that is born blind can't open his own eyes. This should bring them to own that they are fools, and cause them to despair by any endeavors of their own to bring themselves to see the glory of God or the excellency of Christ and the way of salvation.

Third. It may well excite them to come to God for understanding. Blind Bartimaeus was brought by his sense of his misery earnestly to cry, "Jesus, thou Son of David, have mercy on me"; and when Christ asked him what he would that he should do unto him, he said, "Lord, that I may receive my sight" [Mark 10:46–51]. And the apostle James directs us, James 1:5, "If any man lack wisdom, let him ask it of God, who giveth liberally and upbraideth not."2

[Doctrine I Resumed.]

There are many of other sorts of hearers besides profitable hearers. There are some that take little or no notice of what they hear, give scarce any attention to it and care but very little about [it]. There are others that are something affected by it and have joy in the hearing of it, and think themselves profitable hearers and hope that they get saving benefit by it, but they are not sound at heart; their affections are superficial and transient, and are soon gone. There are some that seem to be made so concerned by the Word as to take up good resolutions and make promises, that yet, when they go about their worldly concerns, are wholly diverted by pursuits after profits and the honor and pleasures of this life.

And there are but few that ever bring fruit to perfection. They are but a handful in comparison, a company that the world don't distinguish; they live amongst others and the world don't discern them asunder. But yet there is a great distinction between them: they differ as much as gold differs from dross.

And it is principally these two things that they differ in. We have shown already the difference there is in the first of them, viz. understanding the Word of God. We proceed now to the second, which is,

-- 256 --

[Second.] Bringing forth the fruit of it.3

In this also the profitable hearers of God's Word are distinguished from all others. Spiritual understanding is not mere speculation that rests in the head and reaches not the heart. It is not of an unactive or barren nature, but will surely be producing of good fruits. This is the end of preaching the Word of God, that these fruits may be produced, as the end of sowing seed is the produce of the fruit of it; and therefore though the seed be sown and also springs up, yet if it dies before the fruit be ripened, either through want of depth of earth or through weeds or thorns choking of it, the seed is sown in vain.

'Tis with respect to this that they are called profitable hearers, because they don't only hear but also do. They are doers of the Word. James 1:22–25, "But be ye doers of the Word, and not hearers only, deceiving your own selves. For if any man be a hearer of the Word, and not a doer, he is like unto a man beholding his natural face in a glass: for he beholdeth himself, and goeth his way, and straightway forgetteth what manner of man he was. But whoso looketh into the perfect law of liberty, and continueth therein, he being not a forgetful hearer, but a doer of the work, this man shall be blessed in his deed."

The Apostle warns us against deceiving ourselves in being hearers of the Word only, implying that if we think we are anything the better for the Word, except we do it, we deceive ourselves.

We always find this fruitfulness spoken of as the distinguishing mark of good from bad. God waited for nothing else from the barren fig tree, but only that it should bring forth fruit (Luke 13:7–9). He spoke of cutting of it down because it had not bore fruit and was therefore a cumberer of the ground. And when the dresser of the vineyard interceded, that it might be let alone three years longer, he says, "If it bear fruit, well: if not, after that thou shalt cut it down."

In our context, we have a text very pertinent to the present purpose in the parable of the good grain and the tares, beginning with the twenty-fourth verse. We have there an account that, after the man had sowed good seed in his field, an enemy came afterwards and sowed tares among the wheat; and when the blade was sprung up and brought forth fruit, then appeared the tares also. The tares before looked like the wheat; one could not know 'em asunder. But when each had brought forth fruit, then the difference appeared. The difference was not in the stalk or leaves, but in the fruit. So hypocrites are the

-- 257 --

same in profession as the godly: they come to meeting as God's people, and it may be come to the sacrament as God's people; they go along with the godly and make in many things as fair a show as they: but their fruits are not the same. When ye come there, they are as different as wheat and tares.

Again, all visible Christians are compared to branches in a tree or vine, of which some bear fruit and others do not. They all bear leaves, and the same sort of leaves, so that they make the same appearance; but the barren branches are to be cut off and cast into the fire, and the fruitful branches are to be purged and pruned that they may bear more fruit. John 15:2, "Every branch in me that beareth not fruit he taketh away: and every branch that beareth fruit, he purgeth it" (that is, he prunes it) "that it may bear more fruit."

This is the very way that the Scripture directs us to judge. Luke 6:44–45, "Every tree is known by his own fruit. For of thorns men do not gather figs, nor of a bramble bush gather they grapes. A good man out of the good treasure of his heart bringeth forth that which is good; and an evil man out of the evil treasure of the heart bringeth forth that which is evil."

Here I shall, first, inquire what [are] these fruits that the profitable hearers of God's Word bring forth; and then, tell the qualifications whereby they differ from the fruits that hypocrites bring forth. As to the

First Inquiry, I answer in the general that the fruit is a living according to Christ's command and example.4 Or it might have been answered in one word, that it is holy living.5 But we are to judge what holy living is by the rule of it, which is twofold: Christ's precept and example, both exactly agreeing and directing to the same thing. This the Word of Christ aims at, the doctrine of the gospel is in order to this; therefore it is called "the doctrine which is according to godliness" (1 Timothy 6:3), and "the truth which is after godliness" [Titus 1:1], because the design of these truths and doctrines are to bring men to godliness.

The living a life conformed to the command and life of Christ is abundantly insisted upon as being necessary and saving in the Scripture, and the great characteristic of a Christian. As to obeying Christ's commandments, Christ tells us, Matthew 7:24, that whosoever hears his sayings, and doeth them, that he "will liken him to a wise man, that

-- 258 --

built his house upon a rock"; and in Matthew 7:21, "Not every one that saith unto me, Lord, Lord, shall enter into the kingdom of heaven; but he that doeth the will of my Father which is in heaven."

Christ tells us again, John 14:21, "He that hath my commandments and keepeth them, he it is that loveth me." It is spoken with an emphasis, as if this was the great and most distinguishing sign of loving Christ.

Following Christ's example is joined with keeping Christ's commandments as necessary to true godliness and as the character of a godly man. 1 John 2:3–6, "And hereby we know that we know him, if we keep his commandments. He that saith, I know him, and keepeth not his commandments, is a liar, and the truth is not in him. But whoso keepeth his word, in him verily is the love of God perfected. He that saith he abideth in him ought also to walk as he walked." That is, if he pretends that he is in Christ and don't walk as he walked, his pretense is vain.

But the good fruits that believers bring forth are either internal or external:6

1. Internal, which are the inward exercises of grace. There is a principle of spiritual life infused into the soul in conversion that exerts itself in holy acts and exercises. We are told what those fruits are, Galatians 5:22–23, "But the fruit of the Spirit is love, joy, peace, long-suffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance: against such there is no law."

The Word of God makes those that understand it to be of holy dispositions and affections, to have holy desires and motions of heart that are acceptable unto God: the exercises of love to God and longing desires after him, the exercise of delight in Jesus Christ, the exercises of submission to the will of God, thankfulness for mercies, the exercise of a penitent spirit in mourning for sin and humbling himself before God for it, longing desires after holiness and after more communion with God, the exercises of faith, of dependence upon God and a fiducial committing ourselves to him, that inward worship and devotion that a pious soul often exercises. Such fruits as these, though appearing in no external action, are yet precious fruits of righteousness and such as will be rewarded.

2. There are external fruits. These are those that are more especially called good works, which are often spoken of as a sign of the truth of

-- 259 --

grace. True faith is that "which works by love" [Galatians 5:6], and faith without works "is dead, being alone" [James 2:17]. A true faith renders a person of an excellent spirit. So it makes them of an excellent conversation. It gives them a spirit of love and charity that will be exerting itself in such kind of fruits as we read of, 1 Corinthians 13:4–7, "Charity suffereth long, and is kind; charity envieth not; vaunteth not itself, […] doth not behave itself unseemly, seeketh not her own, is not easily provoked, thinketh no evil; rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth; beareth all things, believeth all things, hopeth all things, endureth all things." Psalms 37:21, "The righteous showeth mercy, and giveth"; and [Psalms 37:26, "He is ever merciful, and lendeth." James 3:17, "The wisdom which is from above is […] full of mercy and good fruits."

It gives a meek and patient spirit. Galatians 5:22, "The fruit of the Spirit is gentleness." James 3:17, "The wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceable."

It makes to be of a humble spirit and conversation. It makes them like little children. They are poor in spirit. Isaiah 57:15, "I dwell with him that is of a humble and contrite spirit," and trembles at my word.

It purifies the heart and life and makes those that have it to keep themselves unspotted from the world, to crucify the flesh with the affections and lusts, cut off offending right hands and pluck out right eyes, to deny all ungodliness and worldly lusts, and to live soberly and righteously and godly in the world. It makes them sell all for Christ, to forsake father, and mother, and wife, and children, and house and lands for Christ's sake and the gospel.

It makes [the] heavenly-minded wean the heart from sublunary things, to spend the life in laying up treasure in heaven, and to have their "conversation in heaven" (Philippians 3:20). Such as these are the genuine natural fruits of the Word of God, that distinguish those whose hearts are as good ground to the seed of the Word from all others.

[Second Inq.] We proposed nextly to consider what these qualities of the obedience of believers are, wherein it differs from the seeming obedience of hypocrites.

There are many hypocrites that are of a very fair outside. They do many things that are materially good, are very orderly and regular as to anything that appears to the world, and are in many things conscientious. Now the question is, wherein the one differs from the other {in qualities of obedience}. There are these three things that the Scripture

-- 260 --

teaches us are the difference of the obedience of one from the other: sincerity, universality and perseverance.

1. The obedience of the godly is sincere; that is, 'tis performed with spirit of obedience. 'Tis obedience of heart as well as hands. Sincerity is when the outward actions do flow from those inward principles and dispositions which they seem to flow from: that when [they] show outward respect to God by worshipping of him or by appearing zealous for his honor, this should be from real inward respect to God; that when men thank God for mercies, it should be from a spirit of thankfulness; that when they do acts of charity, it should indeed be from inward charity and love. If the duty ben't performed inwardly by the heart, as well as outwardly by the hands, 'tis hypocrisy. But evangelical obedience, the true fruit of the Word of God, is performed from a gracious respect to God: as there is outward religion, so it flows from inward religion; truly good works don't come merely from self-love, but there is another higher principle: there is a superior respect that governs.

Therefore false Christians and mere pretenders to religion, whose religion consists only in what is external, are called hypocrites. And the contrary to them are called perfect and upright; what they do, they do with a perfect heart. We read that Amaziah "did that which was right in the sight of the Lord, but not with a perfect heart" (2 Chronicles 25:2).

2. The obedience of the truly godly is universal. They keep all God's commandments. They don't choose out [those] that are more easy and leave others that have more self-denial in them. David makes that profession, that he had respect to all God's commandments [Psalms 119:6]. If a man lives in any one sin, he is looked upon in Scripture account as a worker of iniquity. If a man be a worker of one iniquity, 'tis enough to denominate him a worker of iniquity; and he that keeps the whole law and offends in one point, he is guilty of all. The reason of the case plainly shows that if a man don't fear God in any one point, then7 he don't fear him at all: for the same God that said, "Don't commit adultery, said also, Don't [kill]" (James 2:10).

If all the rest of the members of our body be sound, yet if we have an eye that offends, that is corrupt, if we don't pluck it out, it will certainly carry us to hell. The Apostle tells us that those that are in Christ don't sin; and that he that "sinneth hath not seen him, nor known him"; and that he that "committeth sin is of the devil"; and that he that is born of

-- 261 --

God cannot sin, "because he is born of God" (1 John 3:6–9). It must at least be understood that he cannot live in any one sin.

3. The obedience of the godly is a persevering obedience. The obedience of hypocrites is not so: it is without root and foundation, and so fades in time of temptation; it vanishes and disappears forever. Their goodness is as the morning cloud, and as the early dew that passeth way. The seed of the Word may flourish for a while, but it will die before any fruit is brought to perfection. The stony-ground hearers that received the Word with joy, yet because they had no depth of earth, the seed took no deep root. And so, when the sun rose with a burning heat, it withered away; when affliction and persecution came, they were offended; in time of temptation, they fell away. And so the thorny-ground hearers: the seed sprung up and grew finely for a while, but at length the thorns sprang up and choked it. The cares of this world and deceitfulness of riches drew them away and made them forget the business of religion. But we read, Luke 8:15, that "the good ground are they" that receive the word into "an honest and good heart," and "keep it, and bring forth fruit with patience"; that is, with perseverance, as the word is often used. They endure temptation and continue keeping the commandments of God through all difficulties and trials; they never depart from their integrity.

This is often given as a distinguishing character of the truly godly, that they persevere and never forsake God. They hold out unto the end. Matthew 24:13, "He that endureth to the end shall be saved." Hebrews 10:36, Hebrews 10:38–39, "For ye have need of patience, that, after ye have done the will of God, ye might receive the promise. […] Now the just shall live by faith: but if any man draw back, my soul shall have no pleasure in him. But we are not of them who draw back unto perdition; but of them that believe to the saving of the soul." Hebrews 3:6, "But Christ as a son over his own house; whose house are we, if we hold fast the confidence and the rejoicing of hope to the end." But the time would fail to mention all the places wherein patience and perseverance in a way of obedience are mentioned as distinguishing marks of true godliness.

These three seem to be the principal marks the Scriptures gives us to distinguish the true fruits which the godly bring forth from the false, superficial, external, unsound, unripe and fading, withering fruits of hypocrites.

[Application.]

I shall make but one use of this doctrine, viz.:

-- 262 --

If it be so, that [that] which distinguishes the profitable hearers of God's Word from all others is that they understand it and bring forth the fruit of it, then don't let us separate those in our trial of ourselves. It is a matter of the last concern to us to know what we are and what our condition is; and therefore 'tis of greatest importance to have a good rule to try ourselves by, lest we confirm ourselves in a wrong state and rest more and more securely upon a false foundation.

Let us therefore take heed that in our examining of ourselves we don't separate understanding the Word of God and bringing forth the fruit of it. Because God has always taken care to join them together, it will therefore be a most daring and dangerous presumption for us to separate them.

If we would know whether we are converted or no, we ought strictly to examine ourselves, whether ever we have had the understanding of the Word of God, whether we have understood the main and principal things contained in his Word. Has God ever opened our eyes to behold wondrous things out of his law? Have we ever known what that glorious excellency of God is, that is so much spoken of in the Word of God? Have we seen a majesty and beauty in him, whereby he has appeared to us the greatest good? Have we ever seen the excellency of a Savior, and the sufficiency and gloriousness of the way of salvation, that are the great subjects of the gospel? Have we ever seen what foundation of safety of rest and of happiness there is in Christ Jesus? Have we ever seen the excellency of holiness and the great evil of sin? Have we ever tasted and seen that the Lord is gracious? Let us inquire whether ever we have been convinced of the truth. If we have indeed found these things, they are sure signs of regeneration; but however, if we do think we have understood these things, don't let us rest in that. If we think we have had discoveries of spiritual things, we must not be contented without asking more questions still. We must also examine ourselves whether we bring forth fruit. If we don't, 'tis a certain sign that our supposed discoveries are false; they are but imaginations, superficial glances that have not changed our nature. Therefore it must be inquired whether we live a holy life, whether we keep Christ's commands and follow Christ's example. Are we made of a holy and Christian temper? Are we humble and meek and patient? Have we a spirit of charity? Does faith work by love? If we have faith that we can move mountains, or have knowledge to understand all mysteries, without charity we are nothing [1 Corinthians 13:2.] Are we heavenly minded? Is our heart upon things that ben't seen? Is our obedience sincere? Do we

-- 263 --

perform God's commands? Do we obey from the heart? Do we worship with our hearts? Do we acts of charity with our hearts? Have we respect to all God's commands? Is there no way of wickedness that we continue, no wicked way of acting, no wicked way of speaking? And is there no wicked way of thinking that we allow ourselves in? Is our obedience a persevering obedience, or do we, when temptation comes, cast off God? Does the fear of God at such times fly away?

If we think we do live according to God's commands, don't let us content ourselves only with that, but let us see to it that we have also had a discovery of God's glory, and the excellency of Christ, and the truth of the gospel, etc. Let us join both together in our self-examinations: that is the safest way, and is the way that God has directed us to.

[Doctrine II.]

II. The knowledge and understanding of the Word of God is the root and foundation of fruitfulness.

I draw it from the text. Thus we are told in the parable how the seed that fell by the wayside was devoured by fowls because it did not sink into the ground, took no root at all; which is explained of those that hear the Word and don't understand it. That is meant by its not taking root. But the seed in the good ground did take root, and not only so, but also bear fruit; which is explained that they both understood it and also bore fruit. So that the spiritual understanding of it seems to answer to the root that the seed took, and good works the fruit. Which teaches us that the right understanding and believing of God's Word is that upon which true fruitfulness depends as its foundation.

God makes a discovery of his own excellency, and shows the glory of the Mediator, and convinces of the truth of those great and wonderful things that are declared in his Word, and that changes the nature, gives a new heart and a right spirit, and inclines the soul to obedience, to walk in God's ways and do his commandments.

I shall give these two reasons of the doctrine:

First Reason. Man can't act as a reasonable creature any other way than as he acts from understanding and conviction. God has given us a reasonable nature for this end principally, that it might be exercised and improved in spiritual things. The service of God is what we are created for, and our reason is given us to be improved for that end. Therefore when man acts spiritually, he acts rationally, according to the reasonable nature. He acts as a reasonable creature in obeying of

-- 264 --

God's commandments and performing the duties of religion. But this can't be, except the foundation of obedience and a holy life be the conviction of the understanding.

How can a man, acting as a reasonable creature, choose God for this highest happiness, except he be first convinced that there is happiness to be found in God? How can he mortify his lusts and corruption and deny himself except he first sees the need there is of it, and that there is something better than these enjoyments? How can he rationally follow after charity and meekness, and wean his heart from this world, and set his affections upon things above and not upon things below, unless he first sees reason why he should do thus? The excellency and worthiness and truth of spiritual things must be seen before they can be rationally devoted to and pursued.

Second Reason. Because otherwise these fruits could not be the fruits of the Word of God. The fruits of a holy life ben't only the fruits of the Christian, but they are the fruits of the Word. It is so represented in the parable. 'Tis the fruit that proceeds from the seed as well as from the ground that bears it; but the seed is the Word. But it can't be the fruit of the Word any other way than as it enlightens the understanding. The Word is God's revelation, his teachings. Now teachings or revelations can have no other entrance into the mind than by the understanding. It is impossible that instructions should influence the mind of the instructed any other way than only as they are understood.

[Application.]

The Use of the doctrine is to exhort all that desire ever to be savingly profited by the Word to get the understanding of it. With all your gettings, get this understanding. If you can but once attain to it to understand God's Word, you'll have saving benefit by it as soon as ever you know the meaning of it and believe it will have irresistible power upon your heart. In order to this, follow these directions:

First Dir. You must be brought to see your own ignorance. He that is ignorant and thinks himself wise is not like to be delivered from his ignorance. The first step towards knowledge is the knowing our own ignorance. 1 Corinthians 3:18, "Let no man deceive himself. If any man among you seemeth to be wise in this world, he must become a fool, that he may be wise." There are many men of great temporal knowledge that yet are wholly blind in spiritual matters, in matters of the

-- 265 --

greatest concern, but yet are puffed up with their knowledge, can't bear to own their blindness or to throw up all their knowledge that they are proud of, and own that it is vain and worthless in comparison of that knowledge which the Holy Ghost teaches. They can hardly bear to submit their reason to divine revelation.

But if ever we 'twould be taught of God, we must not be wise in our own eyes nor lean to our own understanding. Isaiah 5:21, "Woe to them that are wise in their own eyes, and prudent in their own sight!" Proverbs 3:5, "Trust in the Lord with all thy heart; and lean not to thy own understanding." Therefore if we would not become fools by professing ourselves wise, we must cast away all carnal reasonings and objections against those doctrines which God has revealed to us and subject our understanding to those mysteries which are beyond them.

Second Dir. If you would get spiritual knowledge and understanding, yet that knowledge of the principles of religion that is within your power, a notional knowledge of divine things, must go before a spiritual; otherwise, the heathen that never heard of the gospel might be converted as well as the most knowing Christians. It is not possible that a person should understand the glory of God as revealed in his Word except he understands what that revelation is and how he has revealed it there. A person can't understand the excellency of the way of salvation except he knows what that way of salvation is.

The more clear and full notional knowledge we have of the doctrines of religion, we shall be under so much the better advantages for conversion: for this is the way wherein God reveals spiritual knowledge. Otherwise, the Word of God would signify nothing. There are many that are concerned about their souls, that, among other means, it is to be feared, they too much neglect this of getting more knowledge and understanding of the principles of religion. And it is to be feared that many parents are negligent of their children's souls in this regard, in not well-instructing of them in the principles of religion, resting only in teaching them just to say their catechist by rote, without understanding anything about what they say.

Third Dir. Make much use of the Word of God. That is God's own teachings and instructions, and it is there only that we have the knowledge of things that pertain to our salvation originally taught. 'Tis there only that we have an infallible guide, a sure rule, which, if we follow, we cannot err. Let us therefore be often in reading the Scriptures, for they are able to make us wise to salvation. Let [us] diligently attend to the preaching of the Word. 'Tis the Word of God that is the seed that is

-- 266 --

sown, and 'tis the understanding of that is the thing that we are directing you [to] obtain; and certainly if we would understand it, 'tis our way to be much conversant with it. How can we ever hope that we shall understand the Word, if we won't so much as read it, nor attend to it when it is preached to us?

Fourth Dir. If we would understand the Word of God, we must often go to him whose Word it is and beg of him to teach. He is the fountain of both Word and Spirit, and he has reserved to himself this work of enlightening the mind with spiritual knowledge, and there is no other can do it; there is none teaches like God. Let us go to him, that he would give us eyes to see and ears to hear and hearts to understand, that he would open our eyes that we may behold wondrous things out of his law. Let us cry for wisdom and lift up our voice for understanding: "For the Lord giveth wisdom, and out of his mouth cometh knowledge and understanding" [Proverbs 2:6].8

[Doctrine III.]

III. There is great difference of degrees of grace and fruitfulness amongst the godly.

We have shown how the godly differ from the ungodly. They differ specifically and essentially. And even the godly themselves ben't without their difference one from another, but 'tis only a gradual difference. They all agree in essentials and are all exceedingly and essentially distinguished from the ungodly. They differ from the ungodly as the living differ from the dead. They differ one from another as the living differ among themselves in degrees of health and strength and stature.

Here, before we proceed to the improvement, we shall consider particularly, [first,] wherein the difference does consist; and second, the foundation and causes; and third, the end of it.

First. We are to show wherein this difference does consist. And,

1. I would premise, negatively, that there are no degrees of imputed righteousness, but that all saints are alike justified in the sight of God by the righteousness of Christ. As there are no degrees in the same person with respect to this, but he is as much justified the first moment of his conversion as ever he is, how much soever he may increase in holiness afterwards; so neither is there any difference in this respect in

-- 267 --

different persons. The weakest saint is as much justified in the sight of God as the strongest. He that has but a spark of grace in his heart, the lowest degree of the sanctifying spirit, has his sins as much pardoned, and Christ's satisfaction and righteousness as much imputed to him, as Moses or Elijah or the apostle Paul had, yea, as much as the saints of heaven have. 'Tis very evident because all the sins of every believer, as soon as ever he believes, are pardoned; and if they are all pardoned and blotted out, cast into the depths of the sea, so that they shall be remembered no more, then there can be no degrees of pardon. If sins are so pardoned that God's anger is all ceased, they can't be more pardoned. Christ's death has fully satisfied for the sins of all believers, is of as much virtue to satisfy for the sins of one as of another. So his righteousness is wrought out for one saint as much as for another. 'Tis the same perfect righteousness is imputed to everyone, and if it be really imputed to all, there is as much as it can be; there can be no degrees of imputation of the same thing. If it be one covenant by which they have their righteousness, then their righteousness must be the same.

[2.] But the difference consists in these two things:

(1) In the degrees of inherent righteousness, in the measure of the Spirit of God that is given to everyone. God gave Jesus Christ the Spirit without measure, but he gives the saints his Spirit by measure and in different proportion. It immediately appears in the proportion of light in the understanding, in the proportion of strength of faith and entireness of dependence on God; in the degree of holy disposition and inclination of soul; in the degree of humility and sense of our own unworthiness; in the degree of spirituality and affections, the degree of heavenliness of the disposition, whereby it is weaned from the world and looks at things which are not seen.

There is a great variety of degrees with respect to these things. Although grace be in a very imperfect state in all in this world, yet some are like babes, others are as grown men: some have made greater progress in their spiritual warfare and have got more of a victory over their corruptions; others, their grace is so weak and corruptions so strong, that they are carnal men in comparison of others. 1 Corinthians 3:1–3, "And I, brethren, could not speak unto you as unto spiritual, but as unto carnal, even as unto babes in Christ. I have fed you with milk, and not with meat: for hitherto ye were not able to bear it, neither yet are ye able. For ye are yet carnal: whereas there is among you envy, and strife, and divisions, are ye not carnal, and walk as men?" And Hebrews 5:12–14,

-- 268 --

"For when for the time ye ought to be teachers, ye have need that one teach you again which be the first principles of the oracles of God; and are become such as have need of milk, and not of strong meat. For every one that useth milk is unskillful in the word of righteousness: for he is a babe. But strong meat belongeth unto them that are of full age, even those who by reason of use have their senses exercised to discern both good and evil."

In9 some, grace is as fire that only causes a smoke, and such as are weak are compared to the smoking flax. In others, it flames out.

Some, though they have faith, yet [it] is so weak that temptations do more easily stagger them. Others are more confirmed and settled. Some have such ardent charity, that their hearts and affections are continually going out after God and Christ. Others are taken up about the world, full of anxious cares and concerns about earthly things.

(2) There is great difference as to the good works that they do. Some do abundantly more for the glory of God in the world and promoting the kingdom and interest of Christ and the good of their fellow creatures than others. This is very much in proportion to the degrees of inherent holiness and love to God. Some Christians that have much love are abundantly more concerned for the interest of religion and for the honor of God and the good of souls than others. They are more careful and tender of these things. They are more studious how to promote them and more constant and laborious in it. They are more affected with joy when they hear of anything done for God's honor and are more hurt for those things that are contrary. Some have more of a spirit of charity towards men, and they abound more in labors of love. These things are in proportion to the strength of grace in the heart, other things being equal.

But1 good works are not always in proportion to the degree of internal holiness, because other things are not always equal. One saint that has not more grace may be favored with much greater opportunities and advantages to do good than another. He may have more occasion in providence of exerting grace in this way, in not only desiring to do good, but actually doing what shall be for God's glory in the world. It is a great mercy to have such opportunities and advantages, because there is no good work done in sincerity shall in any wise lose its reward.

-- 269 --

Second. As to the foundation of this difference, it is no other than God's good pleasure. God distributes unto every man severally as he will. God did not only from all eternity determine who he would give his grace to, but also in what measures and proportion. 'Tis God that makes one man to differ from another. He does not only make the godly to differ from sinners, but he makes the godly to differ among themselves. Ephesians 4:7, "Unto every one of us is given grace according to the measure of the gift of Christ."

The immediate cause of the degrees of grace is the Holy Spirit. 'Tis not only by him that men are converted from sin to holiness, but the degree of holiness is from him also. Whether the difference of degree arises from the different measure given at first conversion, or by a greater growth afterwards, 'tis he that excites diligence and striving after growth in grace and that succeeds it. He will not quench the smoking flax until he hath brought forth judgment unto victory (Isaiah 42:3). 'Tis he that makes his people victorious over their enemies. 'Tis he that begins the good work, and he carries it on. Philippians 1:6, "He that hath begun a good work in you" will carry it on.

If the greater degree of grace be because conversion was earlier, so that the person had a longer opportunity to grow, 'tis God that determines the time of conversion and the time of death. If it be because one person has had greater advantages any way for growth in grace, God gives the advantages and makes them successful. 'Tis God that gives advantages and opportunities to do good works, and stirs up to improve them, and assists in improving of them.

Third. The third and last thing to be spoken to, is the end of these different degrees. And the best account that one can give of God's acts, where he himself has not revealed the reason of them, is that God saw meet. God is infinitely wise, and he has many reasons for his own actions that we have no conception of. Job 33:13, "He giveth not account of any of his matters."

But yet where we have any ground to go upon, there we can discover any of the wise ends of God's dispensations. We can't employ ourselves more excellently than in searching of them out. Proverbs 25:2, "'Tis the glory of God to conceal a thing: but 'tis the honor of kings to search out a matter."

The following reasons may be given why God gives grace to his People in so great a variety of degrees:

1. Thereby God shows that not only conversion but the degree of grace is from his free and sovereign goodness. God shows his sovereignty with respect to conversion by giving his converting grace only to

-- 270 --

an elect chosen number. He has a right to have mercy on whom he will have mercy and to harden whom he will; and he exercises and manifests this right by giving conversion to some only, and those whom he pleases, not confining his grace to wise or unwise, to great or small, rich or poor, moral or vicious. Hereby God manifests the freeness of his grace to be admired and praised of those that are the subjects of it.

So God has also a right, when he does give his grace, to give in what measures he pleases; and God manifests this sovereignty and freeness of his goodness also by the exercise of it. He gives to some a large measure of his Spirit, to others a lesser: perhaps to some that are great and noble and wise but a little degree of grace, but a little communion with himself; and it may be to one that is low and despised he pours forth more abundantly of his Spirit, holds a more frequent and intimate communion with him, unveils his glory and his love more freely and fully. Hereby he shows that he is sovereign as to the degree of grace by exercising of that sovereignty, that his people may praise him, not only that he has converted them, but that he has given them so much grace. God was not obliged to give them any grace at all, so2 he may give as little as he pleases; so that everyone, even the least of saints, should praise God that he has given them so much grace, and those that have much in comparison of others should praise and admire [the] sovereign goodness that distinguished them.

If everyone had grace in an exact equality, they would be ready to think that, if God gave them grace at all, they must have it in such a degree. They would know of no other degree. They would hardly think of praising God for giving so much grace, because none have any less or any more.

2. God has different work for different persons to do, and different places to set them in the church. God gives his people grace to fit them for the work which he designs them for, for all their sufficiency is of God. The saints can't fit themselves for the work which they are called to, but the work which God has intended for them, he will give them grace to enable them to do it.

God sees [it] to be for the beauty of Christ's body, which is the church, that it should be constituted of different members that have a different work and office. See 1 Corinthians 12:17–19, "If the whole body were an eye, where were the hearing? And if the whole were hearing, where were the smelling? But now hath God set the members every

-- 271 --

one of them in the body, as it hath pleased him. And if they were all one member, where were the body?"

Seeing there is a necessity that there should be different kinds of members of a different work, God fits the members for their office as he fits the members of the natural body for their work: he fits the hands for the work that they are to do, and he fits the eyes and ears severally for their work; and this is one reason why God gives different gifts and different graces, as in the seventh verse of the same chapter: "But the manifestation of the Spirit is given to every man to profit withal"; and in the eleventh and twelfth verse, "But all these worketh the same Spirit, distributing to every man severally as he will. For as the body is one, having many members, and all the members of that one body, being many, are one body: so also is Christ."

To the same purpose is that, Ephesians 4:7, "But unto every one of us is given grace according to the measure of the gift of Christ." And we are told the end of it, in the twelfth verse, "For the perfecting the saints, for the work of the ministry, and for the edifying the body of Christ."

3. God gives some saints much more grace than others, because some are designed for greater sufferings and trials than others, and so they have need of more grace. God gives his people grace according to their necessities. When Paul was exercised with a sore trial and besought God about it, God made answer, "My grace is sufficient for thee: my strength is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9). God has too much love and tender care of his saints to appoint sore trials and dreadful afflictions for them to go through, and not to give them grace sufficient that they may not be overthrown in their trials.

'Tis according to God's covenant that he will give grace to enable his people to stand, so as not to be utterly cast down by trials. Proverbs 10:3, "The Lord will not suffer the soul of the righteous to famish." Psalms 55:22, "The Lord shall never suffer the righteous to be moved." And 1 Corinthians 10:13, "God is faithful, who will not suffer you to be tempted above that you are able."

Some are designed for martyrdom. Thousands God has called to seal to3 the truth of the gospel with their blood, to endure great torments for God and Christ's sake. Such have need of more grace than others, and God's manner is to give such extraordinary grace.

[4.] A fourth reason is that God designs them for different places in glory. As it is for the beauty and health of Christ's church, his mystical

-- 272 --

body on earth, that it should be constituted of different members of different degrees of nobility and honorableness, so God in his infinite wisdom sees it to be most for the beauty and order and glory of the triumphant church of the glorified body of Christ that there be different members of different degrees of honor and happiness. As it is amongst the angels, so it will be amongst the saints. In heaven, we are told, there are many mansions; which can hardly be understood any other way than that there are many different degrees of glory and blessedness.

Now God gives different degrees of grace to fit for these different degrees of glory. God sees cause to give the higher degrees of glory as a reward of his grace and fruitfulness in this world. Every man will be rewarded according to his works.

Application.

The Use is of Exhortation, to exhort to strive to grow in grace and in good fruits, and to arrive to a great degree of it. The thing exhorted to carries motive enough in itself, much more than if you were exhorted to grow in riches or to grow in any temporal good qualification and enjoyment. To have a high degree of grace is the same as to arrive at a high degree of excellency and a high degree of happiness. If grace be a thing most worthy to be sought by those that have it not, for the same reason an increase of grace is most worthy to be sought by those that have it.

Grace is the greatest excellency of man, and the least degree of it is more worthy than all the silver and gold and jewels in the world; and if so, then a great degree of it is most desirable.

He4 that has much grace, has much of the divine likeness. He has much of God's beauty imparted to him; he has much of the Holy Ghost dwelling in his heart; he has much of God: and what can be a greater and nobler and more desirable thing?

And as it is most desirable to have much of the habit of grace, so [it is] to bring forth much of the fruits of grace. As the fruit of a tree is the most excellent part, so is the fruit of a Christian. 'Tis the greatest honor and excellency of a Christian to be abundant in bearing fruit, as it is the greatest dishonor to be barren or to bear but little fruit.

-- 273 --

The abounding of grace in the heart will cause an answerable abounding of good works. It tends as much to it as the goodness of ground and good tillage tends to its fruitfulness.

But here the following things may be particularly considered:

First. Consider that it is a certain sign you have no grace, if you don't desire and strive to grow in grace and fruitfulness. 'Tis self-evident that he that loves holiness and is sensible that [it] is above all things precious and desirable, must desire much of it and not be contented with a very imperfect degree of it; and that he that truly hates sin as the most loathsome thing in the world, as long as he sees any of it in his heart, he must desire and strive to get rid of it.

So that if you are a Christian, you are already sensible that a great degree of grace is a thing most worthy earnestly to be sought after, and you do hunger and thirst after righteousness. You are longing for more love to God, for a more unmoved faith in Jesus Christ, for more communion with God, for more of a Christian spirit and temper, for more of a spirit of charity and a spirit of humility and thankfulness.

And you are longing to bring forth more fruit, to walk more holily, to live more to God's honor and well-pleasing, to do more good in the world. This is inseparable from sincerity.

Second. Consider that if you have weak grace and bring forth but little fruit, you are like to have so much the less comfort.5 Grace is of a joyful nature; 'tis not only the beauty but the happiness of the soul. 'Tis the only true spring of comfort, and only the mere exercise of grace naturally excites sweetness and pleasure in the mind. 'Tis the health of the mind, 'tis the proper and natural disposedness and subordination and exercise of the faculties. They that have much knowledge of God and acquaintance with him will have much comfort, for the sight of God's glory and excellency necessarily raises pleasure, and pleasure of the highest kind. They that see most of the divine beauty of God and Christ will live the pleasantest lives.

They that have much grace will be likely to have much communion with God, and so consequently will have much joy. Such have the most love to God. Love is a grace that is so much of the very same nature as Joy, that it almost falls in with it: it is called love of complacency; complacency is pleasure and comfort: faith necessarily carries comfort in the nature of it, a dependence upon the sufficiency and grace of God

-- 274 --

for all good. Hope also includes comfort. They therefore that have much of these are likely to have the most comfort.

Those that are weak in grace are liable to much darkness and to a great many doubts and scruples about their condition. If grace be small, it will be difficultly discerned, and they will be often questioning whether there be any at all or no, and corruptions will be more frequently and more strongly working, and that will cause doubts whether they ben't still under the power and dominion of sin.

The darkness and troubles and fears that many meet with are from the weakness of their grace and because they bring forth no more fruit. They spend their lives in afflicting, distressing fears and doubts They han't much of the light of God's countenance because they bear but little fruit. Their corruptions are more prevalent and the devil has a greater advantage against them.

Therefore if you respect the comfort of your lives, labor to your utmost to get strength of grace. You will be abundantly paid for your pains in the increase of comfort and pleasure in this world. Peace of conscience, and a true sense of the love of God, and a good hope of glory, are things worth taking pains for.

Third. If you are weak in grace, you will be the more liable to fall into sin. Indeed, 'tis God that preserves the strongest as well as the weakest. The strong are no more self-sufficient than the weak, but God preserves them by his Spirit; and they that have more grace, have more of God's Spirit dwelling in them, and therefore have a better preservative from sin.6

When God's people fall into sin, besides that it is a great dishonor to God and to the Christian religion, much greater than if another committed the same sin: I say, besides this, it sometimes brings a train of calamities and mischiefs after it. As it was with David, or has been with many others, it wounds their souls, wounds their internal peace and wounds their outward [peace], and brings God's judgments and many sore inward and outward afflictions, that they oftentimes don't get over as long as they live. Therefore upon this account, 'tis well worth the while to labor to be strong in grace.

Fourth. If you are strong in grace, you will be the more likely to have firm peace and comfort upon a deathbed. To have firm peace and quietness then is a matter of exceeding great consideration. 'Tis dreadful to lie sick, expecting death, and have death gradually creeping

-- 275 --

on one, and not to have a firm peace of mind, whatever the state is to be after death. 'Tis not everyone that is a godly person, that when he comes to die has such a peace.

And 'tis worth the while to take pains all one's lifetime, if it were only to lay a good foundation of quietness and comfort against that time. But those that are diligent and laborious to grow strong in grace are in the right way to obtain it.

Be therefore persuaded to think of your death and to consult your comfort [against] that time, and labor to get strength of grace. How will you rejoice, when the time comes, if you have hearkened to this exhortation! O how happy will it be for you, if you not only have grace at that time, but have also the light of God's countenance and are enabled to rejoice in hope of the glory of God!

Fifth.7 And lastly, consider that the more gracious and fruitful you are here, the more glory will you have hereafter; so that they that abound in grace and good works, they will have infinite benefit by it. It must be so that glory will be greater or less in some proportion to grace and good works, or else persons would have no eternal benefit at all by being more in good works than others. He that spends and is spent in laboring for the glory of God, would be no way the better for it in another world. He would get nothing by it, because he would have no greater reward than those that were comparatively but little in good works; which is directly contrary to the Scripture, which tells us that if we give a cup of cold water to a disciple, "he shall in no case lose his reward" (Matthew 10:42). And the reward for good works is not only in this life but in heaven. Christ tells his disciples that their reward is great in heaven (Matthew 5:12), and that the reward shall be in the world to come (Matthew 19:29), and that they [shall] be rewarded "at the resurrection of the just" (Luke 14:14). And the Apostle tells us if we sow sparingly we shall reap sparingly, and if we sow bountifully we shall also reap bountifully (2 Corinthians 9:6).

Now what greater motive could God afford you to strive to abound in grace and good works, than that you shall be the happier and more glorious to all eternity? Every saint shall shine as a star, but as one star differs from another in glory, so will the saints in the resurrection; and then those that have been abundant in good works shall "shine as the brightness of the firmament" (Daniel 12:3). You are ready enough to labor and strive, that you [may] have an increase of wealth and comfort

-- 276 --

in this world: how much more worth the while is it to strive to have increase of glory forever? The least addition to glory is more worth striving for than a earthly kingdom, because 'tis endless and must be greater than any temporary good.

I shall now conclude by giving some directions for those that desire to have much grace.8

First Dir. If you ever would be much in grace, you must begin early. If you begin late, you will have so much the less time to increase in grace. Grace is of a growing nature; it will continue to grow in the general as long as life lasts, but if it be begun towards the latter end of life, there will be but little time for it to grow. God can give abundance of grace in a moment, but yet those that begin early are under much the greatest advantages to abound, and generally are the most eminent.

Those that begin late won't have time to serve God so much, nor to bring forth so much fruit, to [bring forth] so many good works to God's honor and to their own eternal benefit.

Early religion is most acceptable to God, and he has made many special promises to it, and he generally rewards such by giving of them more plentifully of his Spirit and holding a more free communion with them.

Those that are converted late are under disadvantages. They will have sin to fight against that has been greatly strengthened by long custom. And those that begin early will have every way the advantage of others with respect to growing in grace.

Second Dir.9 If you would be strong in grace and abundant fruitfulness, you must often be comparing your heart and life with the rule. There must be a continual watching over your own heart every now and then, examining and searching to see if you can't find some wicked way in you. Try your heart: see if you can't find some instances wherein it is unchristian and contrary to the rule of God's Word; this must be done especially before a sacrament: there should be a set and solemn examination of this nature at such a time, according to that rule, 1 Corinthians 11:28, "Let a man examine himself, and so let him eat." This he must do lest he eat and drink judgment to himself.

But this should not be the only time. It should be done frequently. If it be done every day, it is not too often. We should be continually doing

-- 277 --

as David, "thinking on our ways, and turning our feet into God's testimonies" [Psalms 119:59].

Third Dir.1 Be most intent in fighting against that sin that most easily besets you. There are some sins that men are more especially apt to fall into, and is more easily overcome of. One, it may be, is most apt to fall into intemperance; another into unclean thoughts and desires; another into passionate anger; another into evil speaking. But wherever it be, let that sin be more especially opposed and resisted by you: fight neither with small nor with great, but against that sin that most easily besets you; aim especially at the life of that sin. This is the direction the Apostle gives, Hebrews 12 at the beginning: "Wherefore […] laying aside every weight, and the sin that doth so easily beset us, let us run with patience the race that is set before us."

Fourth Dir. Be most intent upon increasing the foundation-graces, such as the knowledge of God, the understanding of his Word, believing the truth of the gospel, a realizing sense of a future state of rewards and punishments. Labor to get your heart all enflamed with love to God and abased by humility and a sense of your own unworthiness. These are the foundation-graces, and as these are increased, all other graces will most certainly increase proportionably.

And all these things you must do with the utmost diligence and application of mind. The work of a Christian is compared to running, and wrestling, and fighting, and those exercises which require the greatest labor. You must press forward "towards the mark for the prize of the high calling" (Philippians 3:14). And in all, you must go forth in God's strength.
