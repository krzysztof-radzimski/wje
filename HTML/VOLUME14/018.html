The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost

As the title suggests, The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost is an exploration of the various offices of the third person of the Trinity, though the second person and the nature of justifying faith also receive extended attention. Coming in the midst of a very productive period, this major and lengthy effort reflects surrounding sermons such as None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness and The Sacrifice of Christ Acceptable and in many ways is a gathering of Edwards' theological and pastoral concerns.

We know that the nature of the Trinity preoccupied Edwards during the first half of 1729 because this discourse is as detailed an account of the relationships between the persons of the Trinity as we can find in Edwards' writings to this point in his life. It anticipates his Essay on the Trinity, begun in early 1730. The content of this discourse shows that Edwards was a close student of the debate over the Trinity that had been engaging European theologians since the end of the seventeenth century.1 In response to anti-trinitarian views of the subordinate roles of the second and third persons, Edwards affirms the eternal and equal cooperation of the Son and Holy Ghost with the Father. The idea for this discourse, which at seven preaching units is longer than the Essay in its original form, had been germinating in Edwards' mind since he laid out the basic tripartite structure of it in "Notes on the Scripture" no. 134, written in late 1728. In that entry, he explains that the three offices of the Holy Ghost, paralleling John 16:8, the text of this discourse, are to convince the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment: making the world aware of the necessity of receiving Christ, the sufficiency of his righteousness, and his power as a judge and deliverer.

Edwards probably completed writing and began delivering The Threefold Work in April 1729 before his trip to New Haven in the following month, and he may even have had a preaching unit or two to deliver when he returned. The discourse is divided into three propositions,

-- 372 --

each followed by an application, with a "General Improvement" at the end. It begins with a demonstration of how the Holy Ghost "confers the benefit" of Christ's salvation by making individuals "partakers" of Christ's righteousness. Through an "eternal consultation and agreement," the Trinity set in motion the covenant of redemption. In this covenant, the Holy Ghost performs his offices not as an inferior member of the Trinity, but as an equal of the Father and Son. The Holy Ghost is Christ's messenger, but this for Edwards does not constitute subordination. As he observed several months earlier in "Miscellanies" no. 376, that fellowship with the Father and Son "consists in our communion of the Holy Ghost with them"; so in this sermon Edwards portrays the Holy Ghost as the convincer of sin, influencing the hearts of believers as an "indwelling principle."

If Edwards concentrates on the Trinity in the doctrinal sections of this discourse, in the applications he turns to identification of the signs of grace as marks of the respective works of the Holy Ghost in believers' hearts. Reflecting his collation of Scripture texts in his "Signs of Godliness" notebook, in the application to the first sermon Edwards begins to draw very fine distinctions between the "certain signs" of grace and mere delusions. Having "fears and concerns," or being affected by a certain passage of Scripture or by preaching, does not necessarily mean that such affections come from God, for Satan is also active when God's Spirit is present. As a general rule, Edwards tells his people that when convictions are "agreeable to truth, and make men more careful to avoid sin and to use the means of God's appointments for salvation, there need be no scruples."

The second office of the Holy Ghost is to convince the world of righteousness, or to make believers partakers of Christ's righteousness by applying Christ's redemptive sacrifice. The third preaching unit establishes a distinction between Christ's negative (or passive) and his positive (or active) righteousness offered in atonement. Here Edwards asserts Christ's "sufficiency" or satisfaction for sin against anti-trinitarians who "deny that Christ's active obedience to God's law is imputed to believers." Allowing for individual righteousness apart from righteousness (or justification) in Christ is for Edwards tantamount to works-righteousness. Therefore he is at pains to establish Christ's divinity and the intimate connection of the Father and Son in the redemptive process. Christ, and Christ alone, "is he in whom we have justification." Christ removes guilt by the negative righteousness achieved through his suffering, but this is not enough to admit others

-- 373 --

into heaven. Drawing on "Miscellanies" no. 381, "Christ's Righteousness," Edwards seeks to demonstrate that "the positive precepts of the law" that Christ obeyed need not have been the same law that was given to Adam. Rather, the important thing was that he perfectly obeyed. By this positive righteousness, he purchased heaven for believers.2

For Edwards, those who do not put their faith wholly in the sufficiency of Christ's righteousness, as applied by the Holy Ghost, are guilty of mocking the eternal plan of redemption that the Trinity devised. Amplifying the theme of None Are Saved by Their Own Righteousness, Edwards points out that an individual who thinks his own righteousness is wholly or partially sufficient perverts the covenant of redemption as "a frivolous notion," and as much as states that the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost "busied themselves about it needlessly," or merely to "surprise and amuse the world." If human righteousness is at all acceptable, then God the Father is guilty of cruelty for making his Son go to his death, God the Son committed "the most egregious folly" for dying to no purpose, and God the Holy Ghost comes "to convince men of lies."

Edwards goes on to explain that Christ's righteousness can be looked upon as belonging to believers because of his union with God and humankind by being united to both in nature and love. But it is the Holy Ghost that gives the conviction of this truth by "convincing of the reality and suitableness of it." The effects of true conviction include a "yielding" and "consenting" of the soul, committing oneself to Christ, and "calmness of mind." In these things, Edwards states, drawing on his "Faith" notebook, consists "saving, justifying faith."

Finally, the Holy Ghost operates to make individuals see the sufficiency and excellency of Christ as ruler and deliverer. As ruler, he gives laws, influences hearts, and judges souls at death. As deliverer, he redeems from Satan, from worldly afflictions, and from death and the grave. The Holy Ghost gives conviction of these attributes of Christ, as well as of his excellency as Lord and king. When a person is convinced of Christ's glory as Lord and king, obedience to God's commands no

-- 374 --

longer comes from "slavishness," or "from fear of his wrath," but rather from a new principle of love and gratitude.

In his "General Improvement of the Whole," Edwards reiterates the importance of the Holy Ghost in the work of redemption. Christ is the judge and deliverer, but without the Spirit to convince of this, the Word is a "dead letter." The people of God therefore have earnestly to seek the Spirit if God's presence is to be among them. In his conclusion, Edwards makes the discourse applicable to the situation of the church: its members have to pray for the Spirit to visit them, as in times past, to have true conviction of sin, righteousness, and judgment.

∗ ∗ ∗

The manuscript is fifty-four duodecimo leaves. Its ink varies from dark to light brown, and fluctuations in ink viscosity indicate that this was not written in one effort, but as needed, over at least a two to three week period. That this was a series of regular sermons rather than something intended for the weekly lecture is confirmed by the existence of applications for each of the three overall portions, as well as the General Improvement at the very end.

The style of the sermon marks a transition for Edwards. Perhaps due to the demanding and lengthy nature of the discourse, he used abbreviations more than ever before. While parts of the discourse are fully written out, others are written in a sometimes terse, sometimes fragmentary manner. Scattered sections amount to nothing more than lists of points contained in mere words or phrases meant to be expounded extemporaneously. The uneven nature of the sermon's composition may reflect Edwards' increasingly poor health, which resulted in a debilitation in June. Taken as a whole, however, this composition suggests that Edwards was also experimenting, not so much with changing the formal structure of the sermon itself, but with his own powers of memory and association, as well as with how to produce sermons more efficiently.

The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost John 16:8 And when he is come, he will reprove the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment.

Christ for some time had been talking with his disciples like one departing, one that was going to take his farewell of them; he had been giving of them his dying counsel and instructions. And, as he takes notice, they were filled with sorrow upon this occasion. They had left their business that they formerly were wont to follow. The fishermen had left their nets at Christ's call, they had left their friends, yea, they had left all to follow Christ, with that belief that he was the Messiah and with that expectation that he would never leave them, but would always be with them, and that when he set up his kingdom, they should be advanced and should be great gainers by their being his followers.

They had not yet right notions of the Messiah's kingdom. They still entertained carnal and childish thoughts about the matter, as appears by the discourse of some of them after he was crucified, particularly of those that were going to Emmaus [Luke 24:13–35]. With these thoughts prevailing, they had now followed Jesus and kept close to him for three or four years, and had suffered without doubt very much in their temporal interest, and had suffered abundance of reproach and contempt from their countrymen, and particularly from the scribes and Pharisees and the chief men of the land.

And they all but Judas, who was now absent, had a true and dear love to their Master. And therefore, now to hear so much of his leaving them, to hear him speaking to 'em like a man departing and taking his leave of them, and giving his last counsels to 'em, surprised them and filled them with sorrow.

Besides this, amongst other things, he had told them of persecutions and such hard things that they were to suffer after he was gone. And particularly in this chapter, [the] second and third verses, "They shall

Christ himself took notice how they seemed to be affected by these things. John 16:6, "But because I have said these things unto you, sorrow hath filled your heart."

And he tells 'em in the verse foregoing the text that it will be better for them than if he stayed with them.3 "Nevertheless I tell you the truth; it is expedient for you that I go away: for if I go not away, the Comforter will not come unto you; but if I depart, I will send him unto you."

In our text, he tells them what the Comforter will do when he comes, what will be his work. He had told them before, and tells them further in the ensuing part of the chapter, what his work would be with respect to them. And his name, "The Comforter," signifies much of it.

In our text, he tells them what his work will be with respect to the world in general. And that consists in three things: reproving the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment. The word that is translated "reprove" is "convince" in the margin, and the marginal interpretation is now most commonly chosen by divines.4 So that we may read the words thus: "He will convince the world of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment."

Doctrine.
The work of the Holy Ghost as Christ's messenger is to convince men of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment.

Before we come particularly to insist upon these several parts of the work of the Holy Ghost, we shall speak to two propositions.

First Prop. 'Tis the Holy Ghost that makes application of Christ's redemption. That is to say, that although Jesus Christ prepares the way for man's salvation by his righteousness and sufferings, yet 'tis the immediate work of the Holy Ghost actually to make men partakers of that salvation; 'tis he that doth the finishing stroke. Whatsoever Christ has done, yet if men were not brought to repentance, faith [and] union

-- 378 --

with God and Christ, all would be to no purpose, and 'tis the work of the Holy Ghost to bring this to pass.

All the three persons are concerned in the salvation of man, as they were in his creation. When man was first created, there was a consultation among the persons of the Trinity. God said, "Come and let us make man in our image, after our likeness" [Genesis 1:26]. So it is in the work of redemption: the Father chooses the Christ, provides, chooses, sends and accepts a savior; the Son is the Savior who satisfies justice, and answers the law and brings redemption for his people; the Holy Ghost immediately confers the benefits of all this, and actually makes the elect partakers of the salvation Christ has wrought.

So that the several persons have their work according to the order of persons. The first things to be done with relation to men's salvation are to determine whether men shall be saved, and who shall be saved, and to contrive a way of salvation and to choose and send and accept a savior, and that is the work of the Father.

The next thing is to do the work of a savior, to purchase salvation, and that is the work of the second person.

The last thing to be done is to make the redeemed actually partakers {of the salvation Christ has wrought}.

The Holy Ghost don't only immediately apply Christ's redemption, but 'tis he that does what is necessary to be done upon men's hearts in order to [it]. What Christ does immediately is not upon men's souls, but for them, which yet their souls would never be the better for, were not something done immediately upon their souls. Whatsoever in the work of redemption is done immediately in or upon men's souls is the work of the Spirit, whether it be actually making them partakers of this redemption, by converting them and uniting them to Christ, and carrying on {grace in their hearts} and making of them perfect in holiness in heaven, and filling them with happiness; but also convincing men of sin, making of them sensible {of their unworthiness}.

Second Prop. What the Holy Spirit doth in order to men's salvation, he doth as Christ's messenger. All that he doth amongst men since the Fall is as applying Christ's redemption, or some way in order to the application of it.

The Holy Ghost dwelt in the hearts of men before his fall, and moved and influenced not through a savior. So the angels…. But since the fall, men are never any way made partakers of the Spirit but it is through a mediator, through the Son of God.

The Holy Ghost is Christ's messenger, not exclusive of the Father. The Father and Son are equally concerned in men's salvation, and he

-- 379 --

that sent the Son to purchase redemption, he also sends the Holy Ghost to make application. And as the Holy Ghost proceeds both from the Father and the Son equally, so he is sent by both into the world to influence the hearts of the children of men to their salvation. Therefore Christ says he will send him from the Father (John 15:26), and that the Father will send the Comforter (John 14:26).5

Reasons

1. Negative. Not because the Holy Ghost is inferior to the Son. Though in the Trinity there is such a thing as prior and latter in order, yet there is no such thing as degrees of dignity or excellency. Though one proceeds from another, yet one is not inferior to another. The Son is not inferior to the Father; he thought it no robbery to be equal with God. It is said, though he proceeded from the Father, the Holy Ghost is in nothing inferior to the Father or the Son, though he proceeds from both [John 15:26].

It no ways argues the Son to be inferior to the Father, that he was sent by him. So neither is it any argument {that the Holy Ghost is inferior}.

The reason is because they all are the same God, and it is therefore impossible there should be any {inferiority}. They all are the same substance, the same divine essence; and therefore whatsoever perfection, dignity or excellency belongs to the divine essence, belongs to every one of them. There are none of the three persons can have any more excellency or perfection or dignity than what belongs to the divine nature; and each one must have so much because each one has the divine nature, each one is the divine nature. Whatsoever power or wisdom or holiness, etc. belongs to the divine nature, still belongs to the same divine nature in whatsoever person it subsists, whether it subsists in the {Father, Son or Holy Ghost}.

2. Positive.

(1) 'Tis agreeable to the order of persons in the Trinity and their manner of subsisting. The Father subsists of himself; he is neither begotten nor proceeds. 'Tis suitable therefore that he should be first in relation to what is done in the work of redemption, that he should be first orderer, provider, etc. The Son proceeds from the Father; 'tis suitable that he should be sent by him. The Holy Ghost proceeds both from the Father and the Son. He proceeds from the Son as well as Father, and so is sent by him as well as by the Father.

Second. We shall show what conviction of sin that is that the Spirit of God is the author of in order to men's salvation.

Third. [The] manner [of it].

Fourth. The means of this conviction of sin.

Fifth. The end of it.

First. 'Tis the work of the Holy Ghost to convince {men of sin in order to their salvation}. So dull and stupid are the souls of men naturally,8 so under the power of that blinding, stupefying thing sin, that even natural conscience is lulled asleep and the natural reason is kept from a free exercise by it, so that they will not be sensible, etc. Experience. Instances in Scripture manifest it. Peter's sermon [Acts 2]. Jailor [Acts 16:27–34].

Inq. Wherein lies the difference in the manner of the Holy Ghost's influencing of sinners in conviction and the saints?

We don't inquire what is the difference between the influence and operation itself {in influencing of sinners}.

By the tenor of the Scripture, it seems that the saints only are those that have the Spirit of Christ. They only are baptized with the Holy Ghost and with fire, that have his Spirit poured upon them; they only that are taught of the Spirit, that are led by the Spirit.

And yet it appears by the same Scripture that the same Spirit of God does some way influence even wicked men. Otherwise, how do we read of their resisting the Holy Ghost? [Acts 7:51]. And Hebrews 6:4, of some that fall away, that yet are made partakers of the Holy Ghost?

Therefore, why can't unbelievers be said to have the Spirit of Christ as well as believers? Why mayn't they be said to be baptized {with the Holy Ghost}? Wherein lies the difference {in the manner of the Holy Ghost's influencing of sinners in conviction and the saints}?

Ans. The Holy Ghost influences the souls of believers as an indwelling principle. But not [that] the Holy Ghost not moves upon the hearts of saints as an external agent, but he takes up his abode in the hearts of believers. They are the temples of the Holy Ghost. 1 Corinthians 3:16, "Know ye not that ye are the temple of God, and that the Spirit of God dwelleth in you?" So we read of Christ dwelling in them. [Doctrine Resumed.]

We have spoken of the first part of his8 work, viz. convincing men of sin. That work of the Holy Ghost is only preparatory to those two that follow: convincing men of righteousness and of judgment. These two latter comprehend those convictions and that saving light that are given in conversion, when the soul is first [brought] out of a state of sin, is brought to Jesus Christ the Savior.

There are indeed saving convictions of sin that are given in true repentance, but it seems that those that respect is had to in this place are those that are given before conversion, by the illustration in the ninth verse: "Of sin, because they believe not on me." Which seems to intend these two things, viz.:

1. That they are convinced of sin, and have a sense of guilt lying upon their souls because they believe not in Christ; whereas if they did believe in [Christ], that guilt, and also the sense of it, would be removed. Those fears and terrors would vanish, and there would be no occasion for 'em. And,

2. They are convinced of their sin of unbelief, which is that which under the gospel brings the most guilt upon the soul, and is peculiarly aggravated and provoking, and what greatly strengthens and aggravates all other sins.

But the conviction of righteousness and judgment that follow are plainly such as are given when that great and wonderful work of conversion is wrought and a saving change made. By "righteousness"

-- 392 --

here is undoubtedly meant the righteousness of God in Christ, or the righteousness of Christ as mediator, or his having answered the law and having done all that needed to be done in order to the reconciliation and acceptance of sinners. This is evident by the illustration of it in the tenth verse, "Of righteousness, because I go to my Father, and ye see me no more," that is, "Because I ascend into heaven, enter into the holy of holies, go to my heavenly Father to sit at his right hand."

This points forth two things:

1. As the exaltation of Christ, or his ascending into heaven and going to the Father, is a principal means whereby believers are made partakers of the benefits of Christ's righteousness, and that fulfillment of the law which he has performed.

Christ ascended into heaven for our sakes, as the high priest went into the holy of holies for the people. Christ, having performed perfect righteousness by his obedience and suffering, is gone to heaven, there to plead what he has done and suffered. He is entered with his own blood, he is gone there to produce as it were his righteousness for us, to make intercession for us and, as he says, "to prepare a place" [John 14:2].9

And then by his going to heaven, he makes us partakers of the benefits of his righteousness another way, and that is he thereby ascends his throne and takes possession of his [inheritance], has all things given into his hands, that he may distribute of the benefits which he by his righteousness has purchased to as many as God has given. For Christ to work out righteousness for us here upon earth only, that were not enough. But it was needful in order to our being the better for it, that he should make presentation of his meritorious sacrifice in heaven for [us] to the Father; and not only but that he should himself, in the exercise of his power given him in his exalted state, confer his righteousness on all that believed on him. For the great God don't see meet to have anything to do in a way of mercy with fallen men but through a mediator; so that the Mediator must not only work out righteousness, but must himself make men partakers of it.

Therefore Christ's going to the Father is here mentioned as the finishing stroke, as what crowns all that he did as our righteousness, and the last thing needful to be done by the Mediator in order to the making his righteousness ours: a crowning thing that renders him a complete object of the faith of believers, the last thing needed in order

-- 393 --

to there being sufficient grounds for the world's being convinced of the sufficiency of his righteousness. Therefore it is he shall convince the world of righteousness, "because I go to my Father."

2. Christ's going to the Father is mentioned as being a principal argument whereby he does convince men of Christ's righteousness, not only as the means of our justification before the Father, but a principal means of convincing of 'em of the sufficiency of Christ's righteousness.

Christ's exaltation, viz. his rising from the dead and his ascending into heaven, is often insisted on in the gospel as being the great evidence that what Christ had done and suffered was sufficient for us. So it is, Romans 8:34, "Who is he that condemneth? It is Christ that died, yea rather, that is risen again, who is even at the right hand of God, who also maketh intercession for us." So it is said, "Christ was manifest in the flesh, justified in the Spirit" (1 Timothy 3:16), i.e. God justified him as mediator, declared by opening his prison doors that what he had done was sufficient. It was the highest demonstration that could be given: for Christ put himself in our stead, and took upon him our sins and became responsible for all. And God by…

Therefore Christ's going to the Father is here mentioned as an argument by which the Holy Ghost convinces the world of righteousness: for the Holy Ghost convinces by arguments; he enlightens the reason, and makes use of the gospel. The Word is the sword in the hand of the Spirit [Ephesians 6:17].

Christ's going to the Father seems here to be mentioned upon another [occasion] by our Lord, that is, to convince the disciples of the necessity [of] his going away. They were filled with sorrow.

By "righteousness" here seems to be meant the way of the justification of sinners by Christ. 'Tis not only meant Christ's active {righteousness}: a great deal more is intended than that. So by the term "righteousness," when it is spoken with respect to the Mediator in Scripture, is almost always thus to be taken. Christ is called "the Lord our righteousness," that is, he is he in whom we have justification. So it is said, in the Lord Jehovah we have righteousness and strength [Isaiah 12:2, Isaiah 26:4], that is, in him we have acceptance with God as righteous. We read, Romans 4:11, that circumcision was the seal of the righteousness of faith, i.e. a seal of the way of justification by faith; and [in the] thirteenth verse, that the promise that Abraham should be heir [of the world] was through the righteousness of faith. So, very evidently, it is to be understood, Romans 3:21–22 and Romans 1:17. It is not to be understood of

-- 394 --

the holiness of God's nature, or the personal holiness or innocence of Christ, but God's way of justifying man.

So we understand the term "righteousness" in our text.

Prop. [II.] 'Tis the work of the Holy Ghost to make men understand the way of sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God through Christ.

This is a more exalted operation of the Spirit of God than {convincing men of sin}. This is by that heavenly and divine light that the Holy Ghost causes to shine into the heart at conversion; this is a primary operation of the Spirit of God when he first enters into the soul, to take up his abode there. This is far more beyond the powers of nature than {conviction of sin}. This is an influence of the Spirit most agreeable to his holy nature. Indeed, conviction of sin was agreeable to the nature of the Holy Ghost, as it is an opposition to sin, which is most opposite to his nature. But this holy conviction is the direct light of the Sun of righteousness, the natural breathings of the Spirit.

Here we shall show,

First. That through Christ Jesus there is a way for sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God.

Second. We shall show what understanding for conviction of this way the Holy Ghost gives.

Third. We shall consider the immediate effects that are thereby produced in the soul.

First. There is through Christ Jesus a way for sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God. Or which is the same thing: there is a way for sinners' justification, or sinners' being looked upon as righteous, received as such and treated as such by God.

'Tis impossible that ever any should stand in a state of acceptance with the pure and infinitely holy God any otherwise than as righteous. Sinners can't be received but as having sin removed, nor can they be received into favor without righteousness. 'Tis not consistent with God's nature, with his holiness and infinite wisdom, and his law, the expression {of his holiness}.

Therefore justification consists in these two things, viz. in removing guilt in the pardon of sin and reconciliation, which is only a negative righteousness and a mere removal of God's anger; and then second, a looking upon the sinner as positively righteous and receiving him as the object of favor, not only as merely not the object of anger.

-- 395 --

Now in order to a sinner's being thus accepted with God, there must he some real righteousness that must be the sinner's. God don't look upon sinners as righteous for nothing, when they have no righteousness properly theirs; he don't look upon them to be or to have what they are or have not. 'Tis not a notion, but a reality.

All that is needed therefore in order to a sinner's reconciliation and acceptance {with God} is a righteousness. And then the righteousness must be perfect, that is, righteousness fully and completely answerable to God's commandments, because one sin brings guilt and a desert of punishment incurs displeasure and hatred. And then it is necessary according to the law, the eternal, unvariable rule {of God}.

Now the question after man had sinned was how the sinner should properly be [a] partaker of perfect righteousness, i.e. how his guilt could be removed so that, consistent with God's nature and with his law, he might notwithstanding be looked upon without any offense or displeasure by God; and then, how a complete, positive righteousness should become properly his, that is, should be so his that, consistent with the same divine nature and rule of righteousness, he might be accepted with the same favor and to the same reward as if he had perfectly, without ever sinning himself, performed.

This righteousness we have through Christ, through him that has found how these things might be done. And therefore here, in order to show how there is a way through Christ, we shall show,

1. What righteousness he has performed as mediator.

2. How his righteousness is properly looked upon as ours.

1. What righteousness {he has performed as mediator}.

The righteousness of Christ which he as mediator performed consists, first, in his negative righteousness, or his removing guilt by suffering; second, his positive righteousness in his perfect obedience.

(1) Christ as our surety had a perfect negative righteousness, or, he fully removed the guilt of all that believe on him by his suffering.

What we call a "negative righteousness" consists in a mere freedom from guilt. Such a righteousness had Adam the first moment he was created, before he had performed one act, either good or bad.

Christ, when he came into the world, though he was perfectly free from any personal guilt, was perfectly free from sin, yet he came into the world, as he was our representative, with guilt upon his soul. He had no sin of his own, no guilt, but he took our sins and bore them; he took our guilt upon himself, that is, he took our obligation to punishment.

-- 396 --

Believers that were the sinners were looked upon as innocent, and he who had no sin was looked upon as guilty; the debt was crossed out that was in the sinner's account and was set on Christ's account.

Christ thus standing bound to suffer, when he had suffered and paid the debt, then he became free from guilt. Our guilt lay no longer upon him, nor upon us, but was utterly buried and abolished. It vanished away; an end was made of it.

Then he who as our surety before was looked upon as guilty [and] obliged, is now looked upon as no longer guilty, and so became negatively righteous, i.e. not guilty. As for guilt, it was now utterly done away by his suffering.

Therefore, Christ is said to be justified. 1 Timothy 3:16, "God manifest in the flesh, justified in the Spirit." But what need had Christ of any justification, that had never sinned, was never guilty? Why, he had this need of it because he voluntarily took on him our guilt and as it were became guilty, became a debtor to justice; and therefore when he paid the debt by suffering, then he was guilty no more, but was justified, and the sentence of justification was declared by his resurrection.

This is part of that righteousness of Christ that is the matter of our justification before God. By the word "righteousness," as it is spoken of with relation to the business of our justification, intends nothing else but an answerableness to the law. Therefore that whereby Christ answered the law is part of his righteousness; but he answered {the law} as well by his sufferings as by his obedience. But this brings [me] to the second part of {Christ's righteousness}:

(2) In the second place, Christ as our surety has a positive righteousness, in that he in the flesh performed perfect obedience to God's law.1

The end of that negative righteousness which Christ procured by his suffering is only to deliver from guilt and from punishment, but gives no title to heaven. But Christ's active righteousness is the price of happiness. Christ's passion delivers from the obligation and leaves the sinner in a state of indifference, but Christ's active righteousness purchases heaven.

There are those that deny that Christ's active obedience to God's law is imputed to believers, or that it is any way available to their justification any otherwise than as a necessary qualification in order to render his sacrifice available.

But 'tis very evident that Christ's active righteousness was necessary

-- 397 --

in order to our justification as well as his passive {righteousness}, by the following things:

1. The law is not answered without it. The law is undoubtedly the rule {of judgment}. 'Tis by the law that sinners are condemned. The law stands against them, and that is a principal reason they can't be saved without a mediator, because one jot nor little shall in no wise pass from the law [Matthew 5:18]. The law is the rule of judgment; 'tis by the law that sinners are convinced of sin and of their necessity of a savior. But how can they be convinced of this by it, if the law is now dispensed with? And if one part of the law may be dispensed with, why may not all?

Now the law don't only require that sinners should be free from guilt. If that was all, there would have been no occasion to make any covenant at all with our first parents: for they were free from guilt the first moment they were created. And if that was all, they would have been fixed in a state of immutable happiness without more ado. But God in wisdom saw it meet that he should not only be free from guilt, but should be required to perform an active righteousness before he should have eternal life confirmed to him. This was the price that God fixed—perfect active obedience to his law—and he never altered that price. If he has, the law is dispensed with, and we need trouble ourselves no more about answering the law.

Christ, by suffering in our stead and removing guilt, only places us in the state Adam was in the first moment {he was created}, without any probation at all. And it is no more fitting that…

But without doubt, Christ came into the world not only to free us from guilt, but to do that work which Adam failed of. This is implied in Christ's being called "the second Adam" (1 Corinthians 15:45).

By the same law that Christ, when he had once put himself in our stead, was obliged to suffer, by the same was he obliged to obey. After he was become our surety, he could not be acquitted till he had suffered, nor can he be rewarded till he has obeyed; and as he is not acquitted as a private person, so neither is he rewarded as a private person. But we are partakers as well of the reward of his obedience, as of his acquittance upon his sufferings: for in both, he appears as our head.

2. If sinners should be saved without the active righteousness of Christ, God's authority would not be sufficiently honored. God insisted upon Adam's active obedience before he would fix him in blessedness, doubtless that Adam, by obeying, might acknowledge and honor

-- 398 --

God's authority. God gave us a law that we might have opportunity to honor God by obeying of it; and God now insists upon satisfaction, that his law and authority may not go without its honor. And it's certain that Christ by his obedience has done much more honor to God's authority than Adam by his obedience could have done; and the end of the law being hereby answered, God is satisfied.

Christ's active obedience was as necessary to retrieve the honor of God's law and authority as his suffering. If the sinner, after his sin was satisfied for, had eternal life bestowed without active righteousness, this would not be honorable to that rule which God had fixed, because now God recedes from it.

Supposing this were possible, that the sinner himself could suffer to pay the debt, and afterwards be in the same state that he was in before his probation: if he now at last should have eternal life bestowed on him without performing that condition of obedience, then God would recede from his law and would give the promised reward, and his law never have respect and honor shown to it in that way of being obeyed. But Christ, by subjecting himself to the law and obeying it, has done a great honor to the law and to the authority of God who gave it. That so glorious a person should become subject to the law, has done it much more honor than if man had obeyed it.

3. We are plainly taught it in Scripture. Romans 5:17–19, "For if by one man's offense death reigned by one; much more they which receive abundance of grace and of the gift of righteousness shall reign in life by one, Jesus Christ. Therefore as by the offense of one judgment came upon all men to condemnation; even so by the righteousness of one the free gift came upon all men unto justification of life. For as by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by the obedience of one shall many be made righteous." Which plainly shows that as what Adam did brought death, as it had the nature of disobedience, so what Christ did brought life, not only as a sacrifice, but as it had the nature of meriting obedience.2

As much is plainly implied, 1 John 2:1, "And if any man sin, we have an advocate with the Father, Jesus Christ the righteous." Where surely his being righteous is mentioned as that which makes us acceptable to God, as sin makes us unacceptable and offensive. Galatians 4:4–5, "God sent forth his Son, made under the law, to redeem them that are under

-- 399 --

the law, that we might receive the adoption of sons." By his [being] made under the law, it is fair to understand3 all manner of subjection, both as requiring his passive and active subjection, and that both were to redeem us, that we might be made partakers of the adoption of sons.

But here are two things needful to be taken notice of, in order to the right understanding of Christ's active righteousness:

a. That there was no need that the law that Christ obeyed should be the same that was given to Adam, any otherwise than as to essentials. There was no need that the law should be so exactly the same as to have no precepts added, nor none wanting. It was necessary that it should be the same as to essentials. It was needful that Christ should be subject to the same moral law, but it was not necessary that there should be the same positive precepts exactly.

The moral law, or the law of nature, is an eternal, unalterable rule, always the same. But positive precepts, such as was that of not eating the forbidden fruit, and the precepts of the ceremonial law, such as circumcision, etc. is temporary and alterable. And with respect to these latter, the law that Christ obeyed need not be the same that Adam was to have obeyed.

And as to fact, it was not the same, for there was wanting the positive precept about the fruit of the tree of knowledge {of good and evil}, and there were added the ceremonial laws that Christ was made subject to.

Indeed, this was one part of the moral law that Adam was subject to, that he should obey whatsoever positive commands God should please to prescribe; and so indeed, obedience to positive precepts was comprised under the moral law. By the moral law, Adam was obliged not to eat {of the forbidden fruit} conditionally, if God forbid; and by the same moral law, Christ was obliged to be subject to the ceremonial law. And in this general notion, the law that Adam was subject to, and Christ as our head, was the same.

This indeed seems to be needful, that the positive precepts that Christ {was subject to} should be equivalent to that that Adam {was subject to}, and attended with as much difficulty; otherwise, Christ would not have the same trial that Adam had. And so the positive precepts that Christ was subject to were equivalent [to] the precepts of the ceremonial law that Christ was subject to, as he was a Jew. It was a heavy yoke of bondage to observe all those rights and ceremonies,

-- 400 --

much more than to abstain from one tree in paradise, a yoke which the apostles say neither they nor their fathers were able to bear (Acts 15:10).

It became Christ to be subject to this law that he might fulfill all righteousness, though Adam was never subject to this ceremonial law. But there were other positive precepts that Christ {was subject to} which were abundantly more than equivalent, as by the next particular.

b. I would observe that Christ's sufferings themselves may be considered as part of Christ's active obedience and positive righteousness. Christ, by dying, did not only procure for us negative righteousness or mere freedom from guilt as an expiatory sacrifice, but it was also part of his obedience and positive righteousness which he performed. He had received this command of the Father, that he should lay down his life. John 10:17–18, "Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life, that I might take it again. No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again. This commandment have I received of my Father."

Christ came into the world to do the will of his Father. Christ says, "Lo, I come: I delight to do thy will, O God" (Psalms 40:8); and John 6:38, "For I came down from heaven, not to do mine own will, but the will of him that sent me." And this was a part of his Father's will which he did, that he should lay down his life for his people. Therefore, herein he obeyed God's command. This was a positive precept far more difficult than Adam's {not eating the forbidden fruit}; it was a far greater trial of his obedience, attended with vastly stronger temptations.

Christ's death therefore was not only a sacrifice to do away sin, but it was the most exalted part of Christ's positive righteousness. 'Tis mentioned as such in the Scripture: Philippians 2:8, "He became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross." This is surely mentioned as the highest act of his obedience. This is the principal act of Christ's obedience, and therefore has the principal part in meriting {positive righteousness}. 'Tis the part of obedience which showed the greatest respect to the Father's will and authority, and therefore is most precious and acceptable to him.

So that by Christ's death, we han't only deliverance from hell, but heaven also is merited by it: as appears by Scripture, whereby we are told [that Christ] "redeemed us to God by his blood" (Revelation 5:9); and Hebrews 10:14, "By one offering he hath perfected forever them that are

-- 401 --

sanctified." As an expiatory sacrifice, it saves from hell; as obedience righteousness, it merits heaven. It can merit heaven no other way {than as his principal act of obedience}. By expiating sin merely, nothing positive is purchased.4

We are come,

2. [Second,] to show how his righteousness can be properly looked upon to be believers'.

'Tis what Christ performed, and not believers. Now how can what Christ did be looked upon as ours? And what propriety can there be in accepting of that, as if we had performed, and looking upon us, because Christ performed those things, as if we performed them? This question may be divided into two questions: how this is consistent with the reason of things; and second, how it can be consistent with the law.

(1) How can it be consistent with reason, or wherein lies the propriety of such an interchanging or mutuation of righteousness in God's account?

Now these things are mysteries that it would be presumption to pretend fully to understand and comprehend. But yet so much we may understand as to see a marvelous contrivance and wisdom in it.

Now the foundation of the propriety of this imputation of righteousness seems to lie in these two things: in Christ's union with God, and his union with men. It would not be proper that the righteousness of any person should be accepted by God for another, but a person that was one with God; nor would it [be] proper that it should be accepted for any person, but only a person that he is one with. And,

1. Union or oneness with God is requisite in order to a person's righteousness being accepted by God for another that has no righteousness.

Now Christ has that requisite union with God these two ways: first, as he has the divine nature and excellencies; and second, as he has the infinite divine love.

a. By his having the divine nature and excellencies, he had an infinite worthiness, and so was worthy not only to be accepted for himself, but for others. A person of finite worthiness, let it be never so great, has none to share; he has not more than enough for himself. But he that is infinitely worthy, has worthiness and merit sufficient for himself and for all others: where there is infinity, it is impossible there should

-- 402 --

be any want; the fountain is so great that it is not lessened at all by communication: how much soever he bestows on others, he has not the less for himself.

Therefore an angel, or any other creature, is not able to work out a righteousness that would be properly accepted. That righteousness and worthiness that is to the utmost capacity of their nature is no more than enough for themselves. No other but one of infinite worthiness is capable of proper merit.

Christ by his divine excellency and worthiness is worthy of such love from the Father as to have what righteousness he performs for the sake of men accepted—which he was no way obliged so to be accepted for them—and that his sufferings for them should appease his greatest anger. But this brings me to the

b. [Second] thing wherein Christ's union with God consists, and that is in God's infinite love to him.

As he is infinitely near and perfectly united to the Father in nature, so he is also in love. The Son is one with the Father in nature and also in love. God so loves the Son that he is as himself; he loves him infinitely. 'Tis the same love wherewith God loves himself.

This love of God to the Son gives an infinite value to Christ's sufferings and actions in God's account. If it were not for this, let Christ suffer never so much for sinners, it would not appease God's anger against them. If an angel should suffer to all eternity for others' sins, that would not appease God's anger against sin: for though God greatly loves his angels, yet his love to them, if put in the scales, is not sufficient to counterpoise God's opposition, hatred and indignation against sin.

Christ, by suffering, puts himself in the sinner's stead. But it would signify nothing if he did so, if he were not infinitely beloved of God: for it must be love, the opposite to anger and hatred, and nothing else, that appeases and removes it.

So 'tis God's love to Christ that gives that value in God's account to his positive righteousness. That righteousness which we have described was not what he performed for himself, and he had no need of it himself; and therefore, all that value which God put upon it, must be as performed for them for whom he performed it.

2. The second thing requisite in order to Christ's righteousness being looked upon [as] ours, is his union with us.

If Christ be one with us, then it need be no difficulty to conceive how

-- 403 --

his righteousness should be ours. If he be a stranger to us, and has no relation to us, then indeed it would be strange that what is his should be looked upon as ours, and his suffering be looked upon as our suffering and his obedience be looked upon {as our obedience}. But if he be so nearly united to us that he may [be] looked upon as one with us, then the thing is not so difficult. If he be our head, 'tis not so strange that what belongs to the head, the members should be partakers of, or that [if what] merit the head is should be imputed to the members.

This union of Christ to us, in like manner, consists in two things, viz. union of nature, aid love, as his union with God did.

a. Christ was united to us in nature. By his incarnation, he took our nature upon him, and by that wonderful act of his, united himself to us. He manifested his gracious and wonderful design of becoming the head of the human nature, the head of all the elect of mankind.

He hereby is near of kin to us, he is become our elder brother. Yea, more than a brother: by that act of taking the human nature upon himself, he sufficiently in the sight of God and in the sight of angels assumed the elect part of mankind into an union with himself, and was justly looked upon as their head.5

And then also, believers are united to Christ, and in a sense are partakers of his nature, in that they are partakers of his Spirit. Christ is therefore said to be in them. Romans 8:9–10, "But ye are not in the flesh, but in the Spirit, if so be that the Spirit of God dwell in you. Now if any man have not the Spirit of Christ, he is none of his. And if Christ be in you, the body is dead because of sin; but the Spirit is life because of righteousness."

And they are said to be partakers of the divine nature (2 Peter 1:4). And this is one reason that Christ's righteousness is looked upon [as] ours.

b. Christ is united to us in love. Christ loves the elect with so great and strong a love, they are so near to him, that God looks upon them as it were as parts of him.

Now if it be inquired how much love is sufficient for that, I answer, that if Christ love: men so that when they are to be destroyed, he out of mere love is willing to take their destruction upon himself—or what is equivalent to their destruction—then is Christ's love sufficient in God's

-- 404 --

account to make them one, for this reason, because when it is so, his love is such as does as it were of itself put the lover in the beloved's stead, even to the utmost and the most extreme case. That love that makes the lover willing to be in the stead of the beloved, even in the last extremity and where the beloved's utmost is concerned, even to perfect ruin and destruction, such love makes a thorough union.6 And those whom Christ thus loves, God is willing and sees proper to impute to them what he does and suffers for them. Answerably on the believer's part is faith and love.

These things may something show us how it is consistent with reason, or how it is in itself proper, that Christ's righteousness should be looked [upon] as ours, as if we ourselves had performed.

[(2)] But here another question remains, and that is, how it can be consistent with the law. It won't do that reason and the nature of things will allow of it, but it must also be consistent with the law, because that is the fixed and established rule of all transactions between God and us, that all mankind are under.

But the law says, "If thou eatest, thou shalt surely die" [Genesis 2:17]. And so it is implied in that, if thou eatest not, thou shalt live. It don't say, "If thou eatest, thou shalt die, or somebody else shall die for thee; and if thou, or another for thee, eats not, thou shalt live."

I answer, that the words of the law do allow of our being righteous by the righteousness of another, by the same way of interpreting them, as they render us guilty by the sin of another. The words of the law are to be so interpreted when it is said, "Thou shalt die," as to extend to others than the person sinning; otherwise we have no need of a savior, nor of any other person's righteousness. We, according to the sense of the law, are fallen and become guilty in another's fall and guilt. And the words, according to the same way of interpreting, will allow of our becoming righteous in another's righteousness. The law is to be so interpreted, that he is looked upon as sinning that sins in a surety; and therefore why, according to the same law, is not he righteous that is righteous in a surety?

The words, "If thou eatest thou shalt surely die," can't be so interpreted as to oblige us to a personal dying, because the eating spoken of is not restrained to a personal eating. The word "thou" in the former

-- 405 --

part of the sentence is not confined and restrained to a person, and there is no more reason that it should in the latter part.

If a way therefore can be found out that we may die in another and be righteous in another, in the same sense that we sin and come under obligation to die in Adam, the law is answered.

Thus we have shown that there is a way of sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God through Christ, and what that way is.

Second, to show that 'tis the work of the Holy Ghost [to give] understanding and conviction of this.

1. That it is his work, and his only, to do this, is evident.

'Tis far higher and more beyond the powers of nature than conviction {of sins}. There needs not only the operation, but such an operation as to change the very nature of the soul. Raise [it] from death.

[It is] far beyond the power of men, or angels, or the work of creation.

And I will bring the blind by a way that they knew not; I will lead them in paths that they have not known: I will make darkness light before them, and crooked things straight. These things will I do unto them, and not forsake them." All things are delivered unto me of my Father: and no man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man the Father, save the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him." Matthew 13:11, "He answered and said unto them, Because it is given unto you to know the mysteries of the kingdom of heaven, but to them it is not given."7 Matthew 16:17, "Flesh and blood hath not revealed it unto thee,

but my Father which is in heaven."8 Galatians 1:15–16, "But when it pleased God to reveal his Son in me." But,

2. We shall show what understanding and conviction {of sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God through Christ} it is that the Holy Ghost gives.

Natural men, and those that have never been taught by the Holy Ghost as the truth is in Jesus, may have a sort of understanding of it. They are capable of a literal and notional understanding of it, yea, and of a kind of belief in it. They may have it instilled into them by education, yea, and may be sensible of the fairness and plausibleness of the arguments brought to prove that Christ performed righteousness for sinners, that he died to save 'em and that God accepts them for his sake.

Therefore what is it they want, what lack they yet? What is it they are to expect, if ever they are savingly converted, more than they have? What more understanding and conviction of this way {of sinners' reconciliation and acceptance with God through Christ} is that that the Holy Ghost gives?

(1) The Holy Ghost convinces of the reality of this way of reconciliation and acceptance {with God through Christ} by the righteousness of Christ. Natural men think they believe that Christ is Savior, etc. They hear a great deal about it, and they don't contradict it; they have heard a great deal of arguing and reasoning, and they don't deny the force of the arguments. Yet for all that, it don't seem real to them.9

They hear it as a plausible story, but they never saw the reality of it, never saw the true grounds of the belief of it.

Persons may give a slighty and superficial assent to a thing and yet not realize it. It makes another impression upon the mind; it strikes deep, and influences the will and bias after quite another manner. There is a sensible, real conviction of the truth of the gospel, which declares this way of salvation to us that the Holy Ghost is the author of.

He believes that this is in very deed the way of life, the way of acceptance with God; that Christ is certainly the Savior of the world; that 'tis his suffering and blood that makes satisfaction for sin, and his merits that make men acceptable; that God receives men into his love

-- 407 --

and favor for Christ's sake. It all seems a reality to them: they see what ground and reason there is to believe it; they see the evident characters of truth in it all. John 6:69, "We know and are sure that thou art that Christ, the Son of the living God." 1 John 5:6, "This is he that came by water and by blood, even Jesus Christ; not by water only, but by water and blood. And it is the Spirit that beareth witness, because the Spirit is truth." And [1 John 5:10, "He that believeth on the Son of God hath the witness in himself: he that believeth not God hath made him a liar; because he believeth not the record that God gave of his Son." Ephesians 3:18–19, that you "may be able with all saints to comprehend what is the breadth, and length, and depth, and height; and to know the love of Christ, which passeth knowledge, that ye might be filled with all the fullness of God."

The Holy Spirit, when he enters, he lets in that divine light that discovers truth, and makes it appear as truth and shows the way of salvation, which appears and makes itself known by its own intrinsic evidence, which it carries with it.

(2) The Holy Ghost convinces of the suitableness and sufficiency of this way of salvation. The believing soul, when thus enlightened, sees a suitableness. Before, when under terrors of conscience for sin, there was a sense of having displeased and provoked God and deserved his wrath; and the sinner is sensible that God's authority and his majesty and justice stand against him, and he sees no way to escape vengeance. The sinner would feign make recompense some way himself, for he can see no propriety in {that way of salvation}.

But now he sees how all ends are answered by Christ's righteousness and by his way of salvation; how that God's honor, his majesty, authority and greatness are magnified as much as ever, though his provocation should all be passed by. He now sees a fitness in this way of acceptance into God's favor; how it is adapted to the case of such sinners as he; how well the remedy is suited to the disease, and how sufficient it is for him.

He sees that there is enough in Christ to make way for his acceptance; that his blood is of value enough, and that his1 dignity and excellent righteousness is enough to procure acceptance; and that it is sufficiently excellent for the Father to take such delight in it as to be well-pleased with him for it, however unworthy. Matthew 9:21, "If I may but touch the hem of his garment."

The robes of Christ's righteousness appear glorious robes, sufficient to cover his nakedness, and not only so, but sufficient to make him so adorned and beautiful as to be the object of the delight of the Father.

Though perhaps he may not be able to explain in words much about it, yet he sees a wonderful congruity in these things. He sees it to be a becoming, suitable thing that God should forgive and love poor sinners for Christ's sake.

(3) And lastly, the Holy Ghost causes to see a glorious excellency in this way. It all appears with a cast of glory upon it: there is a charming excellency and wisdom seen in it; it appears now an heavenly and divine way: there are in it the evident shines of divine glory.

It appears glorious as to its author. God the great contriver, and Jesus Christ the Savior, appears excellent, and there are many wonderful and admirable things appear in his person and actions.

And especially his grace and love appears glorious. Natural men see no glory in this dying love of Christ that they tell so much about. But it appears glorious to believers, unspeakably so.

The ends of this way of salvation also appear glorious: the advancement of the glory of God's grace, the exalting and magnifying God's mercy in the sight of men and angels; and the selection, the new creation and the eternal glory of sinful worms; and the exalting of the glory of Jesus Christ the Savior: these appear as excellent ends to one that has been taught by the Holy Ghost.

The means of it also appear most glorious, viz. the righteousness of Christ, his mediatorial worthiness in his living and dying.

The manifestation of the perfections of God in Christ which appear in it seem glorious: God's wisdom in this contrivance, his power in overcoming Satan, his majesty, his holiness appears in its purity and beauty, and specially his grace. 2 Corinthians 4:6, "For God, who commanded the light to shine out of darkness, hath shined in our hearts, to give the light of the knowledge of the glory of God in the face of Jesus Christ." Not all these things particularly at once.

Third place, to show what are the immediate effects of this conviction of righteousness which the Holy Ghost is the author of.2 And,

1. When the soul is thus convinced of the reality, suitableness and glory of this way of acceptance by Christ, the heart and inclination entirely embraces it.

The whole soul accords and consents to it; all prevailing opposition ceases. The heart that before opposed, now yields; it opposes no longer, but entirely closes with it and adheres to it with the inclination and affection. It receives it with entire credit and respect as true and worthy and excellent.

The heart acquiesces in it. It sees that in this way that it needs nor desires no other way. There is a sweet harmony now between the soul and the gospel; it doth in a lively manner accord and consent to it, and cleave to it. Thus we read of receiving the truth in the love of it, or receiving the love of the truth (2 Thessalonians 2:10), and obeying the truth from the heart (Romans 6:17). This is that gladly receiving the gospel spoken of, Acts 2:41. By this, we may understand what is meant (Acts 2:41) when it is said, God opened the heart of Lyddia, that she attended to those things which were spoken by Paul.

2. This is accompanied with the soul's committing itself to Jesus Christ. When the soul thus sees the excellency and sufficiency of Christ and the way of justification by him, then the soul quits all other endeavors for reconciliation and applies to him, fully choosing salvation by him with a hearty consent to be his, entirely hoping in his sufficiency. 2 Timothy 1:12, "I know whom I have believed, and I am persuaded that he is able."

3. Upon all this there follows a quiet rest and calmness of the mind. The mind before, which was in such tumults, so full of storms, encompassed about with terrors and dark and dreadful and threatening appearances, which [was] full of concern and distress, that wandered about seeking rest and finding none, is now set at rest, according to that promise, "Come unto me, all ye that labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest" (Matthew 11:28). And thus Christ proves to them "an hiding place from the wind, and a covert from the tempest," according to that place, Isaiah 32:2.

And I would observe to you that 'tis in these things that saving, justifying faith primarily does consist. This is to believe to the saving of the soul, thus to be convinced, and thus to embrace Christ and his way of salvation and entirely to acquiesce therein.3

Improvement.

We come now to the improvement of this part.

-- 410 --

Use I is of Reproof to them who trust in their own [righteousness], notwithstanding all this glorious provision that God has made for our justification; notwithstanding his manifestation of such wisdom in contriving the way to the wonderment of saints and angels; notwithstanding the great things that have been done, the strange things—the incarnation of Christ, his labors and death—that we through him might have righteousness; notwithstanding the mission of the Holy Ghost and his appointment to this office to convince men of this way {of reconciliation and acceptance} as the only way. Yet 'tis the way, the common way of sinners, that they can't be beat off from when they are afraid of hell, to trust {in their own righteousness} and to go about to do something for their reconciliation and acceptance with God themselves.

They endeavor and hope some way or other to make themselves very into God's favor, or to appease him or make him the less angry, and so at least to do something towards procuring reconciliation and acceptance. Or if they think God won't be reconciled and take 'em into favor for what they do, yet that he ought to do it, and that it would be no other than just and suitable or agreeable to his declarations of his mercy, and the like, and this naturally inclines them to endeavor this way to help themselves. This may well seem like extreme folly and madness for them that hear so much of what great things God has done {for sinners' justification}, but yet how common is it and how obstinate are men in it.

Now let such persons consider what a reflection they hereby cast upon the persons of the Trinity, who have been concerned in this way of reconciliation {and acceptance} as the authors of man's redemption by it. If you can or ought to obtain {justification} by your own righteousness, all that they did relating to it was altogether needless.

And what a reflection do you cast upon them as the contrivers of this wonderful way. The persons of the Trinity, they consulted from all eternity about it as being the main work of divine wisdom. The Father entered into a covenant of redemption with the Son before the foundation of the world, and if your way be true, it was all for nothing; it was only for a frivolous notion.

The Father, Son [and] Holy Ghost, they busied themselves about it needlessly. You, by your practice, reflect upon them as though they made a great ado and consulted to do some great and strange thing to no purpose, but only to suprise and amuse the world.

-- 411 --

Is not this the plain language of your practice? And what a horrid reflection is it upon the wisdom and majesty of the glorious Trinity, as though they were only mere triflers.

But more particularly:

First. Consider what a reflection it is upon God the Father, upon his wisdom and justice, in requiring and insisting upon the shedding of the blood of his only {begotten Son} in order to sinners' acceptance {with the Father}, when there was no need of it.

If you trust what you do is worthy, that God should {pay heed to your righteousness} or anything that you can do, you do as much say that God the Father was unreasonable in his demands to go to require so hard a thing. Yea, you'll hardly escape the imputation of reflecting upon God as though he were cruel, and delighted in the pain and disgrace of his own Son.

You rob God of all the glory of his mercy in sending his Son. Of so dreadful a nature is the sin of trusting to your own righteousness, such dreadful dishonor and reproach does it cast upon the great God who sent his Son into the world: you would make this act of God in sending his Son as much to his dishonor as it is indeed to his honor. This act by which God has so especially glorified himself, you would make the greatest reproach to God that can be.

Second. Consider that your opinion that your own righteousness is sufficient robs the Son of God of all the glory of his love in {suffering and dying}.

As for any need of his doing thus, according to your opinion of your own righteousness, he might have spared his pains; he might have kept in heaven. You see no cause to thank him for doing what there was not a bit of need of.

Indeed, this act of Christ is often spoken of in God's Word as being a wonderful act of love and grace. There are many exalted speeches about it; very much is made of it in the Scripture. Christ counts it his glory, and would have men admire him for it. But you don't see that there is any such glory in it: you don't thank him for his love, for he only did this to give you that that you have already without his giving; he did [this] that you might be partakers of his righteousness, and you want none of his righteousness: you have righteousness enough of your own.

Indeed, he looked upon you [as] poor and that you needed his help, but you think he was mistaken. You was rich enough already; you did

-- 412 --

not want any of his alms. He might have kept his gifts to himself, for all you [care].

According to your opinion of your own righteousness, he was guilty of the most egregious folly in running himself into such a miserable case for nothing. Self-righteousness blasphemes Christ as though he were the greatest fool in the world to leave heaven, where he had perfect happiness. Christ stands high in reputation in the gospel for his {sacrifice}, but in your account, he was the greatest fool in the world for his pains.

Third. Consider that by trusting in your own righteousness, you greatly dishonor the Holy Ghost.

1. You rob him of all the glory of convincing men of righteousness, or of convincing men of the way of justification {and acceptance}. 'Tis that which is more especially the glory of the Holy Ghost, that he makes application of Christ's redemption this way and is the immediate author of that divine light that causes men to understand {this way of reconciliation and acceptance}. This is the most glorious light, the most excellent knowledge; and by giving this heavenly light and producing the effects which are produced by it in the soul, the glory of the Holy Ghost is more manifested than in any other work that we behold.

But he is robbed of all this glory by men's opinion of their own righteousness. If men's own righteousness {produces reconciliation and acceptance}, then this work of the Holy Ghost is altogether needless; 'tis no glorious work. Yea,

2. By this, this self-righteousness reflects upon the Holy Ghost as though he came into the world to convince men of lies. If it be so that your righteousness {produces reconciliation and acceptance}, then the Holy Ghost, in coming to convince men of righteousness, comes to convince men of that that is not true. He comes to convince men that the righteousness of Christ is that alone by which we can have acceptance. But this is a lie, if men's own righteousness be sufficient.

How great a reflection is this upon the Spirit of truth and light. Of so horrid a nature is self-righteousness. Perhaps self-righteous [men] don't distinctly think of all [this]; but yet so, these are the direct and plain consequences of a self-righteous opinion. They rob God of all his glory in this glorious work of redemption, by which he designed such peculiar glory to himself…. [They] rob every person [in the Trinity] of their special glory. And instead of the Father's wisdom and grace, attributes to him unreasonableness and cruelty; instead of the Son's

{love and grace, attributes to him great foolishness; instead of the Holy Ghost's convincing of truth, attributes to him convincing of lies}.4

So exceedingly is it opposite to the nature and design of the gospel; such dreadful work does it make, with God's glory therein manifested; so horribly reproachful is it to every person in the Trinity by just interpretation.

How greatly then are persons to be blamed. How guilty do they make themselves that trust in their {own righteousness}, that think by their works to appease {God's wrath} or procure his favor, or that imagine that in justice or honor God is obliged {to pay heed to their righteousness}.

Such an embracing of it does, as you have heard, depend upon a conviction of the reality, suitableness and glorious excellency of it. We shall offer some things to your consideration, tending to convince of each of these. And in the

We now come to the last work of the Holy Ghost here mentioned, viz. {convincing men of judgment}. This also is part of that spiritual knowledge and saving light {that the Holy Ghost is the author of}.

We may understand what is meant by this convincing of judgment by the illustration of it in the eleventh verse, "Of judgment, because the prince of this world is judged." By this, we understand that this conviction is of something relating to Jesus Christ. The judgment here spoken of is his judgment, as well as the righteousness, his righteousness. It is Christ that executes judgment upon Satan, the grand enemy of mankind: he came into the world for these ends, to justify us by finishing a righteousness for us and to condemn, confound and execute judgment upon Satan. Therefore Christ says, when he was going to be crucified and speaking of his death, "Now is the judgment of this world: now shall the prince of this world be cast out. And I, if I be lifted up from the earth, will draw all men unto me" (John 12:31–32).

So Christ teaches us in another place that he came to bind the strong man, and to spoil his goods [review me]. And the Apostle teaches us, Colossians 2:15, that Christ, "Having spoiled principalities and powers, he made a show of them openly, triumphing over them in it."

By "judgments" in Scripture are commonly to be understood all acts of rule and government whatsoever. Rulers are called "judges" by a common name, and their acts of rule and government are called "judgments."

Whether they be giving laws—thus God's Word and commandments are often called his "judgments" very frequently in the Psalms, and in the book of the Law, "statutes" and "judgments"—or whether they are punishments of the breaches of those laws, or rewards of obedience, or acquittance [of] and justifying the innocent—thus the Psalmist prays when persecuted by wicked men, "Judge me, O Lord; for I have walked in mine iniquity" (Psalms 26:1)—or whether acts of mercy and grace—thus we read of God's judging the widow and the fatherless—or whether it be making war, exerting acts of hostility against enemies, as Matthew 12:20, "Till he send forth judgment unto victory."

And in this sense we find the word particularly applied to Christ, in the prophesies of Christ's coming. Psalms 96:12–13, "Let the field be joyful, and all that is therein: then shall all the trees of the wood rejoice before the Lord: for he cometh, for he cometh to judge the earth: he

-- 419 --

shall judge the world with righteousness, and his people with truth." That is… In so extensive [a] sense it seems to be intended, John 5:22–23. "The Father hath committed all judgment to the Son: that all men may honor the Son, as they honor the Father." 'Tis parallel to that place, John 3, [John 3:35, "The Father loveth the Son, and hath given all things into his hands." And in this sense we take it in this place. By "judgment" seems here to be meant Christ's exercise of his power and dominion, as constituted the king of his people and the captain of their salvation.

The former, the conviction of righteousness, was the saving sight of Christ as high priest of his people. This is the true sight of him as sovereign Lord and king of his people, and their mighty deliverer.9

Christ's judging of Satan seems to be mentioned to illustrate this part of the text, "Of judgment, because the prince of this world is judged," for these reasons:

1. The most notable act that Christ doth in his government of the world, which the Father has committed to his [judgment], is his subduing of Satan, that grand adversary. He came into the world, he tells us, to destroy the works of the devil. If it had not been for that dreadful travail… there would have been no occasion for Christ's mediatorial lordship and dominion.

2. Christ, by judging Satan or destroying his kingdom, lays the foundation of his own rule and dominion as Lord of his people. Satan had got the dominion of the children of men into his own hands.

Christ obtains his kingdom by conquest. 'Tis made up wholly of redeemed captives….

There are Satan's kingdom and the kingdom of Christ, [that] are such opposites, that the taking away of one establishes the other. For this reason probably, Christ here calls Satan "prince of this world": "The prince of this world is judged." Satan was prince and ruler, but Christ establishes his kingdom upon the ruins {of Satan's kingdom}.

3. All Christ's acts of kingly power as the deliverer of his people do consist in the destruction of Satan and his interest, and may be reduced to that head. Christ executes the office of a king or deliverer in subduing as to himself, that is, in subduing sin and Satan's kingdom

-- 420 --

and interest in the soul, in defending us from [him], in restraining and conquering all his {enemies}, that is, Satan and his adherents.

Prop. [III.] That 'tis the work of the Holy Ghost to make men see the sufficiency and excellency of Christ as the Lord and deliverer of his people.

By "deliverer" here we would not be understood as having respect to the whole extent of Christ's work as a savior. When Christ is spoken of as a savior, it respects his salvation by merit and by power both. 'Tis only the latter that we here have respect to: his saving his people by power and dominion, the same as "captain of our salvation," as Christ is called (Hebrews 2:10).

We shall handle this proposition much in the same method as we did the former, and shall show,

First. That Christ Jesus is the Lord and deliverer.

Second. That 'tis the Holy Ghost that shows his glory as such.

Third. We shall consider the natural fruits of this conviction that are evidential of it.

First. Jesus Christ is constituted Lord of his people and captain of their salvation. God the Father hath given to [the] Mediator all power in heaven and in earth; he hath committed all judgment to the Son. God hath given him perfect and complete absolute power and dominion. He hath made him Lord of his people, who are the kingdom.

"Lord," [the] name by which he is commonly called…

"Head." United to him. Head and members…

He is as a father amongst them; they are his family; they are called his children. Hebrews 2:13, "Behold I and the children which God hath given me."

They are his army.

This kingdom is every way a kingdom of grace.

He rules by love and grace, and the benefits and ends of his kingdom are wonderfully gracious.1

And then he hath not only made him Lord of his people directly and immediately, but Lord of all other things for them, that he might order them as he pleased, so far as they any way concerned his church, his kingdom of grace.

Therefore, God has made him head of the angels. God hath made him head of all principality and power (Colossians 2:10). "God said, Let all

the angels of God worship him" (Hebrews 1:6). And they are his ministering spirits, "sent forth to minister for them who shall be heirs of salvation" [Hebrews 1:14].

And so he has the rule of inanimate things, and the whole government of providence is in his hands to this end…. Ephesians 1:22, Christ, "head over all things to the church."

And a principal exercise of this power and authority in this world is in delivering his people, who are here in a militant state. But here more particularly we shall consider how Christ exercises his dominion or his authority to execute judgment, committed, first, in ruling his people; second, in delivering them.

1. In ruling his people. He exercises his dominion in ruling his people.

(1) In giving them rules and precepts for them to live by. Such indeed are all the rules of God's Word that are given to regulate our walks. The Ten Commandments were given by Christ more immediately, and 'twas the Spirit of Christ that influenced the prophets.

But such more especially are those that are called the rules of the gospel, those rules that Christ himself gave with his own mouth, and those that he gave by his Spirit to the apostles: such as those that we should love Christ above "father, or mother, or wife, or children, or lands"; and that we should not look at things "which are seen, but at the things which are not seen"; and that we should deny ourselves and take up {our cross}; that we should love one another as Christ has loved us; that [we should] forgive those that {sin against us}; that we should love our enemies, do good {to those that hate us}; that as much as in us lies, "live peaceably with all men."2 And all those rules of godliness, of patience, meekness, brotherly kindness, charity, purity [and] temperance which we have in the New Testament.3 And particularly, that we should follow the example of Christ, and make him our pattern. These are the laws of Christ's kingdom, by which he governs the city of Zion. These are the rules and orders of his family.

And besides such as these, there are orders of another nature, and they are such as respect the public worship of his church. Christ, as head of the church, appoints officers, sends forth his ministers and ambassadors, appoints ordinances and rules of worship, rules to be observed by his peoples in their joining together to serve God.

(2) In his ruling in their hearts by the powerful influences of his grace. Christ sets up his kingdom in the hearts of his people; he has his throne in every believer's heart. Luke 17:21, "The kingdom of God is within you."

Christ rules his subjects, not as other princes do theirs, by external force, but he rules their hearts. He governs their wills and inclinations, and turns them as the rivers {of waters}. Christ exercises his mighty power this way. Psalms 110:3, "Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power." This power is a gracious, almighty influencer, whereby Christ guides and governs the inclinations and actions of the heart.

By this he powerfully causes a willing obedience to his rules and laws, and evinces subjection to him. He gives laws by his Word, but he causes them to be obeyed by his Spirit and vital influence communicated from himself.

(3) In judging his people in their particular judgment at death. But especially in their general judgment at the resurrection, when Christ shall judge them according to their works. He shall judge them in righteousness.

Many of them here in this world are judged by men's judgments, at other tribunals, exceeding unjustly. They are unjustly and cruelly dealt with, suffer as guilty when they are innocent, are condemned even for their good works. But Jesus Christ is to be their final judge, and he will justify them; he'll make their innocency to appear, and will openly acquit them.

Many of them have been censured here in this world as hypocrites and wicked men, but Christ will try them and will make their sincerity appear. Then the sincerity of their faith and love and zeal will be known.

Many of their good works were kept secret in this world; none knew them but God and they. But then God will bring all their secret piety and charity to light before men and angels.

Then Christ will pronounce the sentence of, "Well done, thou good and faithful servant" [Matthew 25:21]. Then he will openly honor them by placing of 'em at his right hand, and will openly reward them; will give them a crown of life; will give them the possession of their kingdom, and all those blessed, unseen, unheard, unknown things reserved in heaven for them, and shall fix [them] in this state of blessedness and honor to all eternity.

2. We'll consider how Christ exercises the dominion which God has

given him over the world as the deliverer of his people. And that he doth,

(1) In delivering and defending of them from the power of sin and Satan in their conversion. Christ rescues his people out of Satan's hands; he delivers them from their captivity to him. They are then translated from the power of darkness into the kingdom of his dear Son (Colossians 1:13). Satan before had a kingdom in the heart: he held possession of it as a strong man armed. But one stronger than he comes and dispossesses him, breaks his bonds in sunder, opens the prison doors [and] brings out of the prison wherein there is no water. This Christ doth by his mighty power; 'tis a work of infinite strength, thus to redeem a poor soul out [of] a state of captivity to sin and Satan.

And when once Christ has thus rescued them from captivity, he by his mighty power defends them. If Christ did not defend the soul after it was thus delivered, Satan would immediately fly upon the soul again, as a fierce lion flies upon his prey again that is taken out of his mouth. He stands ready to swallow up the soul and devour it at once, were it not that Christ defended.

Christ defends the soul from and in Satan's temptations. Either they shall not be tempted, or they shall be so far supported that they shall not be utterly overthrown by the temptations. Christ won't suffer the gates of hell ever to prevail against his people (Matthew 16:18).

And Christ doth more and more deliver his people from the power of sin and Satan in sanctification. Those that are converted ben't wholly delivered at once from these. Though their influence has received its deadly wound and is dying, yet it is not wholly dead; but still sin and Satan have their interest in the heart. But Christ will more and more deliver from it by mortifying sin and lust and carrying on his work of grace.

Yea, Christ will turn Satan's own endeavors for the hurt of his people into their good. He will make Satan's temptations work together for the good of their souls. They shall be as silver tried in the fire.

Christ will not only deliver his people from Satan himself, but all his adherents. He will deliver them from ungodly men.

And at last, this deliverance will be perfected by Christ in the glorification of his people. At death, the souls of his people shall be forever exalted far above the reach of sin, or Satan or any that assist them. Satan shall not only not have dominion over them, nor interest in them, but he shall no more tempt; he shall not come nigh them.

It shows the mighty power of God sometimes, when the saints are supported and are made to rejoice in such great affliction {and torment}.

Second place, to show that 'tis the work of the Holy Ghost to convince men of the sufficiency and excellency of Christ, in his being thus the Lord and deliverer of his people.

Christ, as he is the greatest king of the most universal dominion, so he is the most glorious king. He is exalted to an high and glorious station by the right hand of God, and there he shines with a luster becoming that station. And 'tis the Spirit of Christ only that can make men behold the glory of this king of saints. The glory and sufficiency of Christ as king and deliverer of his people, manifested by the Holy Ghost, consists,

1. In his mighty power, whereby he is able to govern the world and to rule with omnipotent sway amongst his people, and subdue all their enemies and deliver them from all evil.

'Tis the Holy Ghost only that [causes] men to see the sufficiency of the power of Christ to these ends, that his power is enough for the accomplishment of all things for them that they need. However great difficulties and dangers they are involved in, the Holy Ghost convinces that the power of Christ is sufficient to deliver. However strong enemies are, and however great advantages they seem to have against them… However powerful temptations… However strong the remains of lust and corruption…

They are convinced of the sufficiency of the power of Christ to preserve them safe to his heavenly kingdom, and, in spite of all opposition that appears, to bring them at last to heavenly glory. And to raise up the body from the dead. However4 mysterious the work appears, he is convinced of the sufficiency of the power of his glorious Lord.

The believer especially beholds and admires the power of Christ appearing in the works of his grace in the heart…. And upon all occasions, let him be never so weak in himself, he sees how glorious God's power is by his weakness.

2. It consists in the wisdom of his government. Christ is called "the Wisdom," as in [the] Proverbs 1 [Proverbs 1:20] and 1 Corinthians 1:24, and he reigneth and executes judgment in infinite wisdom.

The Holy Ghost convinces of this wisdom of Christ, of the wisdom of his laws, of the sufficiency of his wisdom to guide and direct them by his Spirit, and lead them in the right way; the sufficiency of his wisdom to deliver from the crafty wiles and secret snares of the devil; the sufficiency of his wisdom and knowledge to turn all things, even the most malicious designs of his enemies, to his good. Jesus Christ, the King of Zion, appears glorious to believers upon the account of this wisdom of his, manifested in ruling and saving his people.

3. The glory of Christ as Lord {of his people} consists in his holiness, particularly the laws of Christ. The rules of the gospel appear glorious upon the account of their holiness: that is what makes them lovely to one that is taught by the Spirit of Christ; this is what renders them more precious than rubies and sweeter than [honey]: they are greatly delighted in upon this account.5

The inward influences of Christ Jesus, whereby he governs the hearts of men, appear glorious also upon this account, upon the account of [the] holiness of them. The graces of the Spirit of Christ, the Christian [life] and disposition, are lovely to a believer because they are holy, and Christ's throne is a glorious throne in his eyes because 'tis a throne of holiness (Psalms 47:8); and Christ appears glorious because he is the Holy One of Israel.

Christ's administration of his government appears glorious upon the holiness of it, in his judging and subduing Satan, that wicked one and father of wickedness; and in judging amongst men, in punishing the wicked and giving glorious rewards to those that bring forth holy fruits. Therefore is Christ called "King of righteousness" in the name of Melchisedec, that remarkable type of Christ. Hebrews 7:2, "Being by interpretation, King of righteousness."

4. And lastly, the glory of Christ as Lord {of his people}, manifested by the Holy Ghost, consists in his wonderful grace appearing in his exercise of his dominion.

Christ's kingdom is rightly the kingdom of grace. 'Twas by his wonderful grace that they became his kingdom [and] were made subject to him; 'twas his love to them that rescued them out of {the hands of Satan}. It is grace that defends them and preserves in Christ's kingdom. It is wonderful grace that upholds and comforts in affliction and delivers from it. It is wonderful grace that exalts to glory out of the reach {of affliction and torment}, and raising from the dead.

The laws of Christ, the gospel by which he governs, are wonderfully gracious, exceedingly tending in their own nature to the peace, comfort, joy and happiness of his people. They are calculated for his people's happiness, such as looking at things unseen, loving one another, living in peace, etc.

His way of governing the hearts of his people is wonderfully gracious. He don't force them, but sweetly inclines their wills to the most excellent things and to their own happiness. He overcomes them by his love; he governs them by infusing his love into them, and so inclining them to obedience. His dominion over his people is by mutual love and esteem. He is therefore deservedly called "the prince of peace."

And when Christ judges his people, he will wonderfully make his grace to appear by the wonderful free reward he will give. He will take them to himself, that where he is, etc.

Third. The genuine fruits of this conviction {that are evidential of it}.

1. Trusting in him in dangers and difficulties. When afraid of Satan [or] beset with temptations. When burdened with indwelling sin… When in great affliction and adversity… When they don't know what to do. When their wisdom is nonplussed.

When afraid of death or in death.

Trust his power in weakness. His wisdom in ignorance and difficulties. His grace in unworthiness.

2. A rejoicing under his rule and government. That is great cause of joy to a true Christian, that Christ reigneth, and especially that he reigneth over him. Isaiah 52:7, "How beautiful upon the mountains are the feet of him that bringeth good tidings, that publisheth peace; that bringeth good tidings of good, that publisheth salvation; that saith unto Zion, Thy God reigneth!" Revelation 19:6, "And I heard as it were the voice of a great multitude, and as the voice of many waters, and as the voice of mighty thunderings, saying, Alleluia: for the Lord God omnipotent reigneth." It was the blessedness of Israel that Balaam took notice of, Numbers 23:21, "The Lord his God is with him, and the shout of a king is among them."6 There is nothing that is greater cause of rejoicing and shouting for Christ's sincere servants, than that Christ is their king.

3. An entire subjection unto Christ, and an universal and willing obedience to all his commandments. All those that have truly seen the glory of Christ as the Lord of his people and captain, this will be the

-- 428 --

effect. Therefore all such [as] live a holy and a Christian life, observing the precepts of the gospel, walking according to those excellent rules, and have respect to all {God's commandments}, they take Christ's yoke upon them and are his steady followers.7

Improvement

We are now come to the improvement of this part.

I. Hence we learn how holiness of life and good works is secured by the gospel. Good works are not what men are justified by, but men are justified freely by faith in Jesus, through whom God justifies the ungodly. And God has promised that whosoever believes in Christ shall be saved: if he has once believed in Christ, he is sure of heaven; and it is impossible he should miss of it, how many sins soever he has been guilty of before, and though he falls into great sins afterwards.

Now some may be ready to say, if it be so, what need we trouble ourselves about good works, and what provision is there made for the holy living of believers? If they have believed in Christ, they may live as wickedly as they please, and enjoy the pleasure of sin as much as they please. They are sure they should go to heaven when they die notwithstanding, because they have believed in Christ and trusted in him for salvation; and then the gospel will be nothing else but an encouragement to licentiousness, and opens a door for all manner of wickedness.

The like objection is proposed and answered by the Apostle (Galatians 2:17).

But I say by this proposition, how the gospel secures good works, though men are justified only by faith and not by the works of the law: for the work of the Holy Ghost in conversion don't only consist in convincing men of righteousness, but also of judgment. He don't only show them the sufficiency of Christ's righteousness to justify 'em and so make 'em receive him as a savior from punishment, but also shows the glory of Jesus Christ as Lord and king, and makes them to receive him heartily and entirely as their Lord and king, their lawgiver, their deliver from the power of sin and Satan; that is, he makes them to submit to his rule and government, to yield themselves up to him as his servants and followers, and to trust in him to mortify and subdue sin, and deliver them from that as well as from punishment.

-- 429 --

Conviction of righteousness and judgment always go together: the Holy Ghost don't give one without the other. The preceding conviction of sin is in order to both [of] these, and these are always given together in concurrence. And evermore when Christ is received as priest, he is received as king also; when his righteousness is received, his government or judgment is also received.

And they that are thus convinced of the glory and excellency of Christ as Lord and king, they are the most likely to yield a hearty and universal and careful obedience to God's commands and to bring forth good works. This is the most effectual means for this end; and this is an effectual means, a means that evermore has the effect and obtains the end.

Indeed, the more they have of this conviction, the less is their obedience from a principle of slavishness.8 But the more it is from a more noble principle, even from love, esteem, and honor and gratitude— which principles alone can make obedience acceptable to God—this is to serve God in the newness of the Spirit and not in the oldness of the letter.

They that serve God only from fear of his wrath, their service is not a service of heart or of the spirit, but only external. Fear will never make a man hearty and spiritual, though it may influence the outward actions; but he that serves God from an high esteem and supreme love and thankfulness, he serves God with the heart.

So that the gospel is so far from being an encouragement to sin, and Christ the minister of sin, that there is nothing else that lays a foundation for a true and upright obedience.

II. Hence we learn how vain the hopes of such persons are that think that they have trusted in Christ's righteousness and yet don't live a life of universal holiness. There are some such: they pretend that they trust in Christ, and that they depend only on Christ's righteousness and have no dependence on their own righteousness, that yet show by their lives that they never were convinced of judgment. They'll tell a very formal story about the light and discoveries they had: how they first see their own hearts; and that it would be just with God to cast 'em off; and afterwards, what a wonderful discovery they had of Christ from such and such places of Scripture. But they never were in concert with Christ for their king; they never liked his government, as is very

-- 430 --

evident, for they don't walk according to the rules of the gospel. They'll do some things perhaps, but they live in the neglect of others. There is some lust or other that they indulge; there is some sin that they practice and that they plead for.

They are not just and upright in their dealings, or they live in some way of sensuality. They ben't of a spirit to take up their cross and follow after Jesus Christ. They won't deny themselves for Christ's sake; they ben't of a spirit to suffer for Christ. They regard their pleasure or their money or their neighbor's good will more than they do Christ Jesus and submission to his government.

There are some that don't observe those rules of charity and forgiveness that Christ so much insisted upon: they are all for themselves, and none for anybody else; they are proud, spiteful, revengeful sort of men; they lay up the remembrance of injuries forever in their breasts.

There are some of these that tell of their experiences that are not heavenly minded; they mind earthly things. Covetousness is a ruling principle in them.

It would be endless to mention all the particulars, but surely they are far from that holy, Christian walk which is the proper evidence of submitting to Jesus Christ as their Lord and king and deliverer from sin as well as punishment.

[III.] I shall conclude the improvement of this proposition by directing of you to some heads of consideration and meditation in order to your being convinced of {the glory and sufficiency of Christ as Lord and deliverer of his people}, and your being brought to an entire, cheerful and joyful yielding of yourself to Christ as your king.

First. Consider how proper it is that Christ should have dominion and power, who is the great God's only Son and heir. If it be fitting that those that are nobly descended and are of royal blood should have honor and rule (Ecclesiastes 10:17), how well doth the kingdom and power and glory suit with Christ, the natural Son of God himself, who han't the blood of earthly kings but the nature of the eternal God in him.

And [Christ] is not only his own natural Son, of the same nature with him, but is his only begotten Son, infinitely more honorable than the highest descended of mortal worms. Psalms 89:6, "There are none amongst the sons of the mighty can be likened unto the Lord."

And as Christ is the only Son of God, so he is the heir of all things. Galatians 4:7, "If a son, then an heir." Hebrews 1:2, "God hath spoken to us by his Son, whom he hath appointed heir of all things."

O how honorable a king have we over us! He is not one of least

-- 431 --

descent, a mean, obscure person that will be a disgrace. But we are the kingdom of the Son of God; we are the citizens of that city that God's heir reigns [over]. How great an honor is it to us to have such an one become our king, and captain of our salvation! And what a great privilege is it that we enjoy, that this heir of God should choose us, the poor children of men, to make more especially his kingdom!

Second. Consider what it was that moved him ever to become the king of the children of men. No profit… It was his love and pity to us that moved him. He saw us from the heights of his glory, poor worms enslaved to Satan and under the power and dominion of that ever-cruel tyrant and enemy, in a deplorable and miserable condition, and he pitied us. His heart earned towards us; it was a time of love with him. Being of an infinitely gracious nature, [he] was pleased to love us with a very dear love; his delights were with the sons of men (Proverbs 8:31).

And that was what moved him to become our head and king, and he set up his government over us: it was that he might be our deliverer. It was a thing well-pleasing to him, to think of reigning over such a miserable, deplorable company, that he might bring them from [being] miserable to the highest heights of happiness.

Third. Consider that Christ lays the foundation of this kingdom of his in his blood. He went through great, unheard of labors and sufferings to redeem us out of the hands of Satan, to bring [us] from our lost estate, so that we might be brought to him and become his church and kingdom.

He has purchased us to be a peculiar people to himself, for him forever to rule over. Never any king obtained his kingdom at so dear a rate. Many kings have gone through many dangerous battles and great hardships and vast difficulties to obtain their kingdom, but never any did or endured such great things to obtain their kingdom as Christ to obtain his kingdom of grace. Acts 20:28, "The church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood."

Christ condescends to esteem his church his riches, his treasure, his crown of rejoicing. And he has won this crown at the price of his own life; he waded through a sea of blood to come at it.

Fourth. Consider the excellency of the laws and rules by which Christ's kingdom is governed. The rules of his kingdom might all be resolved into these two: to be excellent, and to be happy. Christ our king requires us to be of a divine and heavenly temper, to be, as much as our limited natures will allow, like God our heavenly Father, and to

-- 432 --

act and behave ourselves after the most divine and heavenly manner that we can; to be pure in our thoughts and affections, to be amiable in our actions, to follow after "whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are true, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report" [Philippians 4:8].

If we do these things that Christ requires of us, we shall but make ourselves shine with God's image, and shall have a luster and brightness upon our souls superior to that of Moses, full superior to that of the sun in the firmament.

All that is required of us by Christ may be summed up in that one thing, even love: that we should be filled with that divine principle; that we should love God and exert all suitable acts of love towards that infinitely lovely object; that we should love Christ Jesus our savior, the Son of God; that we should love one another even as ourselves.

How excellent a condition is that kingdom, in that [it] is subject to such laws. Obedience to these rules makes a man more excellent than his neighbor, makes a man as a jewel amongst rubbish.

Fifth. Consider the excellent and gracious manner of Christ's governing his people by communicating vital influences to them and governing their hearts. His powerful, inward influence is exceeding pleasant. The soul is never so happy as when it is under the strongest influences of Christ, and his heart is most irresistibly governed by him. Christ don't drive, but he draws the heart; he draws by light and love.

There is the greatest liberty in this his government. Men ben't forced and compelled, but they follow the sweet and gracious influences of Christ upon the will; they are not abridged of anything.9 Christ rules over his subjects as a father amongst his dear children; he governs them by paternal respect and esteem and love. His commands are but fatherly counsels.

Christ's kingdom is his spouse. He is espoused to every soul that is his subject. They stand in the nearest relation to him. Yea, Christ's kingdom is his own body.

Sixth. Compare this dominion of Christ that believers are under to the tyranny of that master that the rest of the world are under.

Seventh. Consider the end of Christ's rule and dominion over his people in another world. What great advantage to his subjects. How glorious will Christ's kingdom be in its perfect, complete state.

Eighth. The angels count it their happiness that Christ is king over

-- 433 --

them. How they sing. Revelation 5:11–12, "And I beheld, and I heard the voice of many angels round about the throne and the beasts and the elders: and the number of them was ten thousand times ten thousand, and thousands of thousands; saying with a loud voice, Worthy is the Lamb that was slain to receive power, and riches, and wisdom, and strength, and honour, and glory, and blessing."

General Improvement.

I shall conclude this discourse and what I have to say from this text in a few words of general improvement of this whole doctrine.

[Use] I. If it be so that 'tis the work of the Holy Ghost thus to convince men of sin, of righteousness, and of judgment, then we learn where ministers should have their dependence in their endeavors, even upon the Holy Ghost.

The Word of God indeed is sharp as a two-edged sword, and is quick and powerful, but it is so only through the cooperation of that Spirit that gave the Word. The Word alone, however managed, explained, confirmed and applied, is nothing but a dead letter without the Spirit. The weapons of their warfare are indeed mighty, but it is only through God (2 Corinthians 10:4). And then only can they hope to have [the Word of God] to success or to come in the fullness of the blessing of the gospel of Christ, but when they go forth in Christ's strength. That is all the encouragement ministers have that Christ has promised them, that he will be with them, i.e. by his Spirit, to the end of the world. Matthew 28:19–20, "Go ye therefore, and teach all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Ghost: Teaching them to observe all things whatsoever I have commanded you: and, lo, I am with you alway, even unto the end of the world."

Ministers therefore ought not depend on their learning or eloquence, nor upon their diligence, or the greatness of their endeavors to promote the good of souls. If they take never so much pains, they should not trust to that. Nor ought they to depend on their own experiences or the goodness of their preparations. But their eye should be to their master that sent them, to make their labors successful through his Spirit.

They should have their eyes to him. Public. Private. Not discouraged when without success. [Not] exalted by success or attribute it to themselves, or think themselves better or wiser than other ministers that have not success.

-- 434 --

Their eyes should be to God to reform men that are vicious in their lives, however established and confirmed they seem to be in vicious courses. Convince secure [men]. Savingly to bring men to Christ… To direct them aright…

And therefore, 'tis ministers' duty to be much in prayer to God for this end. They should be as earnest with God in prayer as he is with the people in counsel, advice and warning.

[Use] II. We may take notice of the wisdom of God in so ordering that each person should have such a share in the work of redemption. We have often observed that it was in this work more especially that God designed to glorify himself1 from all eternity, and he had this principally in view in the creation of the world and in all the great works of providence from the beginning.

And seeing God subsists in three persons that are the same in essential glory, it was seen meet that in this great work they should all of them have their particular glory manifested with a brightness and luster answerable to their equal, essential glory; that not only the perfections of the divine essence common to all might be manifested therein, but that glory of God be shown which consists in the deity's subsistence in three persons and their particular manner of subsistence; that the respect of the creatures might be equal to each of them who are in themselves equal; and that the creature's absolute dependence might be upon each of 'em respectively in this great affair, which is the greatest concern—and indeed the all—of the creature. And also that the glory of each of them might with a suitable luster [appear] to the angels in this work, which the angels desire to look into.

Now 'tis worth our while to consider how this matter is ordered. The Father manifests his glory by entertaining thoughts of mercy towards men after their rebellion, and that he should give his own Son, infinitely dear to him, for this end; and giving all the elect to him to be taken care of by him, and have their selection secured, and in accepting what Christ did, and justifying believers for his sake and taking of them into favor.

The Son, he is the Redeemer. He is he that actually procures salvation, that lays down his life; he redeems by merit, as he is the high priest, and by power, as he is the king of his church.

The Holy Ghost is he that, after salvation is procured, immediately possesses them of it all. Though Christ procures all, he confers all; 'tis

-- 435 --

he that converts the sinner, that brings him out of darkness; 'tis he unites to Christ. He gives holiness and he gives the happiness purchased; he enlightens [the] understanding and renovates the will, elevates and purifies the affections. He gives the heart all comfort and all spiritual blessing in this world; he preserves to the end and fits for glory.

And not only so, but 'tis he that immediately gives eternal life also. 'Tis he that perfects holiness, and 'tis he that perfects the spiritual happiness of the soul. Then they will be perfectly holy and happy forever, because the Holy Spirit fills them.

Now I think it can hardly be said which of the persons in the Trinity has the greatest share in this work of redemption: it's all from every one of them. It's all from God the Father, for he is the person that determines whether or no there shall be any salvation at all: he provides the Savior, by whom all salvation is procured, and he accepts him, and he sends the Spirit; so that it is all originally from the Father. It is also all from the Son, for he procures it all; he is the medium of it all. And it is all from the Holy Ghost, for he applies it all; he possesses of it all. Therefore the glory of all that happiness which we have by redemption is to be given to each of them, and our absolute dependence in the whole affair is to be upon each of them. And glory will be given in heaven forever to the Father, the Son and the Holy Ghost with equal ardor, exaltation and gratitude. As the wisdom of God appears in abundance of other instances in this work of redemption, so this is one remarkable instance for which this work is worthy to be admired by us.

[Use] III. From this doctrine we learn how we ought to be affected towards the Holy [Ghost], and what glory we should give. The doctrine teaches us our concern with him and our dependence upon him, how he is the immediate light of the soul that enlightens it and fills it with divine light and saving knowledge, and how he makes application thereby of the benefits of Christ's redemption.

There belongs to him therefore our dependence upon him, as being him by whom we nextly and most immediately have all spiritual blessings. We should look for life and holiness and comfort from the Father, through the Son and by the Spirit. So we should look for sanctifying influences, for supports under temptations and afflictions, for eternal life and heavenly glory from the Father, through the Son and by the Spirit. And as we glorify the Father for {providing the Savior}, and the Son for {procuring salvation}, so we ought to glorify the Holy

-- 436 --

Ghost as he that, by coming into our souls and dwelling there, gives light and knowledge, gives us holiness and makes us happy.

As he is the source and fountain of all holiness, so he is called the Holy Spirit, and upon that account he should be acknowledged and glorified by us. As he gives us those saving convinctions and divine light, so he is called the Spirit of Truth, and should be admired and glorified by us upon that account. As 'tis he that gives all spiritual comfort and delight, so he is called the "Good Spirit" and "the Comforter," and so he should be {glorified by us upon that account}. We ought to be filled with gratitude to the Spirit of God for his wonderful condescension and beneficence in coming to take up his abode in such {poor creatures}, and so honoring of us as to make us his temple and filling of us with such unspeakable riches, such glorious light and precious grace and sweet counsels. And we should glorify him abundantly upon the account of those more perfect degrees which we expect in another world, when his light will be full and like the noonday sun.

Use IV. Of Exam. By this doctrine, let all try their state. Have you…

[Use] V. Let us do what in us lies to be filled with the Spirit, seeing that 'tis by him immediately, and by him only, that those great blessings {are given}. Let none content themselves without having his indwelling {presence}, without having such a glorious principle of life and action in their hearts.

And let us strive for greater and greater degrees of it. Let it be considered that he that has most of the Spirit will have most {of God}. He that hath most of the Spirit, hath most of God, is most like Christ, has most comfort and joy in the world, and lives most to God's glory; and not only so, but he that hath most of the Spirit here in this world, and has most of the exercises and fruits of it, will have most of it also in the other world. Though there all will have it to such perfection as to exclude anything of the contrary, yet some will have it in greater measure than others, and those that have [it] in greater degree then will shine brightest, "as the brightness of the firmament; and they that turn many to righteousness as the stars forever and ever" [Daniel 12:3].2

That we may so be, let us carefully avoid all things by which it may be quenched. Let us long and thirst for it. Let [us] cherish and yield to and follow all his motions. Let us pray for the Spirit.
