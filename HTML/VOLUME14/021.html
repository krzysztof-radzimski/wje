God's All-Sufficiency for the Supply of Our Wants

In this thanksgiving-day sermon, Edwards turns what would appear to be an expected sort of message for such an occasion—God's bounty toward humankind—into a meditation on the wonders of creation. The sermon seems to have had its immediate inspiration in an effort to explain how God "orders" rain to fall on a drought-stricken land. This question irresistibly leads Edwards to explore the interconnected workings of the cosmos. In doing so, and in keeping with his deliberately "modest" literary style, he hides a great deal of learning in his statements, particularly his exposure to the works of scholars such as John Ray and Thomas Burnet, who were interested in demonstrating how God's wisdom is evident in nature.1

Edwards also demonstrates his concurrence with a preeminent theological assumption of the eighteenth century by assuming that the laws of nature and the laws of reason are one. There is a universal harmony, he maintains, created and supervised by God, that exists between all things for the benefit of humankind. This sermon therefore reflects Edwards' interest in natural philosophy, evident in such early writings as "Of Being," "Of Atoms," and "The Mind."2 Yet Edwards distinguishes himself from other evangelical apologists in the debate against natural theology by his dispositional ontology. A universal harmony prevails because God, who is omnipotent and consonant, communicates himself in the manifold aspects of creation. Edwards states that God's "fullness of power" is such that, "let things be great or small, they are all one to his power." Herein Edwards imparts his personal vision of the magnificence of God.

Though Edwards was fully acquainted with the Copernican view of

-- 472 --

the solar system as well as the works of Newton and his expositors, he nonetheless retains references in this sermon to the Ptolemaic cosmology.3 Why Edwards does this is uncertain; perhaps his congregation was still not ready to accept modern theory,4 or perhaps he deliberately subordinated scientific fact to the theological truth that humankind is the focal point of creation and that God arranges everything for humankind's benefit. However, Edwards does incorporate current learning, such as Newton's Optics (1686) and Locke's Essay Concerning Human Understanding (1690), when in his discussion of the human body he calls sight the chief of the senses.

In the manner of John Flavel, Richard Steele, and Cotton Mather,5 Edwards spiritualizes reality as he tries to show how meaning can be drawn from "the book of nature" as well as "the book of grace." Psalms 65, the text of the sermon, was probably written after the end of a drought; in New England, it seems, the year 1729 was without rain. Several sermons besides this one use imagery that testifies to such a condition.6 The sun's heat and light, the seasonal growth of vegetation, and the flowing river have for Edwards particular spiritual significances, as recorded in "Images" nos. 13–15, written shortly before or concurrent with this sermon. In addition, the significance of the yearly propagation of vegetation, as explored in "Images" no. 19, is also

-- 473 --

discussed here. Yet Edwards, whether talking about hellfire and an enraged God or about the bounty of nature and a benevolent God, always ends up at the same point: the free mercy of God to a sinful people. All humankind, he concludes, can and must drink at God's overflowing fountain of grace.

The date of the colony-wide thanksgiving for 1729, the occasion for which this sermon was most likely preached, was November 13. As was his habit, Edwards only implies the occasion by making a passing mention in the Application to "the custom of the country to have a day of thanksgiving once a year."

Though the regular separation of Explication, Doctrine, and Application obtains, the sermon is structurally distinctive for its lack of detailed, formal analysis of propositions in heads and subheads. In this regard, the sermon verges on being an essay.

God's All-Sufficiency for the Supply of Our Wants Psalms 65:9 Thou visitest the earth, and waterest it: thou greatly enrichest it with the river of God, which is full of water: thou preparest them corn, when thou hast so provided for it.

This psalm seems to be written upon occasion of some special mercies being shown to the children of Israel, in answer of prayers, after some great and general calamity. And, by the latter part, it seems to have been the sending of rain and giving fruitful seasons after some great drought or famine. This mercy is elegantly described in our text and the following verses.

He "enricheth the earth with the river of God, that is full of water"; that is, God never wants water to supply the clouds and to pour upon the dry and thirsty ground. God's river is always full: he can, when he pleases, cause the heavens to yield a plentiful supply for the earth. The water is continually, from year to year, from the beginning of the world to the end, coming down upon the earth; but yet God's river is not empty, but is as full as ever. God's wisdom has so contrived for the continual supply of the earth, and his goodness is so abundant in it, that it is like a river ever-flowing.

This is a beautiful representation not only of [the] greatness, fullness and constancy of God's bounty in giving rain, but also in other things, in the yearly and daily mercies which he bestows upon us.

Doctrine.
God, by the exercises of his common bounty towards men, shows that he has an all-sufficiency for the supply of their wants.

I. He thereby shows the sufficiency of his power to provide for mankind all that they stand in need of. We may justly argue from the

-- 475 --

greatness of God's power appearing in providing those things that are for the support and comfort of our lives, that he is able to do every-thing that we need. His power in those things appears plainly infinite.

He shows his infinite power in creating those things which subserve to our necessity and delight: in creating the earth for us to dwell upon, and the air for us to breath in and water for our drink; in creating the visible heavens and heavenly bodies to shed down their influences upon [us]. He stretches out the heavens like a curtain, and spreads them out like a tent to dwell in [Isaiah 41:22]. God's power as easily extended that vast expanse of the starry heavens as we can spread a curtain, or as a man can set up a tent. Job 26:7, Job 26:13, "He stretcheth out the north over the empty place, and his hands have formed the crooked serpent"; that is, the constellation in the heavens called "The Serpent."7

He shows his almighty strength in forming and fixing those vast globes over our heads, that are many of them far greater, and some of 'em a thousand times greater, than the whole globe of the earth; in fixing a tabernacle for the sun, as it is expressed (Psalms 19:4); and in forming that endless variety of creatures that subserve to our benefit.

And God shows that nothing is too hard for him to do for us by the strength [of his arm] in so ordering, moving and directing those things so as to make them administer to our good, particularly in moving and managing the heavenly bodies so that they may be under advantages in the best manner to diffuse their light and heat all over the face of the earth. What a mighty strength is required to manage the system of the world, to fix the ordinances of heaven and keep the mighty heavenly bodies in their swift and regular courses! God moves those huge globes and keeps [them] in play in their appointed circles from age to age, from one thousand years to another, only by the word of his power. Though they are such vast things, yet they are not unwieldy in God's hands; though their course is so mighty swift, yet God easily keeps them within their bounds and makes them observe his rules.

The sun, at God's command, "is as a bridegroom coming out of his chamber, and rejoices as a strong man to run his race. His going forth is from the end of heaven, and his circuit unto the ends of it"; and by this means, "nothing is hid from the heat thereof" (Psalms 19:5–6).

God commands the morning, and by his mighty power makes the dayspring to know its place (Job 38:12). And he turns the earth to the

-- 476 --

sun as clay to the seal, and the things that are upon it stand as a garment (Job 38:14)—that is, in the night the face of the earth is undistinguished, all confused, like clay before it has had the impression of the seal, without those various and beautiful appearances which are seen upon it when the sun comes to shine upon it and as it were like a seal impresses those figures upon it—and then those appearances of the green fields and trees, they appear as a beautiful garment to cover the earth.

God by his mighty power orders the various seasons. When the sun has got to its appointed bounds towards the north or south, at God's command it returns again; it never transgresses his law. God by his power doth that which none other can do: he binds the sweet influences of Pleiades and looses the bands of Orion (Job 38:31). 'Tis by his power that the bands of the winter are loosed in the spring, and the snow and ice are dissolved. And the earth, which before was as a stone, is opened and mollified, and the sweet influences of Pleiades are shed abroad, and the earth brings forth; the pastures are clothed with green grass, and the trees bud forth and put on their beautiful garments, and all nature is renewed and revived. And again, those sweet influences, they are bound again when winter comes on with the bands of Orion by God's mighty power, and for the good of mankind and in fulfillment of his gracious promise to Noah and his posterity. Genesis 8:22, "While the earth remaineth, seed time and harvest, and cold and heat, and summer and winter, and day and night shall not cease."

God shows his great power in sending abroad at such a prodigious distance so powerful a light and heat from the sun, which is so great in its influences all over the face of the earth. How mighty and powerful is that globe of fire through so vast regions of the universe, and shows the all-sufficient power of him that kindled it, and that maintains its light and heat!8

God shows his great power in ordering of the atmosphere, or that body of air that encloses this globe and the things contained in it, for our good, as in raising and sustaining in the air such vast quantities of water in the clouds. He calls for the waters of the sea, and the waters of the sea obey him and mount up at9 his command, and he poureth it out on the dry land. Amos 5:8, "Seek him that maketh the seven stars

-- 477 --

and Orion, and turneth the shadow of death into the morning, that maketh the day dark with night: that calleth for the waters of the sea, and poureth them out upon the face of the earth: the Lord is his name."

God shows his great power in the winds, in moving such great portions of the atmosphere with such rapidity, whereby he fans and cleanses the air; and wafteth to and fro the clouds, those great quantities of waters above the firmament, whither he pleases they should go. Proverbs 30:4, "Who hath gathered the winds in his fists?"

He shows his mighty power in thunder and lightning, whereby he cleanses and sweetens the air. Job 26:14, "The thunder of his power who can understand?"

God shows his great power in moving and managing the sea, that huge collection of waters, for the good of mankind, especially in the tides. He hereby shows he has his way in the sea, and his path in the great waters (Psalms 77:19).

God, by these and other exercises of his power for our good, shows that he has a fullness of power. He that can do these things, he can't want any power: he shows that he has all creatures in his hands and at his dispose; he shows that, let things be great or small, they are all one to his power; he shows that his power is sufficient to supply all our wants, because when we stand in need of so many and so great things as those that have been mentioned for our support and comfortable subsistence, he that has done and daily doth such great things for us is doubtless able to [do] all other things.

II. God, by the exercises of his common bounty towards men, shows that he has a fullness of wisdom to prepare and order all things for us as we stand in need. As God has shown infinite power in the creating [of] those things that minister to our advantage, so he has shown an equal wisdom in contriving and ordering of them. Infinite wisdom has been set an work how to provide for men in abundance of instances.

Thus when God was about to create man, and it appeared needful to him that he should have light—mankind could not subsist in perfect darkness—then the question was, How mankind should be provided with light that should sufficiently enlighten the habitable world? And God's wisdom contrived the sun, a vast globe and fountain of light, so placed and ordered that it should enlighten one half of the globe at once. But then here would be another question: How all parts of the globe of the earth should be provided with light? And God therefore contrived the diurnal revolution of the sun round the earth in perpetual

-- 478 --

successions, which also served many other purposes: it avoided the fatal inconvenience that would have arisen from the sun's always standing over one side of the earth; it would have burnt it up. And then by those revolutions, man had a more convenient time for that rest and sleep which God saw needful for man. And because it would be inconvenient for man to be wholly without light in the night, God therefore contrived the moon and stars, which should give so much light as to serve the necessary occasions that man might have of passing to and fro in the night, and yet not interrupt his quiet rest.

When God saw it necessary that the body of man should be continually recreated by proper nourishment, then God contrived a way that the earth should bring forth all things suitable to that. So the earth by God's wisdom brings forth abundance of [all] kinds of things for his nourishment and refreshment, some meet to be received immediately, others first to be turned into the flesh of other animals and so be prepared for man's food.

And when God saw it necessary that these fruits of the earth should be propagated to all ages of the world, God contrived that when one plant had brought forth its proper fruit, it should bring forth also a seed, from whence another might spring of the same kind. And God, seeing that those seeds would not spring and grow and bear fruit without some heat and warmth to quicken and enliven it and put it forward, God therefore for this end contrived the sun, which by its heat should cause the grass, herbs, and corn and trees to flourish and grow.

And when God saw that it was not sufficient that there should be merely warmth without moisture,1 now the question was, How the whole face of the earth should be watered from time to time in a convenient manner? In order to this, there must be some way or other contrived how vast quantities of water might be suspended up at some distance above the face of the earth, and might be carried about hither and thither according as God saw meet. And God has contrived a wonderful way for this purpose: he contrived the sun, that by its heat it might cause the water to ascend from the sea in great quantities and to be so purified that it might be suspended in the air, and collected in clouds and condensed in them, and from them descend upon the earth to water it. And God hath so contrived that the water should [not] come down in such great bodies, which would be very incommodious

-- 479 --

and would dash in pieces the fruits of the earth; but that it should distil gently in drops so that we can walk amongst it, and the fruits of the earth are not hurt, Job 36:27–29, "He maketh small the drops of water: they pour down rain according to the vapor thereof; which the clouds do drop and distil upon man abundantly. Also can any understand the spreadings of the clouds, or the noise of his tabernacle?" Thus, according to the text and context, God visits the earth and watereth it and greatly enriches it with the river of God, which is full of water, and prepares corn when he has so provided for it; he waters the ridges thereof abundantly; he settles the furrows thereof; he makes it soft with showers; he blesses the springing thereof [Job 36:9–10]. And very wonderful must the inward make and formation of the plant itself be, that only with those influences of the sun and rain, etc. it should of itself grow in such form and produce such fruit, and bear also another seed that should produce the like again, and so forever.2

God saw that man and beast would stand in necessity of water where they dwelt. And God therefore, besides the rain, contrived that water should be distributed in springs and rivers all over the face of the earth.

And in order to there being a sufficient supply for the clouds and rivers, God saw it needful that there should be a vast collection of waters. God hath therefore provided the seas, which are also exceeding convenient to mankind for transportation and upholding a communication with the distant parts of the world. And that this great body of waters might not grow corrupt by stagnation, and so by infecting the air become fatal to the inhabitants of the earth, God contrived that the sea should be salt and be kept in continual motion by the tides.

And because God saw that the water of the rain and that which is for ordinary use of man and beast should be fresh, God therefore hath contrived that although the most from whence all comes is salt, yet that the waters should be made fresh in the clouds and springs.

God saw that we should for many ends stand in necessity of something else of a very different nature for the preparing of our food, and

-- 480 --

the defending of us from the cold, and many other uses. And God's wisdom invented for us that wonderful thing, fire.

And God hath shown a marvelous wisdom in providing for us in the make of our bodies. God saw it necessary for us in this world that we perceive things at a distance from us, and he hath contrived those wonderful organs, the eyes, which are most curious pieces of divine workmanship.3 And not much less wonderful are those organs by which we perceive sounds, even the ears; very wonderful is God's contrivance for the conveying of sounds. And so God hath contrived organs of speech for the expedite communicating of our thoughts one to another. And every part of the human body is of a piece with these: the body of man is a world of wonders, and has more contrivance in it than all the pieces of human art that ever were in the world put together.

But it would take up volumes to mention the tenth part of the instances of wonderful divine wisdom that are to be seen in the provision God has made in his works for the good of man. But by this specimen, we may see reason to be convinced that nothing is too hard for the wisdom of God that we stand in need of.

III. God hereby shows that he has all-sufficiency of goodness for the supply of our wants. If God has shown such an all-sufficient power and wisdom in thus providing for us, then surely his goodness is all-sufficient, that inclined thus to exert his power and wisdom for our good in so many wonderful instances. But here,

First, the plenty and variety of the common bounty of God to us shows the all-sufficiency of his goodness. Heaven and earth is full of God's bounty: when we look upon the sun, moon and stars, we may behold them as so many wonderful gifts of God to us. The earth and sea are themselves gifts of God's bounty to mankind, and all the fullness of them. The face of the earth is covered with a vast variety of the gifts of God to us, and the sea is filled with them, and the bowels of the earth. What a vast plenty and variety is there for our necessity and delight!

Here is enough for the supply of all mankind, the many millions of

-- 481 --

men that are in the world. Yea, there is an abundance; there is more than enough. We are needy, empty creatures; we are full of our wants. But yet there are none of the needs of this life but that are provided for. God brings it out of his treasures. God's river is full of water, and not only so, but it overflows all its banks. All the many thousands and millions of mankind may drink and quench their thirst at this river.

Second. By the constancy of the supplies which he grants, he shows by this that his goodness is everlasting; it endureth forever. God is never weary of being bountiful. In this respect, his goodness is very fitly compared to a river. A river is never weary of flowing: although it empties vast quantities of water every day, yet there seems to be never the less to come. One may live by a river and be supplied with water one's whole lifetime; there is no danger of spending of it up, or of its running all away.4

How constantly does God supply the wants of mankind. It is wonderful to consider in some instances, as in that instance of the sun's light and heat. The sun has been sending out its rays and shedding of them abroad in vast profusion from the beginning of the world, and yet its rays ben't spent; it is as full of them, probably, as ever; it shines with a constant and uninterrupted brightness, continually still. As this is a notable effect of the fullness and everlastingness of God's bounty, so 'tis very much of an image of it: for God is "the Father of lights, with whom is no variableness, neither shadow of turning" [James 1:17].

Again, how unwearied is the sun in its course. Though the world be so wicked and so full of provoking rebels against heaven, yet God don't cease to cause the sun to arise upon them, which ariseth on the just and unjust. How unwearied is the earth in bringing forth for men's food and clothing: if it were not for God's power and efficiency, it would soon cease.

Application.

I. How greatly are the generality of men to blame, that they reflect so little upon the daily goodness of God to 'em. If God shows so much power and exercises so much wisdom in so plentifully, variously and continually providing for us, as we have now heard, how stupid are we, that we have no more thought about it. How seldom does it come into our thoughts when we look abroad in the world, and see the light of the sun and see the trees and fields, that these things are what God has

-- 482 --

taken care to provide for the benefit of mankind? When we see the variety that there is upon the face of the earth of different sorts of good things suitable to our different needs, how seldom does such a reflection as this come across us? What care has God taken of mankind, that they should not want anything that [is] for their convenience.

And when we gather in the fruits of the earth, and when we eat and drink, how insensible are [we]. How seldom do we think on't that it was God's wisdom that contrived this for our comfort? Yea, are there not some that breath and walk and eat and drink like beasts, and don't think seriously of its being God's goodness that they live upon not once in a week?

If a king should take a poor man from the street that had nothing to eat or wear, and should build a stately house for him with everything convenient for his necessity and comfort within doors and without, and should every day, as duly as the day came,5 send him provision and everything he needed, and so as long as he lived: would it not be a stupid thing in the man if he never or seldom reflected upon it, that it was this king's gracious kindness that he lived upon?

The very cattle and dogs and other brute creatures seem to be more sensible of man's kindness to 'em, than some men do of the kindness of God. Isaiah 1:3, "The ox knows his owner, and the ass his master's crib: but Israel doth not know, my people doth not consider."

'Tis true, 'tis a general custom for men to return thanks after they have set down to meat, and 'tis the custom of the country to have a day of thanksgiving once a year; but how little does it signify if it be nothing but a mere formality, and done with as little reflection of mind and motion of heart about the goodness [of God] as a harp or flute, or something that makes a sound without life?

II. From this doctrine we learn what improvement we should make of the works of nature. We should improve to learn the all-sufficiency of God: it appears both in the works of nature and the works of grace, and the book of nature and the book of grace [and] of revelation both teach us this lesson. We should therefore read in both of them.

We may find suitable matter enough to employ our thoughts at all times, wherever we turn our eyes. When we are travelling, or when at work abroad, we should still be a-reading to learn God's all-sufficiency.

III. If God by the exercises of his common {bounty towards men

-- 483 --

shows that he has an all-sufficiency for the supply of their wants}, then what encouragement is here to look to God and trust in him for whatever we stand in need of. God shows us every day, if we did but consider it, by his daily goodness to us, that he is able to do everything for us that we have need to have done, and that he is wise enough to contrive for us in all difficulties and good enough to give us sufficient for our needs. He shows that he delights in goodness and in providing for the good of man. We see the daily fruits of it, besides what we are taught about it in the Word of God.

Therefore whatever your wants are, be directed to go to this fountain. If your wants are outward, if you stand in need of things for your comfortable subsistence, go to him who hath promised that all those things should be added to you if you seek first the kingdom {of God}. If you want health, if you want ease, if you want for yourself, if you want for your children, go [to] that river of God's bounty that is so full of water. Don't be anxious about those things, but go to God and leave your case with and cheerfully wait in a way of well-doing for what he shall see meet to provide for you.

If your wants are spiritual (as all our souls are in themselves exceeding poor, necessitous things), here is encouragement to go to him and trust in him. Those manifestations of power that were mentioned, as his {plenty, abundance, constancy and all-sufficiency}, may convince us of his ability to redeem our souls from all adversity.

And his wisdom in so contriving {for the supply of our wants} may convince us of the sufficiency of his wisdom to contrive for us and guide us in the way of peace. And he whose goodness provided in such plenty and vast variety for our bodies, though we are sinful creatures, shall he not much rather provide for our souls, our more necessitous parts?

The sun's fullness of light is but an image, a faint resemblance of God's fullness of grace. As the sun's light and influence endures, so God's grace is forever and ever.

IV. We should be stirred up by this doctrine to a constant serving of God and seeking his glory. He that is continual and constant and abundant {in supplying for our wants} abundantly deserves our hearty and cheerful service, and that we should [do] with all our hearts. If God so greatly and so variously improves his power, we should [improve] our power. If God so exercises of his wisdom, if God so constantly and richly gives us his goodness, we should serve him without intermission and perseveringly through all difficulties.
