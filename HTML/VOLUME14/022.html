Sin and Wickedness Bring Calamity and Misery on a People

The occasion of this sermon, a fast day, was an opportunity for Edwards to try his hand at the seasoned American Puritan formula of the jeremiad, in which the minister attempted to turn his listeners from declension and to repentance by listing the many sins of which they were guilty. Like generations of New England preachers before him, Edwards believed that God ordained both private and public judgments. Private judgments were the kind usually associated with the final, cataclysmic day when God would judge "particular persons" on the basis of individual claims to the covenant of grace. Public judgments, however, were based on a temporal or corporate covenant. In the national covenant, "a people as a people"—such as the ancient Israelites or the inhabitants of eighteenth-century New England— were rewarded with prosperity according to their obedience to God's will and punished according to their disobedience. It is the latter type of judgment that Edwards is talking about here.

In the classic jeremiadic mode, Edwards counts down the many sins that "denominate" a people a "wicked" people. These include heresy, luxury, sloth, drunkenness, and vice, which bring disease, poverty, weakness, contention, injustice, corrupt government, and even barbarity. In these, Edwards states, the colonies are sharers with "the nation," the kingdom of Great Britain. In the Application, he discusses the particular sins of Massachusetts. First, he sounds an apocalyptic note by declaring that the "latter times" will be immediately preceded by rampant wickedness among humans and by severe judgments from God. Such judgments have recently been seen in the form of epidemics, fires, storms, droughts, and earthquakes (the most recent just two years before). These are sure signs that God is angry with the land and that its inhabitants have departed from the ways of their forebears.

Stringing together rhetorical interrogatives and exclamations, Edwards then points to the sins of the townspeople. Profaneness is evident in the neglect and abuse of the Sabbath; he singles out young

-- 485 --

people in the congregation for talking and laughing during worship. Enforcing the social mores of deference, Edwards points out that pride is evident in the way more and more people are purchasing fancy homes, clothing, and possessions, "going far beyond their condition" and station in society. He condemns the covetousness of the marketplace, in which businessmen do all they can to deceive their neighbors and get the upper hand. Drunkenness and uncleanness among young people especially disturb Edwards, as he laments that the liberties taken by the youth of the day would be looked on elsewhere "as sufficient evidences of a prostitute." Finally, he joins the majority of the clergy of his province in placing the blame for such evils on the loosening of the strictures of family government. If it is true that parents of yesterday were too strict, he states, "parents nowadays err much more on the other extreme."

This sermon, written in October or November 1729, was delivered on a fast day, in all probability before assembled townspeople, local dignitaries, and members of the Hampshire judiciary during the session of the county court at Northampton, which met on December 2–4 and 10.1 At several points in the sermon Edwards alludes to men of political and ecclesiastical standing under the general description of "persons of public influence." Only in the very last sentence comes the recognition of the occasion, as Edwards simply enjoins his listeners to pray for the reformation of the land "not only on fast days," but continually.

∗ ∗ ∗

The manuscript is composed of sixteen duodecimo leaves with an additional salvaged fragment, folded over and stitched to the preceding leaves. On one side of this fragment is a "bid prayer," a request by a layperson given to Edwards to be used in the pastoral prayer.2 Edwards used the reverse side to complete the Application.

-- 486 --

Edwards repreached this sermon at least twice. Beside the initial quotation of the Scripture text, he wrote in shorthand: "Preached part of this the second time." Above the text on the first page, he later wrote a note indicating that he again repreached the sermon in April 1753 to the Stockbridge Indians. There are no internal indications, such as the customary brackets, to show what parts he repreached, and there are no later revisions.

Sin and Wickedness Bring Calamity and Misery on a People Proverbs 14:34.3 Righteousness exalteth a nation: but sin is a reproach to any people.

This verse, as most of the rest of these divine proverbs, consists of opposite predications on opposite qualities and persons. Here we may observe,

1. The two opposite qualities are righteousness and sin. The righteousness spoken of, being a public righteousness, may be extended so far as to take in all moral virtues and external religion, as well as sincere holiness; and sin is to be understood in a sense answerable for what is opposite to both.

2. We may observe the opposite things that are predicated of these qualities: one exalteth a nation, and the other is a reproach to any people. By "exalting," we are not here merely to understand that it renders a nation or people indeed the more excellent, and so is just cause why they should be more honored, but that it exalts them in prosperity.

So [by] sin's being a reproach, we are to understand not only that it lays a foundation for reproach, as it robs of that which is the greatest excellency of a people—vice will evermore be [a] disgrace—but it brings them to reproach, as it brings them low and exposes them to great calamities. And this I suppose to be what is principally meant.

When a nation is greatly weakened and impoverished and brought into decaying, languishing, calamitous circumstances, they become the object of reproach and contempt among their neighbors. Sin, as it brings 'em to this, is a reproach to any people. [The] doctrine [is] more especially from the last clause:

-- 488 --

Doctrine.
The prevailing of sin and wickedness does exceedingly tend to bring calamity and misery upon any people.

Inq. I. When sin and wickedness may be said to prevail among [a people]. There is abundance of sin and wickedness amongst all people or nations that are or ever were at any time. There is no people or nation but that there are many wicked men amongst them: men that live in wickedness, are haters of God and servants of Satan; men that live in and allow wickedness both of heart and life. Perhaps there never yet has been any nation but what at all times the greater part of them were such as these.

There is a great deal of wickedness in the heart of every one of mankind naturally. And many of those that are what we call "moral men," and those that are truly godly, have abundance of sin in their hearts, and they commit much sin in their lives. But yet in the sense that we intend in the doctrine, a people may be so as not to be said to be a wicked people, or to have sin and wickedness prevailing amongst [them]; but on the contrary, that they should be a people to God's praise. Therefore I answer,

First, when a people are scandalously ignorant, then may it be said that they are a wicked people. This makes a people a wicked people two ways:

[1.] As ignorance of God is in itself wickedness. That is one of the duties that the law of nature requires of man, that he should know God: he has his reason given him for that very end. For although no qualification, action or defect of ours renders us guilty before God, only as the disposition or will has or has had to do in it, and ignorance of divine things is a defect in the understanding rather than the will; yet it was the act of the will, that is imputed to us, that brought ignorance and darkness in spiritual things into the world.

The will concurs in all that are scandalously ignorant, though they have not had advantages and opportunities to know. Amongst the barbarous nations… it falls in with the corrupt disposition of their hearts, and they show it in that they might know much more than they do.

But especially is ignorance in the principles of religion4 wickedness in lands of light, where the ignorance arises from a mere careless

-- 489 --

neglect of those advantages to know which they have and might improve.

[2.] And then when a people are very ignorant, they may be denominated a wicked {people} because there is an open door for all wickedness. As spiritual knowledge is the foundation of all saving religion, so is a common knowledge the foundation of all morality and external religion.

What will those stick at that are ignorant in those doctrines that are the restraints from sin, that enlighten the conscience? Conscience can't restrain if it ben't enlightened; fear won't restrain if they know not what to be afraid of. Lusts exert themselves in their full strength. Thus the children of Israel in Christ's time. Blind guides… took away the key of knowledge (Luke 11:52).

Second. When a people profess wicked principles. 'Tis not every error in religion that is sufficient to denominate a people a wicked people, but they must be fundamental errors, errors that either are contrary to the Christian faith, so as to destroy it, and are inconsistent with a real embracing the gospel, or such as do uphold some scandalous sins. Such are all people that profess heretical doctrines, or are idolatrous or grossly superstitious. The embracing such doctrine is wickedness and gross wickedness, and wickedness can never more be said to prevail or reign than when its foundation is in the judgment: for then, all restraints are wholly gone, conscience itself has its eyes blinded and mouth shut. Therefore when false doctrines are very much prevailing amongst a people, then wickedness may be said to prevail. When atheism or Deism prevails.

Third. When such manners and ways of living as [are] inconsistent with external morality are common amongst a people, then wickedness may be said {to prevail}. A people are not to be called a wicked people, or [be said] to have wickedness prevailing, because they have wickedness in their hearts or defects with respect to aims and ends in what they do. A people as a people are to be denominated from what is visible. When vice overruns a land or town, and becomes a common thing; when the restraints of reputation and the conscience and the laws of men are commonly despised and broke over; when sensuality, profaneness and injustice {prevail, then a people may be said to be a wicked people}. Thus it was in the old world: the sons of God went in to the daughters of men, and the earth was filled with violence (Genesis 6:11–12).

Fourth. Sin and wickedness may be said to prevail {amongst a people}

-- 490 --

when it grows creditable, or at least not very disgraceful, among them; when persons that are vicious and have been guilty of scandalous crimes hold up their heads without blushing—they look upon themselves not much the worse for those crimes that ought to make 'em hold down their heads and to walk softly all their days—and others don't seem to take any great notice of it, don't manifest any great matter of dislike or abhorrence of their crimes in their behavior.

Friends and relations don't mourn much about [it], or think that it is any great calamity has befallen them, when any of the family has fallen into some gross sins.

When such persons as are of openly vicious lives are treated with the same respect as others; persons will as freely associate themselves. So that wickedness is barefaced, and persons make light of it and it is generally excused, and persons give it gentle names and make use of a great deal of complaisance towards it.

Much more does wickedness prevail when wickedness, instead of being disgraceful, is looked upon as creditable. Persons are wont to value themselves upon it and to boast of this and that bold, daring act of wickedness. If it ben't so amongst all sorts, yet if it be so amongst many; if it ben't so amongst ministers and magistrates, yet if there be multitudes of such that seem to take a pride in profaneness and sensuality, and exceeding others in it, and will glory of their achievements in vice; if it ben't so amongst many elderly people, yet if it be amongst young people in their companies: it is a sign that wickedness greatly prevails.

Thus it was amongst the children of Israel in those corrupt and degenerate times, which God complains [of]. Wickedness was not disgraceful among them, neither were they ashamed of it. Jeremiah 6:15, "Were they ashamed when they had committed abomination? nay, they were not at all ashamed, neither could they blush."

This argues not only that sin is very5 common amongst a people, but that they are exceedingly abandoned; that they have so given themselves up that they have wrested out of their minds the natural sense they have about virtue and vice. Men, though they naturally have no true love to virtue, yet the conscience and a sense of shame [are] left in them. But such as these have wrested out those, and have hardened themselves to an unnatural degree.

Fifth. When wickedness and immorality is countenanced or winked at by those whose business it is to suppress it, viz. by the rulers of a people, either civil or ecclesiastical: when civil rulers don't take due

-- 491 --

care to make good laws against immorality or don't take due care to execute the laws, don't show a zeal against iniquities, are no terrors to evil-doers; when the reins of civil government are let loose, and wicked men can be open and barefaced with impunity; ministers don't bear a testimony; and when ecclesiastical discipline is not upheld, but scandalous persons are allowed to come to the Lord's table and to enjoy other privileges of visible Christians.

When they don't set a good example, but rulers themselves are wicked and not conscientious in their lives. However they may seem to discountenance vice and to be zealous in punishing of it, yet it will be to little purpose if they allow themselves in it. If law-makers are also law-breakers, their laws will be but of little force: their examples will have greater influence than their laws or their care to execute them upon others.

Public rulers, they represent the whole people, and if they countenance vice and wickedness, then may wickedness be said to prevail and bear rule amongst them.

It is among a people as it is in the human body: then especially may a disease be said to prevail in the body when it has siezed the more noble and vital parts, so that they don't well do their office. When it is so, the body will be generally diseased, as it was amongst the Jews. Isaiah 1:5–6, "The whole head is sick, and the whole heart is faint. From the sole of the foot even unto the head there is no soundness in it." Their rulers in that time of great corruption countenanced vice and discountenanced virtue. Micah 3:1–2, "Hear, I pray you, O heads of Jacob, and ye princes of the house of Israel; Is it not for you to know judgment? Who hate the good, and love the evil." This was also the case of their prophets and ecclesiastical officers: they did not use effectual means for the suppressing of sin. Jeremiah 6:14, "They healed the hurt of the daughter of my people slightly, saying, Peace, peace; when there is no peace."

When wickedness prevails amongst rulers, it argues a general corruption, because they follow example.

Sixth. When public affairs are wickedly managed, when rulers ben't faithful to the glory and honor of God and the interest of the people that they are set to rule over.

When they seek their own private interest more than the interest of the public; when all their aim is to enrich and advance themselves; when they will make the public weal give place to their own private designs.

When they rule unjustly, oppressing the innocent or justifying the guilty.

-- 492 --

When they are not impartial in judging, but are more influenced by favor or affection than the justice of the cause.

Thus it was in Israel, both amongst civil and ecclesiastical rulers, as God often complains. Isaiah 1:23–24, "Thy princes are rebellious, and companions of thieves: every one loveth gifts, and followeth after rewards: they judge not the fatherless, neither doth the cause of the widow come before them." Isaiah 59:14, "And judgment is turned away backwards, and justice standeth afar off: for truth is fallen in the street, and equity cannot enter." So among their ecclesiastical rulers Jeremiah 6:13, "From the prophet to the priest every one dealeth falsely" And we have like passages in many other places.

[Inq.] II. The prevailing {of wickedness amongst a people} does very much tend to bring a people low and into calamitous and miserable circumstances. It is so both by natural and inevitable tendency, and also by constitution.

First. It doth so by natural tendency. If there were no God that judged the earth, yet such is the nature of vice, that the prevailing of it would have this effect: it would be a reproach to any people, and would reduce them to a low estate.

Sin tends to the temporal ruin of particular persons, but much more inevitably to the ruin of a public society; not only as it directly hurts the particular members, but as it weakens and breaks the bonds of union6instead of making a people subservient to one another's good, which is [the] end of society.

The temporal welfare of a people consists in these following things, viz. in their health and longevity, in their wealth, in their strength and ability to defend themselves, in their peace, in the prevalency of common justice, in public good order and government, in civility and decency. Each of which the prevailing of sin and wickedness amongst a people directly tends to destroy. In the

1. [First place,] the common prevailing of vice amongst a people tends to bring sickness among a people, and to shorten their days. The prevalency of idleness, sensuality, of luxury and intemperance directly tends to this. The indulgence of these lusts tends to spend up the animal spirits, to weaken the frame of the body and to sow the seeds of many diseases. They are as rottenness to the bones.

We look upon it as a sorrowful, calamitous time when it is a sickly and dying time. But when sensuality much overspreads a people, it makes

-- 493 --

sickness and death abundantly more common. Extravagant ways of living has doubtless killed many millions of men.

And only that one vice of excessive drinking, 'tis probability, kills more men in that part of the world that is called "Christian" than both sword and pestilence. Which is probably one reason why in many populous places where an intemperate and luxurious way of living is more common, that there are so few that live to be old, and diseases and death are so much more frequent in proportion to the number of inhabitants than in other places. Solemn observers of virtue [know] that it is life to those that find it, and health to all their flesh (Proverbs 4:22).

2. Vice impoverishes a people. When vice greatly prevails among a people, there are many that spend much of their time idly and un-profitably in tavern-haunting and company-keeping and a-sauntering about from house to house; they spend away their time in idle talk and vain amusements. Especially is it so amongst many of the rich, who give themselves up to an indolent and useless way of living, who spend their whole time eating and drinking, and sleeping and visiting, and taking their ease and pleasures.

They by their idleness cease to be any way benefical members to human society; they are but cumberers of the earth. They impoverish themselves, and often undo their families, and have their wealth, while they have it, to no other purpose but to consume it in vanity. Vice impoverishes a people as it consumes their wealth upon their lust and extravagance, that otherwise might be kept for more profitable uses.

Men's vices often prove very costly things to 'em. How much of the wealth and treasure of the world is expended in this corrupt age in nothing but that which is altogether needless and also very hurtful? How much is expended yearly in this land, for instance, to gratify a useless appetite after strong drink, that might be reserved to be expended much more to the profit of the owners and much more for the good of the public?

3. Vice prevailing greatly weakens a people and makes them far less able to uphold and defend themselves. It weakens them as it enfeebles their bodies, and so renders them the more unfit for the fatigue and hardship and warlike achievements necessary for the defense of a nation.

It weakens them, as it thins their numbers. Proverbs 14:28, "In the multitude of people is the king's honor: but in the want of people is the destruction of the prince."

-- 494 --

It weakens them as it enfeebles their minds and renders them effeminate. It weakens them as it consumes their wealth. The strength and power of a nation or kingdom consists very much in their wealth.

It has ever been found by experience that nations or kingdoms [that] have been very much overrun with luxury and sensuality, that they have declined and have been an easy prey to their enemies.

Thus sin is a reproach to any people, as they thereby lose their power and influence, and so lose their honor and expose themselves to [the] contempt and the insults of their neighbors; and often by this means are reduced from a prosperous and flourishing state, to become subjects and slaves to foreigners' honor.

4. The prevailing of vice makes a people miserable as it destroys their peace. If irreligion and wickedness prevail, then those principles of malice, wrath and revenge will prevail, and then jangling and contention will answerably prevail. Sin and wickedness is ever more the source of contention.

When a people grow very corrupt, then those lusts that are the more immediate springs of contention will abound. There is a concatenation of vices as well as graces. Then pride will abound; men will be too proud to yield one to another. Everyone will be ready to think that they deserve the greatest respect, and will be enraged by the refusal of what they proudly expect. Their high spirits and proud thoughts of themselves will make 'em very quarrelsome and ready to be out of humor with everyone but those that in everything comply with their thoughts and inclinations, and to have an implacable spirit against them that they think have injured [them]. And the sight of such a disposition in them will make others to have the greater aversion to them, and to be more ready to seek their hurt, and to be glad at their calamities. It makes men conceited of their own judgments, so that they won't yield. It makes [them] willful and obstinate.

The more wickedness prevails, the less will those rules of the gospel about meekness, peaceableness, forgiving injuries, not suffering the sun to go down upon our wrath, loving our neighbors as ourselves, loving and blessing our enemies, bearing one another's burdens, looking every one not only at his own {things, but every one also at the things of others}:7 I say the less will they be likely to be regarded.

Men will look every one at his own separate interest, and that will be what will govern him. Whence will abundance of contention arise, it being only their own wealth and their own honor that they look at.

-- 495 --

They will be ready to envy their neighbors, and will be tempted to seek their own interest in a way that others will be displeased with.

And thus comes one of the greatest miseries that can befall a people: to be full of strife and contention. Love and peace is the cement of any society, and the happiness of human society does very much consist in it. How good and how pleasant it is for brethren to dwell together in unity (Psalms 133:1); but they that live in contention, they live a disturbed and miserable life, and under deplorable circumstances is that commonwealth that is full of jealousies, jangling and contention.

5. The prevalency of wickedness tends to overthrow common justice between one man and another.8

The welfare of a people depends very much upon this. If this ben't upheld, men can't subsist one by another. There is no living in society without the observation of common justice and honesty. The safety of men's lives and fortunes, reputations, all their outward possessions, depends upon it. If this were not observed, men would be each other's devourers; they would be like wolves one to another.

But so much the more as sin and wickedness prevails, so much the less will this be upheld, for men will have so much the less of the restraints of religion and conscience; and if men's consciences don't restrain them, there is nothing else will be sufficient to keep them from injuring and doing violence one to another. They will do their neighbors any injury whereby they can any way advantage or gratify themselves. Whenever there is the advantage of secrecy and hopes of not being discovered, men won't scruple to break promises or to take false oaths. Men would not be able to live quietly one by another, because they could put no trust [in] one another.

6. It tends to confound government and good order. Good rulers will not be likely to be found, nor a people be so likely to be unanimous in choosing those that would rule well, if they have to do in choosing of them.

Rulers would be likely to be unfaithful, to oppress those under their power and government, to seek only their own private interest at the expense of the public [and] not to be governed by principles of justice. So much the less will there be of distributive justice. Rulers will turn betrayers of the people's interests.

The subjects, or the ruled, will not pay that obedience and submission

-- 496 --

to authority, but will be rebellious and treacherous towards those that are set over them.

An oath, that is so necessary [an] instrument in government, would be the less regarded.

Ministers and people, and so the very end of people's being united in civil or ecclesiastical societies, will be overthrown, and what was intended for the good of mankind will turn to their hurt.

7. It tends to destroy humanity, civility and common decency. The temporal welfare of a people in great measure consists in these things. A people that are barbarous, inhuman and uncivilized, they are a miserable people. It is a misery for mankind to live together like beasts, with the like stupidity, ignorance and roughness as they; it is a calamity in mankind to be debased in their dispositions and manner below the dignity of the human nature.

But the prevalency of sin and wickedness amongst a people tends more and more to introduce this. Humanity, civility, common decency and religion are so near akin, that if one prevails, the other will prevail, and if the one languishes, the other will decay with it. Beastly lusts, when they get head, will make men of a beastly disposition and behavior all over, especially if they have not those restraints of shame and fear of human punishment, as those restraints are generally greatly weakened. As wickedness generally prevails, wickedness grows bold and barefaced.

The prevailing of wickedness tends to the declining of common civility, as it tends to ignorance. A brutish ignorance will prevail if vice prevails, for men will neglect to seek after knowledge, especially that that is divine or moral, which they find no way subserves to their lusts. Education will be neglected, and children will grow up like the brute creatures, without instruction, and will be answerably barbarous in their manners.

This has been found by experience to be the influence of religion and irreligion. Christianity, where it takes place, civilizes nations, brings them from being like wild beasts to be civilized and polished in their manners. Many [of] the nations of Europe, and our nation, among the rest, before Christianity came amongst them…And when popery was in its height… And how is it now amongst many heathen nations… Thus the prevalence of sin by natural tendency brings a people into calamitous and miserable circumstances.

Second. 'Tis God's manner to bring calamities and misery upon a people in judgment for the prevalency of {wickedness amongst them}.

-- 497 --

'Tis as God has threatened in his holy Word: God rewards a public righteousness with public rewards and punishes9 public iniquity with {public judgments}, which can be done only in this world. In another world, mankind will be rewarded and punished only as particular persons, for the bonds by which they are united in societies will then be dissolved. Though the guilt of all the sin that is in a nation lies upon particular persons, so that it will all be punished in another world, yet a people are punished as a people only in this world, though it may be with spiritual judgments. The prosperity or adversity of a people in this world is much more universally according to the prevalence {of wickedness amongst them} than {the wickedness} of a particular person.

Wickedness provokes God to depart from a people. He withdraws his gracious presence, and ceases graciously to preside over them. And if there ben't the infinitely wise hand of God by his providence to guide the affairs of a people, they will soon run to ruin.

When God is departed from a people, their guard [and] defense is gone. They lie open to every kind of mischief.

A people will be their own destroyers if God forsakes them. Those that should lead them and feed them will devour them, and shepherds will turn into wolves. For the hearts of rulers are in the hand of God; he "turneth wise men backward, and maketh their knowledge foolish" [Isaiah 44:25].

If God be provoked by a people, he can easily reduce them to as low a state and condition as he pleases. The state of kingdoms and nations is altogether at God's disposal.

Application.

[Use] I. We hence learn what cause we have to lament the prevalency of sin and wickedness in our nation. As we are a part of the nation, or of the same body politic, we ought to look upon ourselves as concerned in their guilt and in their calamity. If the nation is subject to calamities and is brought low, our interest is so much embarked with theirs, that we shall feel it as a member feels the disease of the head. We ought therefore greatly to lament the wickedness of the nation.

Our nation was one that was soon enlightened with the gospel in the primitive ages of the church. And again, it was one of those nations that emerged out of popish darkness in the beginning of the Reformation,

-- 498 --

and were distinguished from most other nations of Europe in that the Reformation soon became general in the nation and the authority of the nation soon fell in with it, so that they were able to defend themselves in it. And the Protestant religion has been upheld in the nation to this day, notwithstanding the frequent plots and efforts of those that sought its overthrow. And God several times has preserved the nation and the Protestant religion in it from ruin, when it was upon the very brink of it.

Many have been the special and distinguishing favors that the nation from the first Reformation, and indeed from the first promulgation of the gospel in it, has been blessed with.

But the nation is at this day exceedingly corrupted and degenerated, and by all accounts, they are remarkably so of late years. Amongst all sects and professions, debauchery and wickedness, profaneness and an unbounded licentiousness in sensuality has come in like a torrent and overflowed like a flood.

In the latter times of the nation, there has arisen a new sect of infidels that call themselves Deists, and Freethinkers, that cast off all religion and despise the restraints of it.1 They don't directly deny the being of a God. Formerly, all that pretended to deny the gospel or called in question the truth of the Christian religion, they also denied the being of a God; they never imagined that there was any medium between Christianity and atheism. But atheism has been indisputably confuted by the learning of this age,2 and the being of a God so clearly demonstrated, that no men that pretend to any reason or learning have the face to call it in question.

But these Deists imagine that they have found out a medium. They deny the gospel, ridicule the story about Jesus Christ, and deny the Scripture and all revealed religion, and pretend to own the being of a

-- 499 --

God, and believe what they will about his will or his designs with respect to the future disposal of mankind.

This is grown fashionable in the nations. They glory in it that they have courage enough to cast off the restraints of revelations, and that they dare enjoy their pleasures and give themselves their liberty without being frighted with the threatenings of eternal damnations.

It is grown amongst many in the nation merely fashionable to despise and laugh at religion and godliness and a fear of hell or future judgment. To pretend to a serious regard to the holy Scriptures, or anything of religion, is looked upon as unworthy of a gentleman.

Debauchery is in credit amongst all ranks of persons, and they are remarkably increased in these corruptions lately.

And indeed, religion is at an exceedingly low ebb through the world. There is but very little left of that strict and hearty religion, but little of a zeal for God and godliness. Vital religion is a thing but little talked on, except it be a little in the pulpit, and that much less than it used to be; and there is not only not much of the truth of it, but there is scarce the appearance of it left. There are some: God has a church.

It being so, we have reason to fear what things are coming on the earth. The world, and particularly the nation, has now been degenerating a great while. 'Tis to be feared that God will soon look upon them as ripe for destruction.

We hope that there are glorious times near…. And we may hope that God won't suffer the world to decline much more, that the darkest time of night is come, and that it will not be long before break of day.

But whenever those times come, we may learn by the Word of God that they will be introduced with most terrible judgments on the ungodly, before the harvest that be the vintage. Revelation 14, and [the] latter end: "And another angel came out from the altar, which had power over fire; and cried with a loud cry to him that had the sharp sickle, saying, Thrust in thy sharp sickle, and gather the clusters of the vine of the earth; for her grapes are fully ripe. And the angel thrust in his sickle into the earth, and gathered the vine of the earth, and cast it into the great winepress of the wrath of God. And the winepress was trodden without the city, and blood came out of the winepress, even unto the horse bridles, by the space of a thousand and six hundred furlongs" [Revelation 14:18–20].

There probably will come a remarkable time of trial before those glorious days, wherein God's judgment will be in a terrible manner executed on ungodly men. Those blessed times will be ushered in with

-- 500 --

a great earthquake, such as has not been since men have been upon the earth, so mighty an earthquake, and so great (Revelation 16:18); that is, there will be a very great manifestation of God's power and wrath in those sudden changes and vast revolutions that he will cause in the world and doubtless there will many of the wicked be overthrown and swallowed up in that earthquake.

And the more corrupt and wicked the world is grown before the introduction of those times, the more of God's wrath will be shown before the introduction of 'em. The heavens and earth will be shaken, that the wicked may be shaken out of them, and that those that are built upon such a foundation that they cannot be shaken may remain. God's fire will pass over the earth, whereby the wicked shall be consumed and the godly be tried and refined. Doubtless God has judgments in reserve for the wicked world, and how near the time for the pouring of them out is, we know not. God don't sit in heaven and see all these provocations, and see wickedness reign and rage at this rate, and his wrath and his thunder all the while lie asleep.

How ought all those therefore that would be partakers in those vials which God has to pour out upon the earth to lament the wickedness of it, and particularly the wickedness of our own nation.

Use II. [We shall] say something of our duty respecting the prevalency of sin and wickedness in this land, to lament and be humbled for it. But,

First, we will briefly take a view of the state of the land with respect to our declensions and the prevailing of sin and wickedness in it. This land but a little while ago was a howling wilderness inhabited by wild beasts, and by a barbarous people but not very different from them. Our forefathers that first settled the country. Religious design. Fled from persecution. Leave religion as a legacy to their posterity.3

God was wonderful in his mercy towards our forefathers. The heathen have strangely dwindled away to make room. And though there are some few remains of 'em still, yet they have been driven out for the time as thoroughly as the inhabitants of Canaan were to make room for the children of Israel.

And however there were some superstitious errors in the country at first that are since generally exploded,4 yet the land was justly renowned for religion and piety. It was vastly otherwise amongst rulers

-- 501 --

and ruled, parents and children, young and old, than now it is. The country has quite another face, and we have of late revolted apace, and have corrupted ourselves more and more. Religion is less and less our business and concern; the world engrosses the thought and concern and talk of the land, and profaneness and pride and sensuality lift up their head, are very common and appear more and more openly and barefaced.

1. Profaneness. Profaneness consists in a behavior that shows a contempt of holy things, and is particularly opposite to that part of our duty that immediately relates to the worship and service of God. "Profane" signifies "common," being that whereby men treat sacred things as though they were common things and deserved no special regard.

How much is there of this in the land, which appears in a general carelessness and neglect of religion, a disregarding of the appointed means of salvation and supine neglect of converting grace? How little is this men's concern? How little appearance or shadow of any such thing is to be seen as one travels about the country? And how great a neglect of it here even amongst ourselves?

How many, on the contrary, that are ready to laugh at such things, and at those that appear concerned about them?

How much is there of swearing and a profane way of talking in many parts of the land?

How exceedingly are we declined with respect to the observation of the sabbath! How much is the sabbath encroached upon at its beginning, and how poorly spent during the time of its continuance? How little conscience do many show about what they talk on [on] a sabbath? Not only their hearts, but their mouths are as open for worldly matters on sabbath days as on other days. They think their own that speak… and do find their…

And how much profaneness is there, even in God's house in the very time of worship in some congregations? And5 this especially 'tis observable, that many of our young people are very commonly talking and laughing while in the immediate presence of God almighty, and pretend to a worshipping of him; which is a most scandalous practice, and that which ought not to be tolerated in any society of Christians.

2. Pride and vanity. How excessively do many affect to make a flaunting appearance in their buildings and apparel and way of living, many going far beyond their condition or what would be suitable for them?

-- 502 --

They exercise their care about outward ornaments, which ought to [be] employed about the ornaments of the mind; but the soul is neglected, and is like the inside of [a] sepulchre, full of all manner of filth and putrefaction.

3. Covetousness. How exceedingly does this prevail; how indefatigable are men in endeavoring to increase their estates, to grow rich and great; how busily are they employed? What a stir do they make about it, while they neglect their own souls and the souls of their children! How evident is it by but a little acquaintance and a slight view of the land, to see that the world is what men make their great concern?

How many unjust things are done for the sake of worldly gain? Men will be guilty of oppression; they will play little tricks to deceive their neighbors, to advance their own interest. How many men are there that one cannot trust in and depend upon; how few are there that are altogether just or that observe that rule of doing to others {as they would have them do unto them}?

4. How much drunkenness is there in the land? What multitudes that haunt and throng public houses from day to day? What a deluge of strong drink is yearly drunk in the country?

5. And how much is there of uncleanness? The land is vastly corrupted as to this sin within this few years. Young people take more and more of a licentious liberty in their keeping company.

[How] bold and daring! 'Tis amazing to consider that such things have got such head, and that they are so impudent in their impurities.

And there is not that discountenance of such things as there formerly used to be. It is not now such a discredit; 'tis not accounted such a blot and disgrace to a person. Formerly, things were accounted such a wound as a person never could get over as long as he lived. That ben't much minded: now they are so bold and impudent, that they are not ashamed to hold up their heads.

And parents are very much to blame in its being thus. There are those practices that parents commonly allow of that lead to uncleanness, that is so evident to the common light of mankind, that such things are looked upon as shameful and disgraceful at Canada, and at New York [and] England. I believe there is not a country in the Christian world, however debauched and vicious, where parents indulge their children in such liberties in company-keeping as they do in this country—that is, amongst those that pretend to keep the credit of their children. Such things as are commonly winked at by parents

-- 503 --

here, trusting in their children that they won't give way to temptation, would in almost any other country ruin a person's reputation and be looked upon as sufficient evidences of a prostitute. This brings me to the next particular, viz.

6. The vast declension in family education and government. How little care do parents take in instructing {and} governing {their children}. Parents say that their forefathers were too strict, that they used to lay too great restraints upon their children, that they were too severe in governing of 'em. But if it was so, parents nowadays err much more on the other extreme: they give their children too vast liberties.

7. In the prevailing of a contentious spirit. Which appears in men's so much going to law one with another.

And 'tis especially to be lamented that there is so much contention amongst our rulers. Some or others of them must be exceedingly to blame.

Are not these things exceedingly to be lamented? Especially consider the greatness of our obligation to God: we have been a distinguished people by the mercies we have received in our settlement here. And ever since, we greatly increased and prospered; we have enjoyed great privileges. God has owned [us] for his people, but we have done as the children of Israel. Deuteronomy 32:15–17, "But Jeshurun waxed fat, and kicked: thou art waxen fat, thou art grown thick, thou art covered with fatness; then he forsook God which made him, and lightly esteemed the Rock of his salvation. They provoked him to jealousy with strange gods, with abominations provoked they him to anger. They sacrificed unto devils, not to God; to gods whom they knew not, to new gods that came newly up, whom your fathers feared not."

We should mourn for it by reason of those calamities which the wickedness of the land has already brought upon it, and considering how many more we lie exposed to. We should every one of us mourn for the prevailing of {wickedness amongst us} because we, amongst the rest, have contributed to it, and we ought to reflect and consider how great a share we have had in it. And we know not how great a share we may have in public calamities and judgments. When wickedness greatly prevails, every member of the commonwealth will have his share.

And those that have had the least share {in the prevailing of wickedness} should lament it not only from respect to the glory of God, which is injured thereby, and to the public, which should be nigh at heart, but

-- 504 --

from respect to themselves. For those that have done least, they suffer; they are often sufferers in the common calamities. The whole land suffers for public crimes, or rather upon occasion of public crimes, though there may be many that have not been active in them nor have any otherwise been partakers than6 only by virtue of the public covenant.7 And as one sin carries in it an implicit consent to another, so8 [it] encourages another by reason of the likeness and nearness of nature of all sins.

But yet 'tis very just that the whole should suffer upon occasion of public crimes. For every member has deserved so great and greater suffering, and God sees fit in his wisdom to bring the punishment of them upon us upon that occasion.

But then those temporal evils that are the natural fruit of the prevailing {of wickedness amongst a people} are nothing to those spiritual evils which they bring in ruining the souls of a people, and giving Satan, the grand adversary of mankind, the dominion over them: the departure of God's Spirit and the giving of 'em up to sin. If another world was as open to our view as this, all temporal evils that can befall a people would appear but very small in comparison of those spiritual judgments which issue in the misery of that eternal world.

Second. Let us be exhorted to take heed that we don't contribute to the prevailing {of wickedness amongst us}. There are various ways that persons may contribute to it. Every man that openly lives a wicked life, or9 that openly commits any one act of wickedness, he contributes to the prevailing {of wickedness amongst a people}, etc., i.e. he does that that has a tendency so to do. His wickedness appearing strengthens the causes, as the addition of a soldier strengthens an army: thereby their consent is given. It helps to make wickedness the more common, whereby it becomes less disgraceful in the eyes of men, and the example tends to draw others on.

But those persons that are of public influence had especially need to be careful they don't contribute. If men live wicked lives, the guilt of prevailing iniquity amongst the people among whom they dwell is in proportion to their influence, whether it comes by their being in offices civil or ecclesiastical, or whether it be by their riches, or their

-- 505 --

reputation for learning or wisdom, or their being of an extensive relation or acquaintance.1

Let such therefore be exhorted to take heed that they don't improve their influence to the promoting of sin and wickedness amongst us, but on the contrary, for the suppressing of it and the promoting of virtue and religion.

Let rulers and public officers be exhorted.

Let heads of families.

[Let the] rich and those that are accounted men of understanding.

Let such consider, if they do not, that much more of the public guilt will lie upon 'em than upon others.

And the guilt of public iniquity will be a very heavy burden.

1. It extends to so many.

2. It extends to future generations. When a people are corrupted, they are not soon purged from it. Your damnation for your own sin will be enough.

Those that corrupt the young people have much of the public guilt upon them—those young people that improve the reputation they have for wit, and the like, to draw multitudes on to vicious courses.

Don't let us be partakers of public guilt by suffering the church to be defiled, by neglecting doing what belongs to us to keep it pure.

[Third.] Lastly, let it be the earnest and continual prayer of God's people, that there may be a general reformation of the wickedness that prevails in the land and in this town. If it be a thing that is so much to be lamented, and a thing that we ought so much to strive against, we ought to be earnest in prayer with God, that he would pour out his Spirit upon us, and awaken rulers, ministers and heads of families to a due care and zeal for reformation.

God is able to save us from this great calamity. If we can do nothing else towards a reformation, we can cry to God. Let us do it not only on fast days, but let it be the continual prayer of all [who] mourn for the transgressions of the land, and that have the interest of religion, the glory of God and the public prosperity at heart.
