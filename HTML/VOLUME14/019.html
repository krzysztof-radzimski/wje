The Sacrifice of Christ Acceptable

This piece is a christological statement that asserts Christ's obedience and death, and not good works or natural virtue, as truly atoning for sin. It is part of Edwards' effort to refute anti-trinitarian arguments against the divinity of Christ that denied the need for atonement by the death of an infinite being, as well as the Arminian view of works and grace.1 He does so by exploring the meanings of Old and New Testament sacrifices, specifically the "legal sacrifices" under the covenant of works and the sacrifice of Christ that initiated the covenant of grace.

Edwards begins this sermon with what is for him an unusually long and detailed explication, based on his glosses on the text of this sermon in the previously written "Notes on the Scripture" nos. 62 and 171, in which he explains the ancient Jewish custom of a slave who loved his master's service having his ear "bored" in the presence of judges. Edwards points out that the slave was a type of Christ, in that Christ willingly became God's servant. While other commentators take notice of Christ's voluntary consent, Edwards gives the passage a distinctive interpretation by stating that Christ's offering himself freely was his "highest act of obedience."2

In the first proposition, Edwards demonstrates why Old Testament sacrifices were not in themselves acceptable. Sacrifices under the Old Testament dispensation, or "the law," were not proper atonement for sins because in offering animals the Israelites were only offering to God what was his already. In addition, there was no "moral goodness" in such sacrifices; they could not make atonement for sins and could not merit God's blessing. Why then, Edwards asks, did God bother with having the people of Israel sacrifice at all? If legal sacrifice was not efficacious, he explains, it was at least symbolic. Sacrificing according to the law demonstrated obedience, kept the memory of sins alive,

-- 438 --

taught that the offense of sin could only be effaced by suffering, represented God's willingness to be reconciled to sinful humankind, and typified the sacrifice of Christ.

With the incarnation of Christ, however, a new and efficacious sacrifice was offered. Drawing on numerous "Miscellanies" entries on "Christ's Righteousness" and related topics (including nos. 381, 385, 387–88, 398–99, and 402–03) and "Faith" no. 32, 3 as well as on his investigation into the nature of the Trinity instanced most prominently in The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost, Edwards here explores the work of Christ in offering himself as a sacrifice. Christ's sacrifice or obedience was acceptable because it was holy, propitiatory, and meritorious. First, it was holy by virtue of Christ's divine nature and union with the Father and Holy Spirit. Second, it was propitiatory because Christ offered to God the Father that which he did not already have: an infinitely meritorious sacrifice to match all of the sins of humankind. Christ's offering of himself was not obligatory or compelled; rather, he did it voluntarily, as the servant who had his ear "bored." Thus, Christ was able to satisfy the Father's offended sense of justice and answer the law. Finally, it was meritorious because it not only paid the debt of sin, it also secured the "positive blessings" of grace.

In Edwards' view, offering legal sacrifices is synonomous with offering merely moral behavior as a plea for redemption. Anything that human beings render in the way of sacrifice or obedience to the law, apart from Christ, is worthless. Edwards likens the unconverted to those under the Old Testament dispensation who offered "slain beasts," whereas those who are converted are under the New Testament, the new covenant, and can plead the sacrifice of Christ.

Edwards made a note to himself in the manuscript that indicates when the sermon was preached. At the end of the first preaching unit, he wrote "Sheldon," referring to Elisha Sheldon (b. 1707), who was to be admitted to full church membership in the near future, in all probability the coming sacrament day.4 Sheldon's name also appears at the end of the sermon on 1 Peter 3:4 (133), which was most likely delivered on October 26, 1729, the Sunday before the November 2 sacrament sermon, Glorying in the Savior. This means that Edwards probably began

-- 439 --

to preach The Sacrifice of Christ Acceptable on the lecture day of the intervening week.

∗ ∗ ∗

The manuscript consists of sixteen duodecimo leaves. There are two preaching units in this sermon, the second beginning at the second proposition.

Besides writing the name of Elisha Sheldon at the end of the first preaching unit, Edwards also wrote "Baptism" as a reminder to himself either to perform that sacrament following the service or to announce that it would be administered the next sabbath.

The Sacrifice of Christ Acceptable Psalms 40:6–8 Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required. Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy law is within my heart.5

This psalm was probably sung by David upon occasion of some remarkable deliverance from his enemies. He here praises God that he had heard his cry and brought him out of an horrible pit, and put a new song into his mouth, and that his thoughts of mercy towards him and his people could not be reckoned up nor numbered.

Now it was usual upon experience of [a] very singular mercy to offer sacrifices of peace offerings unto God, which is the occasion of the Psalmist's saying here as [he] does, "Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required."

The Psalmist knew that that was not the thing that God principally required or expected of his people of such occasions. He says much to the same purpose in the fifty-first Psalm, sixteenth verse.6 There was something else that God expected from his people that was a more acceptable manifestation of their gratitude and respect after the receipt of mercies, and another sacrifice that he had respect to, for the sake of which he laid aside his wrath and received the sinner into favor.

We may consider these words two ways: either as they more immediately relate to David himself, or as the Holy Ghost in them has a more principal and ultimate respect to Christ, the spiritual David. For in many passages in the Psalms, the Psalmist has a more immediate respect

-- 441 --

to himself in those things which he speaks; but yet the Holy Ghost has a principal aim at the Christ, the son of David. Whether David himself had an aim to Christ or no, the Holy Ghost might have a further aim in those words which he dictated to David, than David himself had: for as the Apostle teaches us (2 Peter 1:20–21),7 the prophecy of Scripture is not "of any private interpretation. For the prophecy came not in old time by the will of man: but holy men of God spake as they were moved by the Holy Ghost"; i.e. it was not what the penman thought of {in composing it}.

And there is no difficulty in supposing that the prophets themselves sometimes did not fully understand their own prophesies. They did not know everything that the Holy Ghost had in view in the words which they spoke or wrote. It is evident by Numbers 12:6–8, "And he said, Hear now my words: If there be a prophet among you, I the Lord will make myself known unto him in a vision, and will speak unto him in a dream. My servant Moses is not so, who is faithful in all mine house. With him I speak mouth to mouth, even apparently, and not in dark speeches; and the similitude of the Lord shall he behold: wherefore then were ye not afraid to speak against my servant Moses?" By which it is evident that it was God's usual manner not to speak apparently but in dark speeches to ordinary prophets.

If8 we consider the words as they respect David, they are a profession of his willing obedience to God. God often declared that obedience was better than sacrifice. The Psalmist is here declaring his giving of it the preference in his practice; he did not rest in sacrifices or look upon his duty as consisting mainly in them. But he was willingly obedient to God's law; he delighted to do God's will. God had opened his ear. It might have been translated, "Mine ear hast thou bored." It is in the original, "Mine ear hast thou digged," alluding to the law (Exodus 21:5), by which it was appointed that if the servant loved his master's service and freely chose it, his master should bore his ear through with an awl.

"Burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required. Then said I, Lo, I come"; as a willing servant9 says when he is called.

"In the volume of the book it is written of me." There is the greatest

-- 442 --

difficulty1 in understanding this clause. It is generally understood [as] the book of God's decrees,2 that Christ came to do God's will, as it was written of him in the book of the decrees, or in the book of scripture. But this is what seems to me to be the meaning of it (every one may judge for himself): viz. that the Psalmist still alludes to that law and custom about the servant that chose his master's service forementioned, and that by the "volume of the book" is meant the public records. The servant in that case was to be brought unto the judges and was to declare his choice, and his ear was to be bored before them that those public officers might be witnesses of it. Now we may conclude that there was a record written of it, that it might be known what servants had thus done, and that they by that record, as a public evidence of their act, might be obliged by it. It was the custom amongst the Jews to keep records of other things of this nature, as of the conveyances of land, as we have account. Jeremiah 32:10, "I subscribed the evidence of it"; it is in the Hebrew, "I wrote in the book." So the Psalmist is still carrying on the same comparison of a servant that had his ear bored. [The record is] called "volume of the book," that is, "roll."3

But the Psalmist here doubtless speaks prophetically and as representing Christ. Christ freely and willingly became God's servant by becoming incarnate, by taking flesh upon him. And therefore instead of the words, "Mine ear hast thou bored," in the New Testament we have this text quoted: "A body hast thou prepared me" (Hebrews 10:5). And as the servant that had his ear bored [learned] obedience by the things that he suffered—it was a testimony of his real desire to serve his master, that he was willing to suffer this in order to it—so did Christ learn obedience by the things that he suffered. The offering up his body a sacrifice was his highest act of obedience.4 So when it is said, "Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire," it is as much as if he had

-- 443 --

said, "Those sacrifices of beasts, etc. are in themselves worthless. God did not require them for their own sake; but my body crucified is the true sacrifice that God delights in." I have been the longer in opening these words,5 it being so remarkable a text.

Doctrine.
The sacrifice of Christ is the only sacrifice that is upon its own account acceptable to God.

The sacrifices that are of God's appointment are either Christ, the true sacrifice, or the typical sacrifices under the Old Testament. We find in Scripture that thanksgiving is sometimes called a "sacrifice" and "obedience" and a "broken heart," and our bodies—that is, our persons—when offered to God, are called "a living sacrifice." But these are only called "sacrifices" metaphorically; they are not proper sacrifices.

A sacrifice is something offered to God, which by its suffering and death makes atonement and procures acceptance for them that it is offered for. The sacrifices under the law did not truly do this, but they did it typically. And therefore Christ, according to the language of Scripture, may be called the true sacrifice, in distinction from all other sacrifices; and his altar, the true altar; and the tabernacle he officiated in, the true tabernacle. Hebrews 8:2, "A minister of the sanctuary, and the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched, and not man"; and Hebrews 9:24, "For Christ is not entered into the holy places made with hands, which are the figures of the true."

Here are therefore these two propositions to be considered:

I. That the sacrifices of the Old Testament were not in themselves and upon their own account any way acceptable to God.

II. That the sacrifice of Christ was in itself and upon its own {account acceptable to God}.

Prop. I. The sacrifices of the Old Testament were not {in themselves acceptable to God}.

First. They were not acceptable to God as gifts that he received anything by. The sacrifices are called "gifts." Exodus 28:38, "That Aaron may bear the iniquity of the holy things, which the children of Israel shall hallow in all their holy gifts." They are not so-called because they were not proper gifts to God; they were God's own already. As David

-- 444 --

said when he made such large offerings for the house of God, "Of thine own have we given thee" (1 Chronicles 29:14). But they were types of Christ's sacrifice, that was properly a gift.

God stood in no need of their sacrifices. They were of no manner of advantage to him. Psalms 50:7–10, "Hear, O my people, and I will speak; O Israel, and I will testify against thee: I am God, even thy God. I will not reprove thee for thy sacrifices or thy burnt offerings, to have been continually before me. I will take no bullock out of thy house, nor he-goats out of thy folds. For every beast of the forest is mine, and the cattle upon a thousand hills."

God is self-sufficient, and cannot stand in need of anything. Acts 17:25, "Neither is worshipped with men's hands, as though he needed anything."

God received neither profit nor pleasure from such gifts. Isaiah 1:11, "I delight not in the blood of bullocks, or of lambs, or of he-goats."

The heathen had a notion that the gods were pleased with the scent of their sacrifices, with the smoke that ascended up from off their altars. And the sacrifices under the law are said to be made by fire for a sweet savor unto the Lord [Leviticus 3:5]: they were offered with burning of incense, which made a sweet and fragrant smell. But it was only to represent the acceptableness of Christ's sacrifice, which was indeed a sweet savor, and was that in which God was well-pleased and delighted.

Second. The sacrifices of the Old Testament were not acceptable as having any moral goodness in their own nature. There is no moral goodness in offering of slain beasts unto God; the law of nature required no such things. We can't infer that this is one kind of worship [that] is due to God, merely from the consideration of God's nature or attributes, or his relation to us. We may infer our obligation to prayer and praise and other duties of natural worship.6 They were not required to attend it because it was in itself naturally a fit and becoming thing that reasonable creatures should [worship] in this way; and therefore in the twelfth [chapter] of Romans, first verse, the offering ourselves a living sacrifice is called a "reasonable service" in very way of distinction from the sacrifices under the law, because reason and the nature of the thing obliged us to offer this sacrifice, which it did not [in] the sacrifices of beasts. So this is there said to be holy and acceptable to God in contradistinction to them, because they had no moral

-- 445 --

holiness in them; nor were they acceptable to God upon any such account. Micah 6:8, "He hath showed thee, O man, what is good."

The obligation to offer those sacrifices arise only upon God's positive and arbitrary institution. There was no other holiness in them than in their holy places, which was not a moral holiness, but what was typical and depending on positive institution.

Third. Those sacrifices were not propitiatory. They did not nor could not make atonement for the soul; they made no satisfaction to divine justice. How could the burning of the flesh of beasts make any satisfaction for the debt which the sinner had contracted to divine justice by his sins? How was there any compensation for the injury done to the divine majesty by the killing of a beast? The value of the thing offered could not be equivalent to the debt contracted, nor could the suffering of the beast be equivalent to the deserved sufferings of the sinner. And therefore, as the Apostle says (Hebrews 9:9), those gifts and sacrifices "could not make him that did the service perfect, as pertaining to the conscience."

And this same doctrine God taught his people under the Old Testament. When he required that they should offer sacrifices for him, yet he taught them at the same time that they could not make atonement. Micah 6:6–7, "Wherewith shall I come before the Lord, and bow myself before the high God? shall I come before him with burnt offerings, with calves of a year old? Will the Lord be pleased with thousands of rams, or with ten thousands of rivers of oil? shall I give my firstborn for my transgression, the fruit of my body for the sin of my soul?"

And David, when after he had been guilty of his adultery and murder and was brought to repentance of it, he says, by the Spirit of God, "Thou desirest not sacrifice; else would I give it: thou delightest not in burnt offering" (Psalms 51:16). Much less,

Fourth, were they meritorious of God's favor or any positive blessing. The sacrifice of Christ was not only propitiatory, but meritorious, as we shall endeavor to show afterwards. But the legal sacrifices were not so. It is said of Noah's sacrifice (Genesis 10:21) that God smelled a sweet savor, and said in his heart that he would no more curse the ground; but it was not because that sacrifice had drawn and inclined God's heart to Noah and his posterity, but it was the respect which God had to the great sacrifice which that was the shadow of that made God thus propitious. It was the substance, and not the shadow, that sent forth that sweet savor.

-- 446 --

Inq. Of what use were the sacrifices of the Old Testament, or in what respect were they acceptable to God?

It is evident they were of divine institution: they were so from the beginning of the world. Sacrifices did not begin in the church upon God's giving the Law at Mount Sinai, but they were of longer standing. We read frequently of the patriarchs building altars and offering sacrifices unto the Lord: so Noah, he offered sacrifices; so we read that Abel brought of the firstlings of his flock, and of the fat thereof, and his offering was accepted; and it is supposed that those skins that God clothed Adam and Eve with were skins of sacrifices.

And from sacrifices being of such early institution, it doubtless came to pass that the custom of sacrificing became so universal all over the world amongst the heathen. They had it by tradition from their ancestors, from Noah and his sons, or else how should they come all to agree in it?

Seeing therefore this custom was in use in the church from the beginning of the world, it is worthy of inquiring of what use it was of in the church. Those sacrifices, when offered in a right manner, were acceptable to God. So we read (Genesis 4:4) that God had respect to Abel and his offering. Therefore I answer:

First Ans. The sacrifices of the Old Testament were acceptable to God as they were done in obedience. One end of the ceremonial law was to exercise and try the obedience of his people, as it was of all the laws that God gave at Mount Sinai. When God descended on Mount Sinai, Moses said to the people, "Fear not: for God is come to prove you, and that his fear may be before your faces, that you sin not" [Exodus 20:20]. That is what God tells the children of Israel was the end of the Law when he gave it. Deuteronomy 6:1–2, "Now these are the commands, the statutes, and the judgments, which the Lord your God hath commanded to teach you, that thou mightest fear the Lord thy God, to keep all his statutes and his commandments." To the same purpose is that in Jeremiah 7:22–23, "For I spake not unto your fathers, nor commanded them in the day that I brought them out of the land of Egypt, concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices. But this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice."

The law of sacrifices and the rest of the ceremonial law was very burdensome. It required a considerable expense, and also a great deal of care and exactness, in order to its being duly attended.

And when sacrifices were offered to God according to the law, out of

-- 447 --

a spirit of obedience and from a sincere respect to that authority that instituted them, they were acceptable to God.

Second [Arts.] Another end was to bring their sins to their remembrance. By their so often offering sacrifices for sins, they were put in mind of their sins anew. They had their yearly sacrifices. Hebrews 10:3, "But in these sacrifices is remembrance again made of sins every year." Every year they had a day of atonement. They had their monthly sacrifices: they were to offer a special sacrifice to God at the new moon at the beginning of every month (Numbers 28:11). And they had also a weekly sacrifice to be offered every sabbath (in the same chapter, Numbers 28:9). And then they had their daily sacrifice, morning and evening, as in the beginning of that chapter, to the ninth verse. Which was to teach them that they were continually sinning against God every year, every month, every week and every day.

There were sacrifices to be offered at the birth of every child, to teach them that they came sinful into the world, besides other sacrifices for sin that were to be offered upon special occasions: as their feasts at the consecration of priests, special judgments of God or particular transgressions, sins of ignorance and against light, etc.

Hereby they were taught that they were depraved, sinful creatures, that they had sinful natures and were always a-committing sins for which God might justly7 be angry with them.

Third [Ans.] It was to teach them the necessity of satisfaction for sin by suffering. They were taught the necessity of justice being satisfied by a suffering of the penalty. They saw that without shedding of blood, there was no remission (Hebrews 9:22). By the sufferings of those beasts there being slain, they were put in mind that the wages of sin was death; and by their being burnt in the fire, they were taught how they deserved to suffer God's fiery wrath for their sins, and that God would not pardon sin without suffering, without its being punished.

This put them in mind of the evil nature of sin and taught them the holy jealousy of God, and how that he would in no wise clear the guilty. And so this type and shadow, together with God's declarations of his being a holy and jealous God that would in no wise clear the guilty, secured the honor of God's jealousy and justice, and tended to bring off the sinner from a trust in his own righteousness. It taught him the strictness of the law, that sin must be suffered for notwithstanding all that he could do to make amends.

-- 448 --

Fourth [Ans.] They were hereby taught God's willingness to be reconciled. They were by this institution taught both God's justice and his mercy at the same time that they were hereby led to a sense of God's jealousy and severity. They were also led to [a] sense of his free grace, which must always be together in true faith. At the same time that the sinner trusts in God's mercy, it must be with a sense of his awful majesty and justice, or else his trust is presumption and not faith. His faith is not true, because it believes a thing that is false: it believes that God will pardon sin without justice being satisfied. Therefore God so contrived the matter in those days of the church, when so little was known of the true sacrifice, that his people might be led both not to trust in themselves and also to trust in God's mercy.

Fifth [Ans.] And lastly, it was to typify the sacrifice of Jesus Christ and to lead men to trust in him. Christ was the end of all the ceremonial observances of the law; he was the sum and substance of all. By this ceremonial law, the gospel was preached to them; though they had not the direct light of the sun of righteousness, yet they had it reflected from those typical ordinances.

Those sacrifices were to point forth Christ and to lead them to trust in him, to prepare the church for the reception and entertainment of him when he came. They would more readily receive the mystery of Christ's satisfaction by his death, for having been so long taught the necessity of sacrifices in order to purge away sin.

And this institution of sacrificing prepared even the heathen world for the entertainment of the gospel, although they changed the sacrifices of divine institution into sacrifices to devils. So however they thereby received this notion that there was need of sacrifices in order to remission, that suffering and shedding of blood was needful in order to appease the gods, so that when the gospel of Christ's sacrifice came to be preached amongst them, they doubtless the more readily entertained it.

And it is of great use and service even to the Christian church now those sacrifices are abolished, for the now seeing those shadows, and seeing the wonderful agreement between them and the antitype, tends to confirm our faith in Christ [and] our belief of the gospel. And there is abundance of instruction in them; they are full of gospel doctrine.8

-- 449 --

Prop. II. The sacrifice of Jesus Christ is in itself and upon its own account acceptable unto God. When it is said, "Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire, a body hast thou not required," it is as much as to say, "The sacrifices of beasts didst thou not desire, but the sacrifice of my body thou didst desire. In burnt offerings and sin offerings thou hast no pleasure, but thou hast pleasure in my coming to do thy will." That was the will of God, that Christ should come into the world and lay down his life for sinners. And therefore he says, when the time drew nigh, "Not my will, but thine be done" (Matthew 26:39); and Matthew 26:42, "O my Father, if this cup may not pass away from me, except I drink it, thy will be done."

God is well-pleased with Christ as mediator; he is well-pleased in him as high priest and sacrifice upon his own account. As Christ tells us, God loveth him upon this account (John 10:17). Here,9

First, the sacrifice of Jesus Christ was in itself most holy. The sacrifices of the Old Testament, as we showed, have in their own nature no moral goodness. We find them called in the Law, "most holy" things [Leviticus 6:17]. According to the Hebrew it is "holy of holinesses," but their holiness was but typical. But the sacrifice of Christ is properly most holy; there is a transcendent holiness in it, and therefore it must be very acceptable to God.

Now in order to see how holy the sacrifice of [Christ is], there are three things to be considered: the holiness of the thing offered; the holiness of the altar, for Christ tells us the altar sanctifies the gift (Matthew 23:19); and the holiness of the act of sacrificing.

1. The thing offered was most holy, for he made his soul an offering for sin (Isaiah 53:10). He offered up himself; he gave himself for us. Now the soul of Christ was a most holy thing. Christ was perfectly innocent: he was born perfectly free from any stain of original sin; on the contrary, he had a holy nature. Therefore the angel, when foretelling Mary of her being the mother of Christ, calls him "the holy thing which shall be born of thee" [Luke 1:35]. His soul was the holiest of all creatures. This was that which was offered up in sacrifice to God. [In the] offerings of beasts [there was] no holiness.

-- 450 --

2. The altar, viz. the divine nature of Christ. And then with its relation to the divine nature of Christ, it may be looked upon as infinitely holy: for the soul of Christ that was offered up is personally united to the eternal Word, the second person in the Trinity, who is an infinitely holy person. And 'tis that that gives this sacrifice an infinite value in the sight of God. The divine nature of Christ is that altar that sanctifies the gift, supposed to be typified by the altar in the tabernacle and temple: which altar had no real holiness, for what holiness can there be in stones or brass? Now this altar so sanctifies the gift, that it gives it an infinite holiness.

3. There was a transcendent holiness in that act of Jesus Christ in offering up himself a sacrifice to God, and that two ways:

(1) As it was done to vindicate the majesty, holiness and justice of God, injured by the sins of men. Christ by that act testified an infinite regard to God, that when he desired to save sinners, he had rather die than that that salvation should be any injury to his majesty, justice and law.1 He had rather be at infinite expense, for it was an infinite expense that Christ was at; it was an infinite price that he paid. And this he paid that men's salvation might not be with any injury to the glory of God's attributes, and thereby not only testified his love to men, but an infinite regard to the glory of God, and showed his infinite love to God. And therefore as Christ's act of offering up himself a sacrifice was an exercise of so great love to God, so it was a transcendently holy act. Now sacrificing under the law had no holiness in it this way, viz. by the offerer's subjecting himself to death for the vindication of God's justice: for it was not he that suffered, but the beast that was slain. And then,

(2) The act of Christ's subjecting himself to death was transcendently holy, as he did [it] in obedience to the Father. He voluntarily subjected himself to this extreme suffering because he delighted to do God's will, which likewise showed an infinite regard to God and his commands, that he would obey it in so suffering; which also rendered that act of his an act of transcendent holiness. The sacrifices under the law were offered by God's people in obedience, but we speak here of Christ's obedience in suffering as a sacrifice. They under the Old Testament did not obey so.

Second. The sacrifice of Jesus Christ was a sacrifice properly propitiatory.

-- 451 --

It did indeed make atonement for sin; thereby satisfaction is made for the injury done to God. The Old Testament sacrifices, ten thousand of 'em, were not sufficient to make atonement for one sin; but there is sufficient in Christ's sacrifice to make satisfaction for the sins of the whole world.2 In order to our seeing how the sacrifice of Christ doth properly atone for men's sin, consider,

1. What Christ offered in sacrifice to God was that which was in Christ's own power. Christ was under no obligation to offer it till he became mediator, not till he had undertaken to be our surety; indeed, consider him as having already undertaken and having become mediator. And so he became the Father's servant: he was subject to his command, and it was in obedience to his command that he laid down his life. But he was under no obligation to become mediator: that was what he freely consented to in the covenant of redemption.

So that Christ, in offering up his life, in spilling his blood, offered that that was in his own power, in his own right. It was a gift that he freely offered to the justice of God.3

Now the sacrifices under the Old Testament, as we observed before, were not so. When they offered beasts in sacrifice, they offered to God that which [was] in God's power and not their own. They were not arbitrary disposers of what they offered, for all the beasts of the field were God's, and the cattle upon a thousand hills, and it is impossible that atonement should be made for an injury done to God by offering that to him which he before had an absolute right to.

We are infinitely inferior to God; [we] are his creatures, his subjects and servants, his possession. We are God's, and all that we have is his, so that [if] we should offer, as the Prophet says, the fruit of our bodies for the sin of our souls [Micah 6:7], or if we should offer our own blood, we should offer God his own.

But 'tis not so with Christ: he is the Son of God. He is not his servant only, as he voluntarily made himself so, as the servant that had his ear bored; but he is in himself God's equal, his fellow. Zechariah 13:7, "Awake, O sword, against the man that is my fellow." When Christ therefore offers his life, he offers his own and that which he is the true disposer of.

-- 452 --

2. What Christ offered in sacrifice to God was equivalent to what justice demanded of us for our sins.

The desert of our sins may be considered as a debt that we owed to divine justice. And therefore if atonement is indeed made, it must be by the offering of something whereby the debt may be paid, which can't be except that [the] thing offered be equal to the debt, except it be of the same value.

Now the demerit of sin in infinite. [The punishment] that we had deserved by our sins is death, that is, eternal destruction, which is infinite suffering.

But the sufferings of Christ were equivalent to this. The suffering was the same in kind, so far as it was possible for the suffering of one that was perfectly holy to be of the same kind with the damnation of the ungodly. Christ suffered the death of his body with extreme outward pains, and he suffered in his soul great darkness from God's withdrawing his comfortable presence and extreme agonies as the effects of God's wrath against our sins that were laid upon him.

Christ had not despair as the damned in hell have; and in some other respects, wherein it is impossible that an innocent person should suffer the same as the guilty, Christ's sufferings were different from those of the damned. Christ was a green tree, and the wicked are the dry tree; they are both cast into the same fire, though the fire has not exactly the same effect upon both.

And though Christ's sufferings were but temporal, yet they were equivalent to our eternal sufferings by reason of the infinite dignity of his person. Though it was not infinite suffering, yet it was equivalent to infinite suffering, for it was infinite expense. His blood which he spilled, his life which he laid down, was an infinite price because it was the blood of God, as it is expressly called. Acts 20:28, "The church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood." His life was the life of that person that was the eternal Son of God, though it was the life of the human nature.

Now upon this account, the price offered was equivalent to the demerit of the sins of all mankind, [and] his sufferings equivalent to the eternal sufferings of the whole world.4

-- 453 --

But how far otherwise is it with respect to the legal sacrifices. There were nothing of an equivalent offered in them. If the angels of heaven had died and were made sacrifices for men, that would not have been equivalent to men's eternal sufferings, much less the death of goats and calves.

3. Christ, that gave himself in sacrifice, is so united to them he died for, that it may well be accepted as their sacrifice. It may well be accepted of God as if we had offered that price, and justice in Christ's suffering may well be satisfied for our sins; it may be looked upon as though we had suffered, because Christ is as we. He laid down his life out of love to us; he has been pleased to unite himself to us in his heart, to love us so as to put himself in our stead in the most extreme case.

And he is appointed hereto of the Father. God chose him for our mediator; he is God's elect, and his anointed and sealed. And therefore God looks upon him as our head, and the head being united to the members, the sufferings of the head may be looked upon as the sufferings of the members. The husband being united to the wife, the debts of the wife may be looked upon as his debts, and his goods as her goods. But there was no propriety in looking upon the sufferings of the beasts slain in sacrifice, as though they were the sufferings of him that they were offered for, for there was no union of this nature between them.

4. By the sacrifice of Christ the law was answered with respect to our sins, for the law admits of the sufferings of a surety that are equivalent. The threatening of the law is thus: "In the day that thou sinnest thou shalt [surely die]." Now according to the tenor of the covenant of works, when it is said, "In the day that thou sinnest," it means, "Thou or thy representative": for these words were not only spoken to Adam, but to all his posterity. So that as they are spoken to us, they are to be thus explained: "In the day that thou eatest, or thy surety." Therefore the last clause, which is no more personal than the first, will as fairly admit of a like explanation: "thou shalt die," that is, "Thou or thy surety shalt die." So that as we sin in a representative according to the tenor of the covenant of works, so we may die by a representative according to such an interpretation of the covenant, as the words will fairly admit—that is, provided there can any representative be found

-- 454 --

whose sufferings shall be equivalent to our eternal sufferings, that shall undertake for us and God shall appoint.

But the sacrifices of the Old Testament no way answered the law. They could not be said to die in the death of slain beasts, much less be said in them to suffer a death equivalent to an eternal death, which was what the law threatened.

Thus we have shown how the sacrifice of Christ is properly a propitiatory sacrifice.

Third. It was a meritorious sacrifice. That is, the sufferings of Christ did not only satisfy justice for our sins, but merited God's favor. Merely by our sins being done away, we stood entitled to no positive blessing at all: we were only freed from obligation to punishment. But Christ's sufferings have not only satisfied and appeased God's anger, but they merited God's favor and eternal life.

The end of sacrifices under the law was not merely typically to atone for sin and appease God's wrath, but to procure positive blessings. They were for a sweet savor to God, to procure his favors: this was often the end of those sacrifices that were vows. They were vowed in order to the obtaining of some special favor or blessing of God.

So the blood of Christ was not only a price to pay a debt, but it was also a price to buy positive blessings with. Christ hath purchased the church with his blood (Acts 20:28); the purchased possession is purchased by Christ's blood.5

But the sacrifice merits positive blessings by the holiness of the act, [as it was] an expression of respect to the Father's righteousness. Christ's submitting [to] shed his blood was the principal act of his obedience, as is intimated in our text: he delighted to do God's will, and had his ear bored. This was a command that he received of the Father, viz. to lay down his life. John 10:18, "I lay down my life, and have power to take it again. This commandment received I of my Father." And Philippians 2:7–8, "He took upon him the form of a servant, and became obedient unto death."

This was a most glorious act of obedience because it was so expensive. Christ therein showed an infinite respect to God's commands, in that he would obey it at such infinite expense. And it was therefore meritorious because Christ was not obliged to it: he was not obliged to

-- 455 --

appear in the form of a servant in such a manner to obey God. It is an act of obedience of infinite value in God's account, and a price sufficient to purchase all those eternal blessings which are promised in the gospel to believers. It is so sweet a savor unto God, that he has complacency in Christ for it. John 10:17, "Therefore my Father loveth me," and delights in all that are Christ's upon the account of it.

Application.

Inf. I. Hence learn the reason why this sacrifice is but once offered: because it is the true sacrifice, a sacrifice truly acceptable to God as a propitiation for sin. That sacrifice that truly makes atonement for sin has no need to be offered more than once. If it won't make atonement for sin by once offering it, it will not, though it be offered never so [many times];6 but if sin be atoned for by once offering, what need is there of its being offered up again? Hebrews 9:25–28, "Nor yet that he should offer himself often, as the high priest entereth into the holy place every year with blood of others; for then must he often have suffered since the foundation of the world: but now once in the end of the world hath he appeared to put away sin by the sacrifice of himself. And as it is appointed unto men once to die, but after this the judgment: so Christ was once offered to bear the sins of many; and unto them that look for him shall he appear the second time without sin unto salvation."

Now the sacrifices of the Old Testament, they were offered up often: they were offered daily; and the high priest every year entered in once into the holy of holies with the blood of bulls and goats, and there was a remembrance again made of sins. It was because they were not able to take away sins, as the Apostle argues in the beginning of the Hebrews 10: "For the law having a shadow of good things to come, and not the very image of the things, can never with those sacrifices which they offered year by year continually make the comers thereunto perfect. For then would they not have ceased to be offered? because that the worshippers once purged should have had no more conscience of sins. But in those sacrifices there is a remembrance again made of sins every year. For it is not possible that the blood of bulls and of goats should take away sins." And Hebrews 10:11–14, "And every priest standeth daily ministering and offering oftentimes the same sacrifices, which can never take away sins: but this man, after he had offered one

-- 456 --

sacrifice for sins forever, sat down on the right hand of God; from henceforth expecting till his enemies be made his footstool. For by one offering he hath perfected forever them that are sanctified."

The Papists, they hold that the sacrifice of Christ is to be offered often. They suppose that their priests make a new offering of the body and blood of Christ every Mass, which they hold to be a propitiatory sacrifice and the same that Christ offered upon the cross, which is very contrary to the holy Scriptures.

[Inf.] II. Hence we learn why the legal sacrifices do now cease: because they were not the true sacrifice. They were not upon their own account acceptable to God, had no moral goodness in their own nature and were only shadows of the sacrifice of Jesus Christ. They were to point forth that, and prepare the church for the reception and entertainment of it. And now that true sacrifice has actually been offered: Christ, the true high priest, has come into the world and offered up himself; he bore our sins in his own body on the tree; he was a minister of the true tabernacle, which the Lord pitched and not man, and has entered into the true holy of holies, even into heaven, with his own blood. And this sacrifice of his is published and declared in the gospel, which is commanded to be preached to every creature under heaven.

And the substance being come, there is no further occasion for the shadows; they all vanish. When we have the direct light of the Sun shining above the horizon, we have no further occasion for his light reflected from the moon.

Inf. III. How great is the sacrifice of Christ, that was the sum and substance and the end of all the sacrifices that were in the church since the beginning of the world. Sacrificing always till Christ came was made a very great part of outward worship; it was so both before and after Moses, and all was only to point forth the sacrifice of Jesus Christ, and to lead the church to that.

This sacrifice of Christ is surely a thing that God makes much of, that he took care to have it represented so soon in the church, even immediately after the Fall; and appointed so many different kinds of sacrifices in the law of Moses; and set apart one family, and even a whole tribe, to be concerned about this business of sacrificing. God took care to have this sacrifice of Christ represented every day, week, month and year, besides abundance of special occasions. A great part of the law of Moses is about sacrificing, and all to shadow forth Christ's sacrifice. How much doth this argue that God made of this sacrifice.

Great part of the worship of the heathen world consisted in sacrificing,

-- 457 --

which though it was from the devil as they used it, yet as it was from tradition from Noah and his sons, it shows the greatness of the sacrifice of Christ, for he was the only true sacrifice.

How many thousands and hundreds of thousands of beasts have been slain to represent this. At one time, at the dedication of the temple, two and twenty thousand oxen and an hundred and twenty thousand sheep (1 Kings 8:63).

[Inf. IV.] Exh. Let this sacrifice be acceptable unto us. If this was that which was represented and [shadowed forth] by all other sacrifices, if this be the substance, if this be so holy a sacrifice, if it be truly propitiatory and meritorious, let it be that which we look to and trust in.

Let that be what we seek to see, the excellency, suitableness and sufficiency of it. If it be a truly propitiatory [sacrifice], and sufficient to make atonement for all our sins, let us go to Christ, betake ourselves to the Lamb slain for satisfaction for our sins.

Consider:

First. Necessity.

Second. The excellency.

[1.] The holiness.

2. The love of Father and Son. The freeness. The wisdom. The glory of Christ's person. How much all God's attributes are glorified by it.

Third. 'Tis the only way to find rest of heart with respect to the conscience, [the] only way to cast off those burthens, to be freed of that dreadful sound. Matthew 11:28, "Come unto me, all ye that labor and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest."7
