True Nobleness of Mind

The text of this sermon, Acts 17:11, tells how the people of ancient Thessalonica and Berea differed in their response to Paul's preaching of the gospel. Whereas the prejudiced Thessalonians rejected Paul's message outright "without examining into it," the Bereans were "noble, ingenuous and good-spirited" in their willingness to hear it with an open mind and to compare it closely to the prophecies of Scripture. "They herein acted rationally," Edwards declares. The example of the Bereans becomes in this sermon, preached in February or March 1728, a model for a proper reception of Christianity and the manifestation of its teachings.

The central theme of this sermon, the reasonableness of Christianity, harks back to All God's Methods Are Most Reasonable, but here, rather than discussing the system of decrees by which God orders the moral world, Edwards is focusing on the frame of mind in which an individual should "entertain" Christianity.1 Human nature is innately rational and so in need of acquiring wisdom, the wisdom that is supplied only by an acceptance of the gospel. The grace that follows acceptance quickens natural human reason, helping it to achieve a higher state. Thus it is "congruous" to reason to believe in the existence of God, a future state, revelation, the mystery of some parts of religion, and the divine authorship of the Bible.

In the remainder of the Doctrine, Edwards describes the place and character of the Christian in what amounts to a profile of the virtuous citizen. He who entertains Christianity turns his back on the "childish vanities" and "trifles" of the world and its pursuits in favor of things "more agreeable to the dignity of his nature." Whereas carnal men "delight in filthiness," the believer "abhors those things" and "has more regard to his high character that he bears and the dignity that God has put upon him." Reason rules over the passions, as God intended.

Christian self-possession exhibits itself in society. The Christian is

-- 229 --

"courageous," that is, he does what is proper and decent without fear of what harm will come to him or of what others will think of him. His courage is not that of "noble warriors," who are motivated by "animal spirits," as seen in "horses and bulls," but rather a "true and rational courage." The Christian also is "of a liberal bountiful disposition," "communicative and open-handed" and "ready to lay out… for the benefit and comfort of others." A believer's heart is filled with "benevolence to mankind." Finally, the Christian is "public-spirited," keeping his eye not on his own interest but on the good of his neighbors, his country, and "the universal church."

The Application is an indictment of those who do not sincerely and readily entertain Christianity. Some are ashamed of it, others mock it, and still others do not live up to their profession. Yet Christianity is "the highest ornament to great men." Social, political, and religious leaders are particularly distinguished by the nobility of Christianity; they are above self-interest, have an eye to the greater good, and do not have an exaggerated opinion of their own reputation. These qualities, Edwards states, are becoming to those "in public place." "If you are a Christian," he concludes, "you will act according to the dignity of the human nature, yea, you will act like… the angels of heaven."

True Nobleness of Mind Acts 17:11.2 These were more noble than those of Thessalonica, in that they received the word with all readiness of mind, and searched the scriptures daily, whether those things were so.

We have an account, in the foregoing part of the chapter, of Paul and Silas coming and preaching at Thessalonica, and how they were received by the Thessalonians. Some of them believed and joined themselves to them, but the greater part of them shut their eyes against the light, and were moved with envy against these ministers of the gospel, and raised the whole city in an uproar against them. But Paul and Silas, escaping out of their hands, came to Berea and preached the gospel there; and here they met with another sort of treatment than what they did at Thessalonica. These were more noble and ingenuous; they were not so unreasonable. The Thessalonians, they were prejudiced against the gospel and rejected it as false without examining into it; they shut their eyes against light. But these are willing to receive light: they are more impartial than they; they are willing to give the arguments which Paul and Silas allege their [due].3 They are more noble in that they ben't so envious and malicious, as they don't begrutch Paul and Silas the honor of being extraordinary ambassadors of God to teach men the way of salvation.

There are two things that are mentioned that they manifested their nobleness or ingenuousness of mind by:

1. By receiving the word with all gladness. They acknowledge the reasonableness and excellency of the things they taught, and were glad to hear of such wonderful mercy and of the accomplishment of the glorious things prophesied of in the Old Testament that they had so

-- 231 --

long expected, and were glad that there was a way of salvation for them. Hereby they act like reasonable, ingenuous and good-spirited men. And,

2. In searching the scriptures, to see whether those things were so. They don't take it upon trust, but they consider and weigh the arguments that are offered, and search the prophecies, to see whether or no they agreed with the things that Paul and Silas preached about Jesus Christ. They herein acted rationally. They received the gospel upon good grounds after deliberate and mature consideration.

Doctrine.
It is true nobleness of mind readily and sincerely to entertain Christianity.

I say "readily," because it is said they of Berea "received the word with all readiness of mind." They entertain Jesus Christ as a welcome guest, becoming his dignity and excellency. They treated the gospel ingenuously and as it became so excellent a thing to be treated, as it became them to entertain the word of so excellent a person.

I say "sincerely," because there are many that do seem to receive and give credit to the gospel that don't do it as the Bereans did. They receive it upon trust, because other men say so; therefore they believe it. They believe it merely because they have been educated in that way, or they pretend to believe it and receive it for their outward advantage or credit; but they never received it from real conviction that the thing was really so. They don't receive it because they are convinced by evidence, or from any excellency they see in it, or because true tight is let into their minds from God's Word. Such as these don't sincerely receive Christianity; their faith is built upon a rotten foundation; the Word has no rooting, and so will not bear fruit.

But that it is thus as we have said, will appear by these following things:

I. 'Tis a most rational thing thus to entertain Christianity. It is requisite in order to a noble mind, that a man should be rational, a person of a good understanding. A man can't be a man of a truly noble spirit and yet be a fool or a man [that is] very ignorant; however ingenuous a disposition a man may have, that disposition has no opportunity to display itself in a man that is ignorant and foolish. Wisdom is a most essential thing in order to a noble mind.

A man acts most rationally in believing the truths of the gospel. The doctrines of Christianity are in themselves most rational, exceeding

-- 232 --

congruous to man's natural reason. Many of the doctrines are taught by the light of nature: such as the being of God and his being an almighty, all-wise, most holy, a good and bountiful Being; and that he created the world, that he made all things for himself, and that he4 upholds and rules the world and disposes all things according to his pleasure, so as to show forth his glory. And it is also evident by the light of nature from these premises, that all reasonable creatures are obliged to adore, to love, obey and trust in this maker and sovereign of all things, and that sin or disobedience to God's command deserves his wrath and displeasure. All these doctrines of the Word of God human reason teaches, and they are called the doctrines of natural religion.

It is also exceeding congruous to human reason to suppose that the soul of man does not die with the body, but that there will be a future state wherein good men shall be rewarded and wicked men punished. It's very agreeable to5 what reason tells us to be very worthy of the wisdom of God, who governs the world, that there should be general judgment; that at the world's end, God shall call to an account all the inhabitants of the world, as at the end of the day the master takes account of his servants, and that therefore all the dead should be raised to life for this end.

'Tis very agreeable to reason also to suppose that God, who created us to serve him and to enjoy him, should some way reveal himself to mankind; that he should give them some revelation wherein he should teach them concerning his nature and will, and reveal things of another world to them, and tell them the way how they obtain peace with him and enjoy his favor.

And it's very consonant to reason to suppose that in such a revelation, God should reveal many doctrines that are above human reason: for it would be strange if God should know nothing about himself but what man is capable of knowing by his own natural reason; and therefore if a revelation of God has some doctrines in it that are above the discovery of reason, it would be the more agreeable to reason for that: and therefore the Christian religion may the more reasonably be received and believed for having some such doctrines, such as the doctrines of the Trinity and incarnation and satisfaction of Christ. And indeed, the incarnation and satisfaction of Christ are exceeding reasonable doctrines. A wonderful wisdom and congruity and rationalness

-- 233 --

appears in them, as might be particularly shown if the time would allow.

And besides the reasonableness of these doctrines in themselves, there is abundant evidence that the book where they are revealed is the Word of God. 'Tis evident many ways. There is no other book that there can be any pretense of its being the revelation of the will of God; if this be not, there is no other book that shows any rational method of obtaining peace with God after we have offended, or that tells us what he requires of us in order to the obtaining of peace with him again. There is no other book but this that gives any tolerable account about another world, a future state. The revelations that are in this book have been confirmed by abundance of miracles, exceeding great and of all kinds, and the most convincing that can be thought of, and were wrought the most publicly that was possible. The miracles that were wrought by Moses were in the sight of the whole nation of the Jews, probably near two millions of souls; and the miracles of Christ and his apostles were wrought before great multitudes of the Jews and of all nations, and were published throughout the world by giving testimonies6 at a day when, if they had not been true, the whole world could have contradicted them.

And besides, there have been continual proofs of the authority of this book by the continual fulfillment of prophecies all along. And many other arguments might be adduced besides the stamps and characters of divinity everywhere appearing in the Bible, that are self-evident proofs of its coming from God.

I can't here enlarge upon arguments, but these may serve to show that they that believe the gospel act most rationally.

Those that refuse to believe the truth of the gospel, they show themselves to be of ignoble dangerous minds. They show that their minds are blinded; they are miserably deceived by their own ignorance and the prejudices of sin; they shut their eyes against clear light and will not entertain the plainest truths.

Those that willingly entertain the gospel act most rationally, in that they act most justly. They entertain that that7 is most worthy to be received, that is most excellent. Those that reject it act most unreasonably, in that they reject what is most worthy of acceptance, refuse that as odious that is exceeding excellent and honorable.

-- 234 --

He that entertains Christianity acts rationally, in that he acts most prudently. He consults his own interest and benefit. He, as it becomes a rational man, prefers his eternal before his temporal interest. He chooses rather to deny himself while he lives here in this world than to be miserable forever. He prefers everlasting blessedness, as a rational man would do, before that happiness that lasts but a few days. Therefore godly men, in Scripture account, are the only wise men, and sinners are accounted the greatest fools. They act like madmen, to run themselves into eternal misery, when they are told and warned of it. They prefer a little carnal pleasure in this world before eternal blessedness.

II. He that sincerely entertains Christianity, he therein shows a disregard of those things that are little and despicable, and principally minds those things that are of the most superior nature and of the greatest importance. It is a token of an ignoble mind, a mean spirit, to be taken up about little mean things, to be very much concerned about childish vanities. Carnal men, their thoughts and their hearts are taken up about mean things, about things that are but vanity, of very little importance, that tend a little to men's true interest, that rather serve to amuse them, as children are amused with their trifles, than really to benefit them. They are in the pursuit of those things that are below the dignity of human nature, not at all suitable to be chosen for the happiness of those beings that have a spiritual8 substance within, that are endowed with understanding, that are rather the enjoyments of beasts than of those that have the image of God upon them and an immortal nature. Their minds are tied down to the earth, to the dust under his feet, contrary to what was intended when God made the earth for man and not man for the earth.

Those things that carnal men set their hearts upon are mean and despicable upon many accounts. They are of natures inferior to the human nature, and therefore too mean for him to make his highest happiness, and then are not able to yield any true or satisfying happiness to man. And they are of an exceeding fading and vanishing nature; they are as it were shadows that vanish while men embrace them.

But now the sincere Christian, his heart is lifted above these things. He is in a measure brought to despise earthly vanities and trifles; he sees that they are not able to afford him true happiness; he is convinced

-- 235 --

of their vain and mean and shadowy nature; he looks for things of a more noble and excellent nature, more agreeable to the dignity of his nature, and he chooses them for his portion. His mind is lifted above the earth, and soars towards things of a spiritual and heavenly nature.

He minds those things that are of the greatest importance, that are of greater weight than the affairs of earthly kingdoms, and the least part of which is more worth than the whole world. He is in pursuit of what is able to afford true satisfaction and happiness, and that is everlasting; that will not only please for a few days, but will make the soul happy forever; things that will not fade, but are incorruptible. Kingdoms, crowns and scepters are but trifles in comparison of those things that the Christian concerns himself about. He therefore that herein exercises Christianity, exercises true greatness of mind.

III. He that truly entertains Christianity, he shows an abhorrence of those things that are filthy. To delight in filthiness, in those things that are impure and beastly, shows an ignoble spirit in the lowest degree. A man of a noble mind abhors such things as those that debase his nature and are a defilement and a dishonor to him. He will keep at a great distance from anything that savors of filthiness. 'Tis offensive to him, and what he can't bear.

All wicked men do delight in filthiness; they love that which is above all things most abominable and most defiling to the soul. They greedily swallow it down, as the dog eats his own vomit, and wallow themselves in it, as the swine wallows in the mire. They make themselves like beasts by their delighting in sensual lusts, by worldlymindedness, intemperance, lasciviousness. They make themselves like the most filthy poisonous beasts in malice and spite and envy. And all sin exceedingly defiles the soul and makes it more filthy than the beasts. Job 15:16, "How much more abominable and filthy is man, that drinketh iniquity like water." Isaiah 57:20, "The wicked are like the troubled sea, whose waters cast up mire and dirt."

But the Christian abhors those things that are filthy and that thus defile the soul. He has more regard to his high character that he bears and the dignity that God has put upon him, who has made him a child of God and an heir of a heavenly kingdom. Spiritual filthiness is that which his soul loathes, which he don't love to come near and abhors the very appearance of it; but he delights in those things that are pure, worthy and honorable. The Christian is of that disposition to do as we are commanded, Philippians 4:8, "Finally, brethren, whatsoever things are

-- 236 --

true, whatsoever things are honest, whatsoever things are just, whatsoever things are pure, whatsoever things are lovely, whatsoever things are of good report; if there be any virtue, if there be any praise, think on these things."

IV. To be a Christian is to be of a noble mind, in that Christianity delivers men from slavery to their passions. They are inferior servile minds that are most under the government of their passions, that are slaves to those worst of masters. Our passions were given us to be our servants, and not our masters. Carnal men are so mean-spirited as voluntarily to become servants to what were given to be their servants. The Christian delivers himself from this mean slavery, asserts his own liberty and the dominion that is his right; he obtains victory over the passion of anger. 'Tis a token of a little soul, to be apt to fly out into a rage, to be of a wrathful, malicious and revengeful disposition. This arises from weakness of mind. He that has strength and steadiness of mind is not easily ruffled and disordered by every wind.

'Tis the Christian temper to be meek and calm and not easily provoked, to be undisturbed by affronts and injuries. The Christian is above these things; he is out of the reach of them; he does as it were dwell above the clouds, out of the reach of winds and storms, and enjoys a perpetual serenity and tranquillity. He knows that men are not able to do him any real hurt. His heart is fixed, trusting in God, and he is like Mount Zion that cannot be moved.

So he has the dominion over other passions and appetites. He governs himself by reason and true wisdom, and is able to deny himself and cross his carnal appetites for the sake of his own real interest, and for the good of others, and for the sake of what is truly decent and excellent.

V. To be a Christian is to be of a noble disposition, because Christianity gives true courage and fortitude to the mind. Courage is by the consent of mankind reckoned as one necessary qualification of a noble mind, and cowardice is looked upon as meanness of spirit. But 'tis the Christian, and the Christian only, that is endowed with true courage and fortitude. Courage may be thus defined: it is a strength of mind whereby anyone is enabled and prompted to do what it is proper that he should do without being hindered or impeded by fear. Now wicked men have not this courage: they are afraid to do what it is proper they should do; their fear has such power upon them, that it takes away their strength; it confounds them. They are afraid to do what God and nature and the good of mankind and their own interest requires of

-- 237 --

'em. They are so cowardly, that they are afraid to do what is necessary for them to do in order to the escaping of their own destruction and the obtaining of their own happiness. They are afraid to engage in religion; they are afraid of hurting themselves and of bringing affliction upon themselves; they are afraid of being reproached by their companions, afraid of the laughter of fools. They fear where no fear is. Proverbs 28:1, "The wicked flees when no man pursues."

But the Christian is not so. Their hearts are filled with a holy courage and resolution. They are not frighted by such scarecrows as terrify the wicked; they are not afraid of doing that which is their duty to do, and that which it is proper they should do. They ben't frighted by the thoughts of self-denial and suffering. They are not afraid of reproaches; they ben't frighted from the practice of religion because they are laughed at by others. Yea, they are not afraid of persecution. They have such courage and resolution, that they are able to overcome the world with all its terrors and cruelties. Yea, they have courage against the terrors of death, and are bold to meet him who is the terror of kings and makes the hearts of the mighty to tremble and become as wax in the midst of their bowels.

There have been hundreds of thousands of Christians9 that, upon trial, they have shown such courage that kings and emperors, with all their terrors and torments they could invent, were not able to daunt their spirits, but they have boldly triumphed over all their rage in the midst of flames and upon hot gridirons; that showed a courage like that of Shadrach, Meshach and Abednego, that could [not] be daunted by Nebuchadnezzar's burning fiery furnace.

Such courage as this is Christian courage, which far exceeds the courage of the renowned heroes, Alexander and Pompey and Caesar, [and] is of vastly a nobler kind. Yea, Christian courage is the only true courage, in that 'tis the only courage that has its foundation in reason and virtue. The courage that most of the bold warriors are praised for is not any strength of mind obtained by rational considerations or any sense of prudence and virtue, but rather foolhardiness joined with heat of blood and fury of the animal spirits—just the same sort of courage as we see in horses and bulls—and therefore can't denominate a man noble-spirited. But 'tis Christianity that only gives a true and rational courage. Proverbs 28:1, "The wicked flees where no man pursues: but the righteous are bold as a lion."

-- 238 --

VI. To be of a Christian temper is to be of a liberal bountiful disposition. This is esteemed one great qualification of a noble mind, not to be niggardly speaking and selfish, but to be communicative and openhanded, to be ready to lay out ourselves for the benefit and comfort of others. And this is a disposition abundantly insisted upon in the gospel, to be ready to distribute, willing to communicate, not to look every man on his own things but everyone on the things of others; to love our neighbors as ourselves, to scatter abroad and cast our bread upon the waters. And Christianity does not only require this, but it also disposes to it everyone that truly embraces Christianity; it fills the heart with a noble diffusive love and benevolence to mankind, and makes to love not only in word and in tongue but in deed and in truth.

Men oftentimes get the reputation of being liberal and noble-spirited, when they only give for their own honor and credit. Great men oftentimes give great presents one to another when 'tis not from any real love to the person they give to, but 'tis only for their own sakes that they do it: but this is not truly to be bountiful. But Christianity disposes men to give from real love and sincere good will, to give expecting nothing again, to give to those that are not able to requite us, to give and not let our right hand know what our left hand doth, and not from any design of advancing our own credit amongst men.

VII. And lastly, to be of a Christian spirit is to be public-spirited. To be public-spirited is to be nobly spirited by the consent of all. The Christian, he don't only look at his own good and the good of a few round about him, but the good of his country, yea, the good of the universal church. He is concerned for the best good of mankind; he longs for the flourishing of religion in the world, for the prosperity of Jerusalem; he rejoices when he hears any such news that religion flourishes in such and such a town or land; he prays for the propagation of religion to the heathen, and has a love to mankind in general and especially as to their souls.

This is a more noble public-spiritedness than any natural men have, upon two accounts. [First,] it respects a greater good. A natural man may seek the outward good of the public, he may seek their wealth or their defense from enemies or their honor, but is not so much concerned for their salvation, their spiritual and eternal good. And then, [second,] it is more extensive. A natural man may be concerned for the good of the country he belongs to, but a Christian is concerned for the universal church and the world of mankind, has an universal benevolence.

Thus to be a Christian is to be of a truly noble disposition.

-- 239 --

Application.

I. Hence we learn how intolerable their sin is who are ashamed of religion. However excellent and noble a thing Christianity is, there are some that are ashamed of it. That is a temptation to some not to venture upon a religious course of life, that they are ashamed of it; they fear that they shall be mocked by their companions. And if they do in some measure seek God and concern themselves about their souls, they are not bold in it; they hide it as being ashamed of it. And when they are again with their companions, they'll conform to 'em beyond bounds, or else they are afraid it will be taken notice of that they are scrupulous and conscientious, and so they shall be laughed at. They are ashamed to profess Christ before men. They hide their religion, not because they would not be hypocrites, but because they are ashamed of it.

They are ashamed of [that] which is the highest excellency of the human nature, of that which is the most rational, prudent, honorable and noble thing in the world. They are ashamed of that which does above all things ennoble and advance their souls, and think that more honorable which does above all things debase them.

Those that do thus, betray a great degree of ignorance and folly. He foolishly mistakes that which is above all things for his honor, to be for his disgrace. He also shows a great degree of cowardice, that so little a thing as the decision of fools will terrify him and make him afraid openly to practice the most excellent thing in the world. He shows a great degree of falseness; he shows himself a false friend to religion; he pretends to espouse religion and to be a friend to it, but is ashamed to appear openly. As such, he pretends to [be] a friend of Jesus Christ, but durst not own himself to be so before men. Therefore surely it can never be expected that Christ will own them to be his friends before his Father which is in heaven.

We treat Christ and Christianity most unworthily if we show ourselves in the least daunted by the laughter and derision of wicked men, and don't appear bold and open and resolved friends of religion. If we do not, we may justly have our part with those fearful and unbelieving and abominable who shall have their part in the lake that burns with fire and brimstone.

II. We learn how much yet more provoking their sin is who mock and make a derision of religion. There are some such who don't only refuse to embrace Christianity themselves, but they will laugh at those that do. If any that they formerly were companions [with] begins to

-- 240 --

appear more sober and serious than the rest, they make him their game. Such as these act the devil's part, and are like to have a pact with him and his angels. We can't suppose but that almighty God, as he beholds these things, so surely he will not endure them. He will not bear to see that which he has recommended to men as the highest dignity of their nature made a mock of. As they see cause to deride others, make them the object of their derision here, so they may expect to [be] depressed as some of the lowest and be looked upon as some of the basest amongst the damned crew, and will be some of the most despised of them.

III. If it be so that a Christian spirit is a truly noble spirit, hence we learn that Christianity is the highest ornament to great men. Meanness and baseness of spirit don't suit greatness of place and character, but it becomes such as are advanced above other men in station to [be] of a noble mind: it becomes them to be rational men, men of understanding, wisdom and prudence; it becomes them to disregard those things that are little and despicable, and principally to regard those things that are of the most superior nature; it becomes them to abhor those things that are vile and filthy, and to do and to love what is pure and honorable; it becomes such not to be slaves to their passions, but to be of calm and serene minds; it becomes great men to be men of true courage and fortitude, to be of a liberal and bountiful disposition and to be public-spirited. Certainly these things do become great men; but as we have shown, 'tis Christianity only that does truly give them [a truly noble spirit].

There are many great men that, being naturally as other men of sinful nature that are ignorant of these things, they don't know what is true nobleness and greatness of mind. They are strangers to Christianity. 1 Corinthians 1:26, "Not many mighty, not many noble are called." But 'tis the effect of ignorance and blindness, and because they don't know what would be an ornament to themselves.

They are often ready to imagine that they have greater liberty than other men to gratify their appetites. They, being great, are impatient of restraint. But if it be so, then it is because they have a greater liberty to be base and inferior than other men. But however the corrupt and ignorant imaginations of many great men may think the contrary, yet 'tis the highest honor and dignity of great men to fear God devoutly and humbly to worship and adore him, to humble themselves greatly before him, with abasement and contrition of heart to repent of sin. It is an honor to a great man to be meek and patient; 'tis their honor to be

-- 241 --

ready to forgive injuries. Some think it their honor to show the greatest resentment of an affront offered to them, but 'tis a mistake: 'tis a token of a mean mind easily put into a ruffle. 'Tis an honor to a great man if he done wrong, gone out of the way, to acknowledge his fault, to be ready to own his miscarriages. Such things as these are an honor to kings and princes and all in public place, and the contrary is a great dishonor and is unbecoming a high character. Such things as these were an honor to King David, and Solomon, and Hezekiah and Josiah. They were an honor to them in the sight of their own people that they ruled over, and in the sight of other nations that were round about them, and will be to their honor to the end of the world.

IV. If it be so, that to entertain and practice Christianity shows true nobleness of mind, hence learn why God will make Christians to be kings in another world. We read that they shall be made kings and priests, that they shall reign forever and ever, that they shall sit with Christ on his throne, that they shall receive a crown of glory, and a heavenly kingdom is appointed to [them]: it shall be in reward for their nobleness of spirit. So far as they are of a Christian temper, they are of a disposition and temper fit for kings, fit for such a high state of honor and advancement. As their minds are exalted above the minds of other men, so God, who orders all things beautifully, will make their state and condition higher than the state of others.

V. If it be as we have heard in the doctrine, then those are much to blame who act below Christianity, which they profess. There are many that make a profession of this excellent religion, that don't show forth the virtues of him that hath called them. They don't manifest that truly noble spirit which we have described, and which the gospel prescribes. And some godly men suffer the contrary spirit much to prevail: they debase their minds below a Christian spirit by suffering earthly things very much to take up their hearts and affections; they too much neglect the noble object that they should be conversant with, and pursue after little despicable things and things that cannot profit.

There are some that profess Christianity, that nevertheless are very much subject to their passions; they give way to anger and a spiteful spirit. They are also often guilty of showing too much fearfulness and cowardice in the cause of God: they are afraid to do their duty; they are afraid they shall get the ill-will of their neighbors. They also very much fail upon the head of charity and liberality, and a care and concern for the good of others; they are niggardly and private-spirited. How exceeding short do many come in these things, contrary to the noble and

-- 242 --

excellent nature of Christianity and to the great dishonor of religion and of themselves.

VI. And lastly, be exhorted truly to entertain Christianity. If you would not be a mean, base-spirited, despicable creature, receive the gospel and practice the rules of it: for whatever other qualifications you have—if you have good moral abilities, or if you have great acquired knowledge; if you are naturally of a generous disposition, as the world calls it; if you have a great spirit of government, or are naturally very bold—yet you are a person of a mean inferior mind; till you are a Christian, you act irrationally and foolishly: you set your heart upon mean childish vanities and are wholly ignorant of those things that are truly excellent; you debase yourself below the nature of a man and make yourself like a beast; you delight in filthiness and those things that a noble soul would abhor; you are a weak, fearful, cowardly creature; you are a slave to your passions and carnal appetites, a servant of servants; have no true generosity; are of a narrow and confined spirit.

But if you are a Christian, you will act according to the dignity of the human nature, yea, you will act like superior sorts of beings, like those beings that are higher than men, like the angels of heaven. You will be companions for them, yea, you will be like the great God himself, will be a "partaker of the divine nature, having escaped the corruption that is in the world through lust" (2 Peter 1:4). Wherefore let all those that have any respect to their own honor or true dignity by patient continuance in well-doing seek for honor, glory and immortality.
