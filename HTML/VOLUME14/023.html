The Day of Judgment

Following a period in which Edwards is writing with some frequency on Christ's work, heaven, the work of redemption, and related topics,See, for example, "Miscellanies" nos. 215, 432, 434, 435, and 450 (Works, 13, 343, 482–84, 497–98); and "Notes on the Scripture" no. [158] (Dwight ed., 9, 511–12). The Day of Judgment represents a gathering of these themes into a unified whole that recapitulates sermons from the early Northampton years such as All God's Methods Are Most Reasonable, with its examination of the divine decrees; The Threefold Work of the Holy Ghost, which expounds Christ's role as mediator and judge; and several awakening sermons that portray saints and sinners at the end of the world. The Day of Judgment, preached in December 1729, confirms and depicts in some detail the activities of the "great day" when the final sifting of humanity will take place.

The final judgment is necessary because God, as "the sovereign ruler of the world," must vindicate his offended majesty and honor. His sovereignty gives him the "right" to judge because he is the lawmaker; any transgressions of his commands are his to punish. God also has the power to judge. Persons "cannot withdraw themselves from being under him as a judge. If humankind won't have God for their lawgiver, they shall have him for their judge." God acts in his role of judge both in "men's particular judgment at death," when the soul is brought into the "presence of God," and in "the great and general judgment," when humankind as a whole will "appear before the judgment seat."

As Paul asserts in his sermon to the Athenians, there is an appointed day for the judgment. The Greek philosophers could not deduce it because it is not "sufficiently discoverable by the light of nature"; yet it is a doctrine that for Edwards is thoroughly reasonable. A "public" judgment is particularly suitable because it will be a forum for God's "majesty and dominion," righteousness, and justice. God will then "rectify" the confused state of the world and "more fully accomplish" the reward of the godly and the punishment of the wicked.

The third head explores why Christ will judge the world. Christ's

-- 507 --

assumption of the "human nature," being united to humankind, makes him the person of the Trinity through whom God the Father "transacts" with humankind. Christ earned his "headship" through his sufferings. "'Tis needful" that Christ judge the world in order to complete the work of redemption that he began in his incarnation. In the meantime, Christ is the "King of the church" and will retain that title until his enemies are defeated. Christ's position as judge is for the comfort of the saints and the conviction of the ungodly.

Edwards then gives a lengthy description of the day of judgment. Christ will first descend with "holy and awful magnificence" in full sight of the entire world, upon which "the dead shall rise and the living be changed." The bodies of the dead—"however the parts of the bodies of many are divided and scattered"—will be reconstituted by God so that they can be judged, "some to the resurrection of life, and others to the resurrection of condemnation." All shall be arraigned before Christ, "the godly being placed at the right hand, the wicked at the left," the godly full of joy and the wicked "full of fear and horror." Then the books that record the deeds of each individual will be opened and read, and their works will be compared to the rule of Scripture, both law and gospel. Finally, the sentence against each person will be pronounced and executed. Christ will judge in righteousness and holiness, infallibly distinguishing the saints from the damned. When the judgment is complete, "this world will be dissolved by fire" and the happiness of the saints and misery of the damned will be increased and "perfected." Only then will Christ have finished his work.

Believers must wait for the day of judgment for many "mysteries of providence" to be resolved. The frequency with which Edwards deals with issues such as the prosperity of the wicked, the afflictions of the godly, and the prevalence of "public injustice" in the world, as he does here, suggests that these were questions that his parishioners often discussed with their pastor. In response, Edwards promises that God "will put all such things right."

Edwards spends most of the remainder of the Application in trying to awaken the slumbering. However secure individuals may feel or however much they may ignore their eventual judgment, sinners must consider that one day they shall be called to account for their lives before the supreme, omnipotent, inexorable, and just God. Edwards particularly confronts those who live in "secret wickedness," who are "not just and upright in their dealings with men," who "plead for the

-- 508 --

lawfulness" of ungodly practices, and who "are wont to excuse their wickedness." To avoid the condemnation of God, persons must try their own sincerity to make sure they do not wear the "disguise" of a saint and make "a fair show." Persons must also judge and condemn themselves rather than others. The sermon ends with a word of assurance to the godly. The day of judgment will be a day of joy; all of the difficulties and oppression they suffer now will then be vindicated.

The manuscript is thirty duodecimo leaves and an additional odd-sized folded fragment made of a salvaged prayer bid, which appears after the twentieth leaf. Leaves nine through twenty are slightly longer than the other pages in the manuscript. The ink of the first few leaves is medium brown, but at the top of the fifth leaf it turns gray; Edwards later went back over the earlier part of the sermon with this gray ink. The sermon can be assigned to December 1729 because it contains a variety of papers common to a transitional group of sermons from that time.Works, 13, 105, n. 9. Edwards later repreached the sermon, as indicated by his shorthand notations, but contented himself with merely rewriting the doctrinal statement and bracketing sections rather than making substantial stylistic revisions.

In keeping with the evolution of his sermon-writing style, the manuscript has many abbreviations and dashes in it that saved him both time and space in composing. Generally he used dashes to complete repeated phrases, and so they do not present great difficulty in supplying a reading. In some cases, however, particularly in parts of the Application where Edwards composed rather telegraphically, the readings must remain conjectural.

This sermon was posthumously printed in the Worcester rev. ed., 4, 202–25. The text of this earlier printing varies at times significantly from the original and includes without comment Edwards' revisions for repreaching.

Because he hath appointed a day, in which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained.

These words are part of a speech that the apostle Paul made amongst the philosophers and learned men of Athens in Mars Hill, a place of concourse of the judges and learned men of Athens. Athens was the principal city of that part of Greece, which was formerly a commonwealth by itself, and was the most noted place for learning and philosophy and human wisdom in the whole world, and that for many ages, till at length, the Romans having conquered Greece, their renown from that time began to diminish; and Rome, having borrowed learning from them, began [to] rival it in learning and civil and polite arts. However, it was still very famous in the days of Christ and the apostles, and a place of the concourse of their wise men.

Therefore, when Paul came there and began to preach up his doctrine about Jesus Christ, a man that had lately been crucified at Jerusalem, we read in the eighteenth verse [that] the philosophers thronged about him to hear what it was he had to say. The strangeness of his doctrine excited their curiosity, for they spent their time in endeavoring to find out new things and valued themselves greatly upon their being authors of new discoveries, as we are informed in the twenty-first verse; though they despised his doctrine in their hearts and reckoned it very ridiculous, for the preaching of Christ crucified was to the Greeks foolishness (1 Corinthians 1:23). The philosophers of the Epicureans and Stoics, two different sects of philosophy, they called him a babbler, but yet had a mind to hear what the babbler had to say.

Upon this, Paul rises up in the midst of 'em and makes a speech to 'em. And, as he speaks to philosophers and men of learning, in how

different a manner [he] speaks to 'em: there is evidently a greater depth in his discourse, and philosophical ratiocination, and height of style than in his ordinary discourses to common men, such as would be likely to draw the attention and gain the assent of philosophers. He showed himself to be no babbler, but a man that could offer such reason as they, however they valued themselves upon their wisdom, were not able to gainsay. His practice here is agreeable to what he says of himself, 1 Corinthians 9:22, that he became "all things to all men, that he might by all means save some." He not only to the weak became as weak, that he might gain the weak; but to the wise he became as wise, that he might gain {the wise}.

And in the first place, he begins and reasons with [them] about their idol worship. He declares to them the true God, and how unreasonable it is to suppose that he delights in such a kind of superstitious worship. He begins upon this first, as being what they would be more likely to hearken to as being so evidently agreeable to the natural light of human reason, and also agreeable to what some of their own noted poets and philosophers had said, as he tells them in the twenty-eighth verse. The Apostle don't begin immediately to tell 'em about Jesus Christ, and his dying for sinners, and his resurrection from the dead; but first draws their attention with that which they would be more likely to hearken to. And then, having thus introduced himself, he proceeds to speak to 'em concerning Jesus Christ. He [tells] them that the times of this ignorance, which they hitherto had been under about the true God, God winked [at]; he suffered the world to lie in heathenish darkness. But now the appointed time was come, when he expected that men should everywhere repent, "because he had appointed a day, in the which he will judge the world in righteousness by that man whom he hath ordained,"

Doctrine.
God hath appointed a day in which he will judge the world in righteousness by Jesus Christ.JE here left ten lines blank, but after he had begun using the gray ink that appears in the latter part of the sermon, he inserted the list of propositions. In revising for repreaching, he rewrote the doctrine to state: "There is a day coming wherein there will be a general judgment of the whole world by Jesus Christ."

I. That God is the supreme judge of the world.

II. That there is a time coming when God will in the most public and solemn manner judge the whole world.

III. That the person by whom {God will judge the world is} Jesus Christ.

IV. Describe it.

V. That it shall be done in righteousness.

VI. Take notice of those things that will be consequent upon it.

I. God is the supreme judge of the world.

First. God is so by right. He is by right the supreme and absolute ruler and disposer of all things, both of the natural and moral world. The reasonable understanding part of the creation are the proper subjects of a different sort of government from those things that are without [reason]. God governs the sun, moon and stars; he governs even the motes of dust that fly in the air. Not a hair of our head falls to the ground without our heavenly Father. God governs the lowest creatures: that is to say, God by his providence does, according to his own decrees, order all events concerning these creatures.

Reasonable creatures, they are also subject to the same sort of government. That is, all their actions and all events concerning them are ordered by sovereign providence according to absolute decrees, so that nothing there among [them] doth, nor any event that relates to them, happen without a divine ordering and disposal according to his decrees. The rule of this sort of government is God's own wise decree, and nothing else.

But reasonable creatures, because they are intelligent and voluntary agents, are the subjects of another sort of government—that is, only with respect to their actions as causes by counsel, or with respect to their voluntary actions—which is called moral government; which moral government consists in these two things, viz. in giving laws, and in judging.

-- 512 --

God is by right, with respect to this sort of government, the sovereign ruler of the world, both by reason of his infinite greatness and excellency, by which he merits and deserves and is perfectly, and only, fit for it. He that is so excellent as to be infinitely worthy of the highest respect of the creature, he has thereby a right to that respect he deserves by a merit of condignity; so that it would be injustice to deny it him. And he that is perfectly wise and true, and is only so, he has a right in everything to be harkened to and to have his determinations attended and subjected to.

And God has also a right by reason of every creature's absolute dependence on him. All creatures, and reasonable creatures no less than others, are wholly derived from and are wholly dependent on him every moment for being and all good, so that they are properly his possession. And as he has right by virtue of that to give what rules of action soever he pleases, what rules soever are agreeable to his own wisdom, [so] the mind and will of the creature ought to be entirely conformed to the nature and will of the Creator and to those rules he gives that are expressive of it.

And he has, for the same reason, an absolute right himself to judge of their actions and to fulfill the sanctions of his law. He that has an absolute and independent right to give laws, has evermore the same right to judge those that he gives laws to.

'Tis absolutely necessary that there should be a judge of reasonable creatures with respect to their moral actions. Sanction, or rewards and punishment annexed to rules, is necessary to the being of a law. A person may instruct another without sanctions, but not give laws; but those sanctions themselves are vain, they are as good as none, without a judge to determine the execution of them.

God has right to be the supreme judge, so that none has any right to reverse his judgment, or to receive appeals from him, or to say to him, "Why judgest thou thus?"

Second. God is supreme judge of the world in fact. He has power sufficient to vindicate his own right. As he has a right that cannot be disputed, so he has power that cannot be controlled. He has omnipotence wherewith to maintain his dominion over the world, and he maintains his government in the moral as well as the natural world.

Men may refuse subjection to God as a lawgiver, they may shake off the yoke of his laws by rebellion; yet they cannot withdraw themselves from being under him as a judge. If men won't have God for their lawgiver, they shall have him for their judge. The strongest of creatures

-- 513 --

can do nothing to control God, or to avoid him as their judge. He is able to bring them to his judgment seat and is able to see that the sentence be executed.

There was once a notable attempt made by opposition of power entirely to shake off the yoke of God's moral government, both as lawgiver and judge, by some of the mightiest of creatures, viz. the angels; but they miserably failed in their attempt. God notwithstanding acted as their judge in casting them out of heaven and binding those proud spirits in chains of darkness till a further judgment and further execution. "God is wise in heart, and mighty in strength; and none ever hardened himself against him, and hath prospered" (Job 9:4). Wherein God's enemies deal proudly, he is above them.

God ever has acted as judge, bestowing what rewards and inflicting {what punishments} he pleased on the children [of men]. And so he doth still: he is daily fulfilling the promises and threatenings of the law in disposing of the souls of the children of men; and so he evermore will act.

God acts as judge towards the children of men more especially,

1. In men's particular judgment at death. The sentence is executed then upon the wicked, and the reward bestowed in part, which is not done without a judgment. The soul, when it departs from the body, goes and appears before God to be disposed of by him according to his law. But by their appearing before God to be judged at death, we need understand no more than this: that the soul is made immediately sensible of the presence of God. God manifests himself immediately to the soul with the glory and majesty of a judge. The sins of the wicked is brought forth, and the righteous of the saints, by God to their consciences; so that therefore they know the reason of the sentence given, and their consciences are made to testify to the justice of it. And so God's will for the fulfilling of the law in their reward or punishment is made known to them and executed. This is undoubtedly done at every man's death.

2. There is the great and general judgment, when all men shall together appear before the judgment seat to be judged; which judgment will be much more solemn, and the sanctions of the law shall to a further degree be fulfilled. But this brings me to show,

II. That there is a time coming when God will in the most public and solemn manner judge the whole world of mankind.

The general judgment is not a doctrine that is sufficiently discoverable by the light of nature. Some of the heathen indeed had some

-- 514 --

obscure notions about a future judgment, but the light of nature or mere unassisted reason was not sufficient to instruct the world of fallen men in this doctrine. 'Tis one of the peculiar doctrines of divine revelation and a doctrine of the gospel of Jesus. There were some hints of it in the Old Testament, as Psalms 96:13, "The Lord comes to judge the world in righteousness, and his people with truth"; and Ecclesiastes 12:14, "For God will bring every work into judgment, with every secret thing, whether it be good, or whether it be evil"; and some other such like passages. But this doctrine is with abundantly the greatest clearness revealed in the New Testament. There we have it frequently, particularly declared and deciphered with its circumstances.

But although it be a doctrine of divine revelation and brought to light by the gospel, the brightest and most glorious revelation that God has given to the world; yet 'tis a doctrine that is exceeding agreeable to reason, and which reason gives great confirmation of. That there will be a time when all the inhabitants of the earth, before the dissolution of the world, shall stand before God and give an account, and that God will in a public manner by a general and just judgment set all things to rights with respect to their behavior and actions in the world, is a doctrine exceeding agreeable to reason; which [I] will endeavor to make appear.

But I would premise that what we would inquire into, is not whether all mankind shall be judged by God, for that is a thing that the light of nature clearly teaches, and we have spoken something of that already; but whether it be a thing rational to think that there should be a public judgment of all mankind together. Which will appear very congruous to reason by the following considerations:

First. 'Tis a more glorious display of God's majesty and dominion. It will be more glorious because it will be more open, public and solemn.

Although God has now actually the dominion in the earth—'tis he that reigns and does all things according to his own will, orders all events, and is actually judge in the earth, is continually disposing of men's souls according to their works—yet he rules after a more hidden secret manner.

It is a common [thing] amongst the proud sons of men to refuse to acknowledge his dominion. Wicked men, they question whether there be a God that takes care of the world, that orders and judges in it; and therefore they don't fear him.

There are many of the kings and great men that don't suitably

-- 515 --

acknowledge the God that is above them, but seem to look upon themselves as supreme, and tyrannize over mankind as if they were accountable to no other.

There have been and are many atheistical persons that don't acknowledge God's moral dominion over mankind, and therefore they throw off the yoke of his laws and government.

And how great a part of the world is there, and has there been, that have not acknowledged that the government of the world belonged to the God of Israel and the God of Christians, but have paid homage to other imaginary deities as [though] they were their sovereign lords and supreme judges.

How great a part of the world has Satan from age to age usurped the authority over, and set up himself for god of this world in opposition to the true God.

Now how congruous is it that God, in the winding up of things, when the present state of mankind is coming to a conclusion, should manifest in the most public and open manner his dominion over the inhabitants of the earth by bringing of them all—both high and low, rich and poor, both kings and subjects—together before him to be judged with respect to all that they ever did in the world; that he should thus openly discover his dominion in this world, where his authority has been so much questioned, denied and proudly opposed; that those very persons that have thus denied and opposed should see it, and be themselves with the rest of the world before the tribunal of God; that God, however he is not now visibly present upon the earth, disposing and judging in that visible manner that earthly kings are, that yet at the conclusion of the world he should thus make his dominion visible to all and with respect to all mankind: so that every eye shall see him, even they which have denied him, and shall find that God is the supreme Lord of them and of the whole world.

Second. The end of judgment will be more fully answered by a public and general, than only by a particular, {judgment}. The end for the sake of which there is any judgment at all, is to display and glorify God's righteousness; which end is more fully accomplished by calling men to an account, bringing their actions to the trial, and determining their state according to them, the whole world being present to behold, both angels and men.

At the day of judgment there will be the most glorious discovery of the justice of God that ever was made. Then, God will appear to all to be universally righteous towards everyone; the justice of all God's

-- 516 --

moral government will at that day at once be discovered. Then, all objections will be removed; the consciences of every man shall be satisfied; the blasphemous cavils of the ungodly will be forever put to silence; and there will be argument given for the saints and angels to praise. Revelation 19:1–2, "And after these things I heard a great voice of much people in heaven, saying, Alleluia; Salvation, and glory, and honor, and power be to the Lord our God; for true and righteous are his judgments,"

Third. 'Tis very congruous that the irregularities that are so open and manifest in the world should, when the world comes to a conclusion, be publicly rectified by the supreme governor. The infinitely wise God, who made this world to be a habitation for men, and placed mankind to dwell here, and has appointed man his end and work, must take care of the order and good government of the world that he has thus made. God is not regardless how things proceed here upon the face of the earth: it would be a disparagement to his wisdom and the perfect rectitude of his nature to suppose so.

But this world is a world of confusion. It has been filled with irregularity and confusion ever since the fall. And the irregularities of it ben't only private, relating to the actions of particular persons alone; but states, kingdoms, nations, churches, cities and all societies of men in all ages have been full of public irregularities. The affairs of this world, so far as they are in the hands of men, are carried on after a most irregular and confused manner.

Though justice sometimes takes place, yet how often does injustice, cruelty and oppression. How often are the righteous condemned and the wicked acquitted and rewarded; how common for the virtuous and pious to be depressed, and the wicked to be advanced; how many thousands of the best of men have suffered intolerable cruelties only for their virtue and piety, and have had no help, no refuge, to fly to in this world.

The world is very much ruled by men's pride, covetousness and passions. Solomon takes much notice of such like irregularities in the present state of mankind in his Book of Ecclesiastes, whereby he shows the vanity of the world.

Now, how reasonable is it to suppose that God, when he comes to put an end to the present state of mankind, will in an open public manner, the whole world being present, rectify all disorders, and that he will bring all things to the trial and put them to rights by a general judgment;

-- 517 --

and that then those that have been oppressed may be delivered, that there the righteous cause may be pleaded and vindicated, and wickedness, that has been approved of and been honored and rewarded, may receive its due disgrace and punishment; that the proceedings of kings and earthly judges may be inquired into by him whose eyes are as a flame of fire, and that the public actions of men may be publicly examined and recompensed according to their defects.

How agreeable is this to divine wisdom, thus to order it; how worthy and becoming the supreme governor of the world.

Fourth. And lastly, by a public and general judgment God more fully accomplishes the reward he designs for the godly, and the punishment {for the wicked}. One part of the reward which God intends for his saints is the honor that he intends to bestow upon them. He will honor them in the most public and open manner before the angels, before all mankind, and before them that hated them.

And it is most suitable that it should be so. 'Tis suitable that those holy humble souls that [have] been hated by wicked men and have been cruelly treated and put to shame by them, and that they have haughtily domineered over, should be openly acquitted, commended and crowned before all the world.

So one part of the punishment of [the] ungodly will be that open shame and disgrace that they will be the subjects of. Though many of them have proudly lifted up their heads in this world, have had a very high thought of themselves, and have obtained outward honor amongst men in this state; yet God will put them to open shame by showing all their filthiness before {all the world}, by manifesting his abhorrence of them, by placing them on his left hand amongst devils and foul spirits, by turning of them away into the most loathsome pit of hell, to dwell there forever.

These ends may be much more fully accomplished in a general, than in a particular, judgment.The first preaching unit ends here. The second begins at the top of the ninth leaf without the usual recapitulation of doctrine and previous points.

We are come now, in

III. [The third place,] to the person {who will judge the world}, Jesus Christ, God-man. It will be the second person of the Trinity, that same person that we read of in our Bibles, that was born of the Virgin Mary…. He will be present as judge, both in his divine and human

-- 518 --

natures: his human body, the same body that was crucified, and rose again, and ascended into heaven. Acts 1:11, "This same Jesus, that is taken up from you into heaven, shall so come in like manner as ye have seen him go into heaven." It will be his human nature that will then be seen by men's bodily eyes.

And his divine nature will then also be present, which is united to the human nature; and it will be by the wisdom of that divine nature that Christ will see and judge.

Inq. Why is Christ appointed to judge the world rather than the Father or the Holy Ghost?

We cannot pretend to all the reasons of God's dispensation. God is not obliged to give us an account. But so much [as] we may learn by divine revelation, as to discover to us a marvelous wisdom in what he determines and doth with respect to this matter, we learn by God's Word.

First. God saw meet that that person that was in the human nature should be the judge of those that had the human nature. John 5:27, "And hath given him authority to execute judgment also, because he is the Son of man." Seeing that there is one of the persons in the Trinity united to the human nature, God chooses in all his transactions with mankind to transact by him. He did so of old, in his discoveries of himself to the patriarchs and in giving the Law, and leading the children of Israel through the wilderness and in the manifestations he made of himself in the tabernacle and temple: when, although Christ was not then actually incarnate, yet he was so in design; it was ordained and agreed in the covenant of redemption that he should be. First. Christ can't fail of being just in judging through mistake. He can't mistake some for sincere and godly that are not so, or take some for to be hypocrites that have real sincerity in them. His "eyes are as a flame of fire" [Revelation 1:14], and he "searcheth the hearts, and trieth the reins of the children of men" [Revelation 2:23]. He can't err as to what is justice in particular cases, as human judges often do. He can't be blinded by prejudice, as human judges are very liable to be. Deuteronomy 10:17, "He regardeth not persons, nor taketh reward." He can't be deceived by the excuses and false colors and pleas of the wicked, as human judges. He can't err in assigning every one his proportion of reward and punishment according to men's wickedness or good works.

Second. He can't fail of judging in righteousness, through an unrighteous disposition, who is infinitely just and holy in his nature. Deuteronomy 32:4, "He is a rock, his work is perfect; all his ways are judgment; a God of truth without iniquity, just and right is he." 'Tis impossible that an infinitely powerful and self-sufficient Being should be under any temptation to be unjust. 'Tis impossible that an infinitely wise Being, that knows all things, should not choose justice; for he that perfectly knows all things, perfectly knows how much more amiable justice is than injustice.

Application.

Use I. Of Instruction. Hence many of the mysteries of providence may be unfolded. There are many things in God's dealings towards the children of men that appear very mysterious if we look upon them without having an eye to this last judgment, which, if we consider that, have no difficulty in them:

First. That God suffers the wicked to live and prosper in the world. The infinitely holy and wise Creator and governor of the world must necessarily hate wickedness, but yet we see many wicked men "spreading themselves as a green bay tree" [Psalms 37:35]. They live with impunity; things seem to go well with 'em, and the world smiles upon 'em.

-- 535 --

Many that have not been fit to live, that have had God and religion in the greatest contempt and have been open enemies to all that is good, and that by their wickedness have been the pests of mankind—many cruel tyrants whose cruelties have been such that it would even fill one with horror to hear or read of—yet have lived in great wealth and outward glory, have reigned over great and mighty kingdoms and empires, and have been honored as a sort of earthly gods.

'Tis very mysterious that the holy and righteous governor of the world, whose eye beholds all the children of men, should suffer it to be thus, except men look forward to the day of judgment; and then the mystery is unraveled. Although God for the present keeps silence and seems to let them alone, yet then he will show himself with suitable manifestations of his displeasure against their wickedness. They shall then receive their condign punishment.

The saints under the old testament were much stumbled, [as] Job (Job 21), David (Psalms 73) and Jeremiah (Jeremiah 12). It was a great difficulty to them, because then a future state and a day of judgment was not revealed with that clearness that now it is.

Second. God sometimes suffers some of the best of men to be in great affliction {in the world}. The wicked rule, and they are subject and are trampled under their feet; the wicked are the head, and they are the tail. 'Tis a very common thing for the best men to be undermost in the present state of things. The ungodly rule and domineer, and they serve and are oppressed. This seems to be great confusion. When the wicked are uppermost, and the godly are oppressed by 'em, things are quite out of joint. Proverbs 25:26, "A righteous man falling down before the wicked is a troubled fountain, and a corrupt spring."

And if we look no further than the present state, it appears strange that God should suffer it to be so, so that sometimes one wicked man shall make many hundreds, yea thousands, of precious saints a sacrifice to his lust and cruelty, when he puts them to death for no other cause but for that which they ought above all things to be esteemed and commended for.

But when God shall put an end to the present state of mankind, these things shall be put to rights. The case won't be thus always; and though God suffers it for the present, yet 'tis but for a moment in comparison. When things come to be settled and fixed by a divine judgment, the righteous shall be exalted, honored and rewarded; and the wicked shall be depressed and put under their feet.

However wicked men seem now to prevail against 'em, yet they shall

at last have the ascendant and shall be conquerors. They shall see the just vengeance of God upon those that hate them.
