INTRODUCTION by George S. Claghorn

THIS fragment of a previously unpublished letter of Jonathan Edwards is, in many ways, one of the most intriguing. His unnamed correspondent had raised a number of objections to his Treatise on the Religious Affections. Many of the issues raised are still vigorously debated by his interpreters: election, assurance, perseverance, and participation in the divine nature.

In reply, Edwards follows his usual practice of stating objections before answering. Thus he gives us an understanding of the main concerns that had been addressed to him and the general viewpoint of the writer. The correspondent claims that Edwards introduced a new faculty, in attributing a "new spiritual sense" to the saints. Edwards had denied that in Affections three times in one paragraph.1 The correspondent also takes, in Edwards' view, a non-Calvinistic stance on election and may have based his attack on assurance on the doctrine of falling from grace.

The correspondent's further objection is that, when Edwards says the Holy Spirit communicates his "nature" to believers, he implies that the Spirit communicates his "essence." That threatens the transcendence of God and Christ. Edwards' reply addresses these questions. The issue seems to be: Is Edwards a pantheist or not? Is he some sort of monist, who believed that God is within the world, as soul to body, or identified with being as a whole?

The discussion centers on Edwards' statements in Affections, Part III, First Sign, where "communion" and "communication" are used synonymously with "participation."2 Edwards emphasizes, in reply to the objection, that God's "proper nature" does not mean his "essence" in this context. He characterizes the divine nature imparted to the believer as an attribute, quality, or characteristic rather than as identical substance. In this transaction, the saints receive holiness, spiritual beauty, and happiness. Edwards repeats his analogy of the sun shining

-- 632 --

into a crystal, which reflects light but does not itself become the sun.

Nothing in this letter suggests that the correspondent knew any of Edwards' earlier, private writings. If he had, his suspicions might have been even more aroused. In his notes on "The Mind," Edwards had written: "When we speak of being in general, we may be understood [to speak] of the divine Being, for he is an infinite being. Therefore all others must necessarily be considered as nothing. As to bodies, we have shewn in another place that they have no proper being of their own; and as to spirits, they are the communications of the great original Spirit. And doubtless, in metaphysical strictness and propriety, he is, as there is none else."3

The next paragraph of this note, however, refers to creation, and the one immediately following, to created spirits. Edwards surely believed in the reality of the world and of human beings distinct from the Creator. He did state in these early, private writings that other minds are "emanations" of God.4 Since he did not elaborate on this in any systematic way, however, there is no reason to believe that he meant anything other than participation and communication.5 There is no reason to believe that Edwards was denying creation or holding to the neo-Platonic view of emanation. His point is that God's power, light, and love have gone forth, not his essence.

Those who interpret Edwards as a monist or some sort of pantheist frequently cite his autobiographical "Personal Narrative"— again, not a public document of his times. There he relates his mystical experiences, defying description and overwhelming him with a sense of the closeness of the presence of God. He says:

I have sometimes had a sense of the excellent fullness of Christ, and his meetness and suitableness as a Saviour whereby he has appeared to me, far above all, the chief of ten thousands. His blood and atonement have appeared sweet, and his righteousness sweet; which was always accompanied with ardency of spirit; and inward smugglings and breathings, and groanings that cannot be uttered, to be emptied of myself, and swallowed up in Christ.6

-- 633 --

It should be noted that these impressions, though vivid, reflect more his sense of harmony with the divine, of humility and personal longing for God, than any merger of essence with him.

Edwards employs three principal figures of speech in Affections to represent the relation of God to his saints: light, water (fountain), and sap (branch).7 All are derived from the Bible. Taken literally, each could be construed as pantheistic. This, however, is not the intent of the Scriptures or of Edwards. The metaphors illustrate the dependence of saints on God, and the similarity of qualities in both (holiness, joy, and the like), rather than identity of substance. Another image is that of a seal and the wax it imprints, or the forehead upon which it is a sign. Are not all these images types of a Christian being in Christ; or rather, of Christ in us, God and Christ communicated to us?

Sometimes Edwards' enthusiasm leads him to use language that was easily misinterpreted. For example, he describes the relation of God and believers as being like the sun and "little suns, partaking of the nature of the fountain of their light."8 On the other hand, he explicitly denies that "communicating" or "partaking" implies monism:

Not that the saints are made partakers of the essence of God, and so are "Godded" with God, and "Christed" with Christ, according to the abominable and blasphemous language and notions of some heretics; but, to use the Scripture phrase, they are made partakers of God's fullness (Ephesians 3:17–19; John 1:16), that is, of God's spiritual beauty and happiness, according to the measure and capacity of a creature; for so it is evident the word "fullness" signifies in Scripture language.9

Lest there be any doubt as to Jonathan Edwards' intent on this score, the present letter repeatedly rejects monism, the doctrine of believers being or becoming one with the divine substance.

The exposition in Affections expands Edwards' earlier thoughts in "The Mind": "[God's] love to the creature is his excellence, or the communication of Himself, his complacency in them, according as they partake of more or less of excellence and beauty, that is, of holiness, which consists in love; that is, according as he communicates more or less of his Holy Spirit."1 In turn, this echoes events in Edwards' own life,

-- 634 --

which he describes in the "Personal Narrative": "God, in the communications of his holy spirit, has appeared as an infinite fountain of divine glory and sweetness; being full and sufficient to fill and satisfy the soul; pouring forth itself in sweet communications; like the sun in its glory, sweetly and pleasantly diffusing light and life."2 Edwards' reply in this letter to the inquiries addressed to him seems sufficient to convict the objector of not having read Affections carefully enough, or not with figural imagination in interpreting Scripture.

Finally, a word about the manuscript fragment itself. The draft, located in the Andover collection, is written on the back of a Massachusetts proclamation dated February 26, 1745 (o. s.), for a fast day on March 13, 1745.3 This is a folio sheet 43 centimeters long. The paper has been folded twice across and once lengthwise, leaving the upper quarter worn and discolored from being on the outside. The document is basically in good condition, with one small hole, one centimeter wide, in the shape of a bird in flight. The opening and the ending of the letter are missing, Edwards' fair copy is not extant, and the identity of the correspondent is unknown. He was a clergyman, probably in the Colonies, not abroad.

The writing, in two columns, well illustrates the style of Edwards' letter drafts. His flying pen wrote words difficult to decipher, often appearing as little more than horizontal squiggles. Lines are crossed out as he struggled to rephrase his thoughts. To complicate matters, certain words, later crimped into creases, can now be discovered only by a physical examination of the manuscript.

Jonathan Edwards made good use of broadsides. He launched one on a second career as the inside cover for his "Miscellanies," Bk. 6. Nine others in the manuscript collection, including the present document, contain letter drafts. Six of these are on the backs of commencement announcements, three on fast-day proclamations, and one on the notice of a provincial act "more effectually to prevent profane Cursing and Swearing."

There are several good clues to the date of the letter. The draft could not have been written before February 26 (1746, n. s.) and probably not before March 13. The writing is in the distinctive black ink used in "no. 94. Extracts from Lowman" and "Remarks on Lowman," both in

-- 635 --

"Notes on the Apocalypse." Edwards used the same ink in sermons written between August 1746 and October 1747 and in "Catalogue" entries from 1746 and 1747. The "Tractate on Revelation 16:12" has been dated in late 1746 or early 1747 and parallels the fourth answer to the fifth objection of An Humble Attempt to Promote Explicit Agreement and Visible Union of God's People in Extraordinary Prayer. Moreover, the Humble Attempt was completed before September 23, 1747.4 The conclusion must be, therefore, that the letter draft was prepared between March 13, 1746 and September 23, 1747.

Further confirmation is offered by the figuring Edwards did on the upper left-hand side of the verso, the same side as the letter draft. He divides 365 by two (182), multiplies 365 by three (1095), then adds the results to get 1277. This is evidently an attempt to estimate the "time, times and a half a time" spoken of in Daniel 7:25 and Revelation 12:14. Edwards links these references to the time of the Antichrist's reign and the church's being in great trouble, in the writings cited above.5 Edwards does not use 1277 in print, preferring 1260, but he was unmistakably thinking of the same period of time, 42 months.6

The proclamation on which Edwards drafted this letter is one of eleven issued that year by Governor William Shirley. John Draper (1702–62), who printed the broadside, was printer to the governor and council of Massachusetts-Bay and publisher of The Boston Weekly News-Letter. He was prolific in output, producing books by both leading New Light ministers during the Great Awakening and their critics. Draper was most widely known for his annual publication of Ames' almanac.7

The extant part of Edwards' letter begins within a response on the issue of assurance.

-- 636 --

UNPUBLISHED LETTER ON ASSURANCE AND PARTICIPATION IN THE DIVINE NATURE

...8 the faith and so also there will be laid up for me a crown of righteousness. And those words (2 Timothy 1:12), "I know whom I have believed, and I am persuaded that he is able to keep that which I have committed unto him against that day," are interpreted thus: "Upon supposition that I keep myself through my approaching trial of martyrdom and so approve myself a true believer (which I don't yet know that I shall), then I do know that I have already believed in Christ and have committed my soul to him, and that he is able to keep it against that day." And that in Philippians 1:21, "For me to live is Christ, and to die is gain," is interpreted, "On supposition [that] I live to Christ to the end of my days, then I shall live to Christ (or, for me to live will be Christ) and to die will be gain. Therefore, though I am uncertain whether I shall either live to Christ, or but that I shall go to hell when I die, yet I am in a strait between these two, not knowing which to choose, whether the gain of dying (and so to depart to be with Christ), or to live here, to live to Christ."

I won't charge upon you these absurd interpretations of Scripture; possibly I have misunderstood you when you speak of persons' being unable to say whether they shall persevere through future trials and about the Apostle's speaking in assured strain, on [your] supposition; though I am not able to find out how I should misunderstand you.

And9 if it be as you say, that the apostle Paul expressed himself as uncertain about the state of his soul in the texts you cite, this is a full and clear proof of the thing you seem to deny, viz. that a saint may sometimes have an absolute assurance and yet afterwards be without. For this apostle, before he uttered those words in Philippians 3:12–13, had abundantly spoken most positively and assuredly, and as one that was certain of his own sincere Christianity and future reward (2 Corinthians 4:8, 2 Corinthians 4:14–18, with

-- 637 --

2 Corinthians 5:1–8, 2 Corinthians 4:16–17 and 2 Corinthians 6:1–11, 2 Corinthians 1:12, 2 Corinthians 1:21–22). And in 1 Thessalonians 2:1–10 he most solemnly appeals to God concerning his sincerity and holiness.

Concerning that text in Galatians 2:20. You say, "I know that the Scripture speaks of Christ loving and dying, or giving himself, for those who yet are supposed never finally to obtain the benefits of his death."1 I shall not undertake in this letter to go through with the controversy about universal redemption and about the interpretation of those scriptures that speak of Christ's dying for all, etc. But I don't remember that the Apostle uses the very same words, of Christ's loving and giving himself, joined in like manner anywhere else, unless it be in Ephesians 5:25, where they are used concerning that church of saints that are Christ's spouse, and his body, of his flesh and his bone. And 'tis certain that the Apostle, in this text in Galatians, don't speak of Christ's loving him and giving himself for him only as he might those that have no saving benefits of his death; because in the same sentence he speaks of saving benefits and consequences of Christ's loving him and giving himself for him just as Christ's living in him and his living by the faith of the son of God. And he speaks of those benefits with as much positiveness as of Christ's loving him and giving himself for him.

As to that text, 2 Peter 1:10, I shall not pretend in this letter to enter into the controversy between Calvinists and Arminians about the meaning of the word "election" as it is used in the New Testament, whether it signifies a particular election as the Calvinists suppose, or only something general extending to all that live under the gospel, as you suppose the word is used here. Let that be as it will, I think it is plain as it is possible anything should be, by other words in the same sentence, that what the Apostle exhorts to "make sure" in this place is something special and saving.

You say the word bebaios is always in Scripture and in all other writings used to signify the certainty and stability of the thing itself, and never used to signify the assurance of knowledge. I am far from pretending to such an universal and perfect acquaintance not only with the Greek Testament but all other Greek writings, as to assert positively, as you do, that this word is used thus or thus always in 'em all, and never any otherwise. I know the word bebaios properly signifies "firm" or "sure." Yet I don't suppose there is anything in the Greek word, any more than the English word "firm," that hinders it being used for the firmness of evidence (or judgment, knowledge, or hope), or the firmness of a thing

-- 638 --

with regard to its evidence to the mind, any more than the firmness [of] the thing in itself. And however it is with all other Greek writings, yet if you had more fully examined the Greek Testament alone, and had not spoken at a venture, you would never have so positively asserted the contrary with regard to the use of the word there.

In 2 Corinthians 1:7 'tis expressly used for the firmness of hope: "Our hope of you is steadfast (bebaia)." In Colossians 2:7 'tis used for firmness of faith and so of the judgment, as it is also in Mark 16:20 and Hebrews 2:3 and in 1 Corinthians 1:6. In Hebrews 6:16 the word is used not for ratifying and making things firm in themselves, but firm in their evidence to men's minds. And in Tit 3:8 διαβεβαιουσθαι is put for affirming a thing as evident and certain. And the nature of the thing makes it evident, that the apostle Peter in this very text [2 Peter 1:10] does also use the word in this same sense, when he exhorts those he writes to to make their calling and election sure; not to make the thing firm and established in itself, but firm and sure as to the evidence of it. For neither their calling nor election (in any sense of the words) nor the saving benefits of them, could be made more firm in themselves than they were already, if they were true saints as the Apostle supposes (see v. 2 Peter 1:1 and 2 Peter 3:1), unless we hold falling from grace.

But if you hold this doctrine and this be the ground of your objection, I think you ought to have explained yourself on this head, that I might have known how to have replied to you in answer to this and other objections. But you not only say that this text is not to my purpose, but that it plainly makes against me; here I confess myself extremely at a loss what you should intend or have respect to, or which way you supposed the words not only did not make for me but against, and that plainly.

As to my saying that the Spirit of God in his saving operation communicates himself to the soul in his own proper nature,2 implying, as you suppose, God's communicating his essence. After all that is said, the objection is only about the use of language and propriety of expressions. It can't be anything else, for I have particularly explained my meaning and expressly declared what I do not mean, that by his proper nature I don't mean his essence; and have also declared particularly what I do mean, viz. that by the Spirit of God's communicating himself in his proper nature, I mean communicating something of his holiness. And this explanation I am careful to give immediately in the place where I first use the expression. And then, further to explain myself, say that "the grace which is in the hearts of the saints, is of the same nature with the divine holiness, as much as it is possible for that to be, which is infinitely

-- 639 --

less in degree."3 So that the exceeding great objection is, that I in your opinion signify this that I declare to be my meaning by an improper expression; and if that be still a grand difficulty with you, I hope you will consider the matter more fully.

I confess, my skill in the English tongue does not extend so far as to discern the great impropriety of the word as I have used it. The word "nature" is not used only to signify the essence of a thing, but is used very variously. Chambers in his Dictionary observes on this word as follows: "‘Nature’(natura, φύσις) is a term very variously used. Aristotle has a whole chapter wrote expressly to enumerate the various acceptations of the Greek word φύσις, rendered into English ‘nature,’ and among Latin writers the different acceptations are so many that a certain author reckons up fourteen or fifteen. Mr. Boyle, in a precise treatise of the vulgarly received notion of nature, gives us eight principal ones."4 That property which is natural to anyone and is eminently his character, I think, is, without abuse of language or going cross to the common use of it, called his proper nature, though [it] is not just the same with his essence. Thus we say concerning an exceeding good-natured man, that ingenuity is his very nature. I think holiness may, without absurdity, be said to be the proper nature of the Holy Spirit on two accounts: (1) As 'tis his peculiar beauty and glory and so may in a special manner be called his nature, as brightness may in a peculiar manner be said to be the nature of the sun, and [as] that which is in a peculiar manner the nature of honey is its sweetness. (2) 'Tis the proper character of the Spirit above all other things, in that office and work of his wherein we are concerned with him. This is that in his nature which he especially manifests and exercises in his office, acts, and operations towards us; and therefore this in his nature is singled out from all other things to denote him by, as he is revealed [to] us; so his name by which he is called in Scripture is the Holy Ghost. And this is that in his nature which he communicates something of to the saints, and therefore is called by divines in general a communicable attribute; and the saints are made partakers of his holiness, as the Scripture expressly declares (Hebrews 12:10), and that without imparting to them his essence.5

-- 640 --

Light and heat may in a special manner be said to be the proper nature of the sun; and yet none will say that everything to which the sun communicates a little of its light and heat has therefore communicated to it the essence of the sun, and is sunned with the sun, or becomes the same being with the sun, or becomes equal to that immense fountain of light and heat. A diamond or crystal that is held forth in the sun's beams may properly be said to have some of the sun's brightness communicated to it; for though it hasn't the same individual brightness with that which is inherent in the sun, and be immensely less in degree, yet it is something of the same nature.

I don't know that I have exceeded the due bounds of a writer in divinity, in expressing that communication that God makes to his saints of his holiness in the very forms that God himself does in his Word, in calling it their being made partakers of the divine nature; and I think the representation I have made of the matter is such as the scriptures do abundantly warrant in innumerable texts that might be mentioned.

With regard to the objections you make against what I say about a new spiritual sense given to the hearts of the saints, etc., and [my] calling it a new principle, a new habit giving a person ability and disposition to exert the faculties of the soul in exercises of a new kind. This you say is properly to introduce a new faculty. You...6
