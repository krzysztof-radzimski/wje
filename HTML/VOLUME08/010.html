APPENDIX III
HEAVEN IS A PROGRESSIVE STATE

THE "Miscellanies" attest that Edwards always believed in two sorts of progress in the sinless perfection of heaven. The first is best described by the running title of Miscell. no. 777 (written shortly after the charity sermons): HAPPINESS of HEAVEN is PROGRESSIVE and has various periods in which it has a new and glorious advancement, and consists very much in BEHOLDING the manifestations that God makes of himself in the WORK OF REDEMPTION "It seems to me probable," he wrote earlier in no. 421. heaven (c. 1730), "that that part of the church that is in heaven, have been from the beginning of the world progressive in their light and in their happiness, as the church on earth has; and that much of their happiness has consisted in seeing the progressive wonderful doings of God with respect to his church here in this world." This kind of progress manifests Edwards' superlative understanding of "the communion of the saints," which he expressed with stunning understatement at the end of that entry: "The church in heaven and the church on earth are more one people, one city, and one family than generally imagined."

Recall that the wheels of Ezekiel I were symbols crucial in Edwards' interpretation of God's end in creation. The heavenly saints are engaged in beholding God (just as earthly saints are) through contemplating, knowing, enjoying and glorying in his works on the earth.

It is in the wheels of his providence that move on earth, that he in his throne in heaven makes progress towards the ultimate end of the creation of both heaven and earth, and the ultimate end of all the affairs of both; for this is the end of the journey of the whole chariot, both wheels and throne, for both are moving towards the same journey's end. And the motion of all is by the wheels on earth; and if so, doubtless it is on these wheels that all the inhabitants of heaven, both saints and angels, are carried towards their ultimate end; for all are Christ's family, they are either his servants and attendants in the

-- 707 --

affair of redemption, which is the grand movement of the wheels, or are his members and parts of his body.1

So in heaven's increasing joys there is a replication of the story of the redemption that God has been working from the Fall of man to the end of the world. The "separated souls" who have died look forward (as we do) to things promised in Scripture: the reign of Christ on earth, his coming in glory, the resurrection, the Day of Judgment, Christ's enthronement as head of his people, and the marriage of the Lamb (cf. Revelation 19:7). Heaven's progressive state is correlated with the great events in the work of redemption on earth to the final consummation in heaven, variously designated by Edwards. It is important to know that as a biblical theologian Edwards gives most attention in his writings to this first sort of progress.

This progress, however, is taken up into his understanding of the "Beatifical vision." In seeing God and union with him, the story of redemption becomes a second sort of progress, or overflows into it. Edwards gives three accounts of why there is progress in eternal life with God. These grounds I provisionally call anthropological, metaphysical, and theological.

We begin, however, with the historical movement of the work of redemption. The saints above, like the angels, "know more than they did before Christ's incarnation. They know by the church, by God's dealings."2 Before that transitus to the church without national boundaries, however, there were "periods of new and glorious advancement" in the church of Israel, and there have been similar providential periods during the centuries since the age of the apostles. Also, the happiness of heaven "admits new joys" upon many "new occasions" accompanying the main events. If there is happiness in heaven over every sinner that repenteth (Luke 15:7), the happiness of that world must evermore be increasing. There was great joy over the saints who went to heaven with Christ's ascension, "for 'tis unreasonable to suppose they only arose for a few days to die again" (cf. Matthew 27:50–53). Joy over whole nations converted, over the success of the gospel after the ascension, over the reformation from popery, over popes and persecutors cast into hell3— and over the plantation of the church in America. Other entries mention increase in heaven at the time of Constantine the Great, the overthrow of

-- 708 --

Antichrist, and the reign of Christ on earth. That the happiness of heaven is unchanging must surely be a false notion.

Things promised in Scripture and yet to come will be an "exceeding great" addition of happiness and glory to the saints. "Separated souls" now in heaven are (in more ways than beholding the work of redemption) "progressive before the resurrection": "I mean that they are progressive with a preparation for another and more perfect state; their state is itinerary, viatory; their state, their employments, their glory and happiness, is subordinate to a future, more glorious state."4 Thus Edwards writes of the resurrected Christ sitting with his bride in the Father's throne that "the state of things then is the state of perfection; all the state of the church before, both in earth and in heaven, is a growing state."5

In another entry6 roughly contemporary with the charity sermons, Edwards used the figural analysis of the twofold "imperfects" and the twofold "perfects" introduced in sermon fifteen7 (citing also 1 Corinthians 13:10, 1 Corinthians 13:12 on the progression from seeing in a glass darkly to seeing face to face, from childhood to manhood) to account for the growth still to come to souls departed. Expressed as a mathematical proportion, the Mosaic dispensation: the gospel dispensation:: the gospel dispensation: the happiness of separated souls now in heaven. Or as the happiness of the church in the millennium: the happiness of heaven. In each of the latter periods a "more perfect" has come. Each of the former ages was "no glory" by comparison. Similarly, post-resurrection glory and happiness will exceed the present happiness of separated souls in heaven, who nevertheless are said already to be "the spirits of just men made perfect," Hebrews 12:23. Thus figural interpretation of the future work of redemption lent itself to manifold perfects, not only the twofold perfects of the fifteenth sermon. Separately or together, resurrection/judgment/ enthronement/reign/the marriage of the Lamb are the end of all things.

The story of redemption reaches the final consummation toward which the whole creation moves in events that Edwards holds together as one in a lengthy appendix to the entry we are discussing.

The end of the creation of God was to provide a spouse for his Son Jesus Christ that might enjoy him and on whom he might pour forth his love, and the end of all things in providence are to make way for the

-- 709 --

exceeding expressions of Christ's love to his spouse and for her union with and high and glorious enjoyment of him, and to bring this to pass; and therefore the last thing and issue of all things is the marriage of the Lamb, and the wedding day is the last day, the day of judgment; or rather this will be the beginning of it. The wedding feast is eternal and the love and joys, the songs, entertainments and glories of the wedding never will be ended. It will be an everlasting wedding day.

This was Edwards' exultant comment on Revelation 19:7, "for the marriage of the Lamb is come and his wife hath made herself ready."8 Ephesians 1:9–10, on the gathering together in one of all things in Christ, was cited shortly before the foregoing quotation.

We come now to the second sense in which heaven is a progressive state. An important clue to the meaning of this second sort of progress is that an active hope continues to abide in heaven forever. Beyond heavenly progress correlated with great periods or events in the history of redemption until its consummation in the marriage of the Lamb, there is another sort of progress that Edwards evidently expected. This is progress for the "eternal duration," the subject of the peroration with which End of Creation concludes, and of the ending of Sec. III of Ch. I of that dissertation as well. Perfection apparently admits of both degrees and growth.

Edwards gives three reasons for his understanding of progressive perfection in heaven. The three reasons or grounds may be roughly divided into anthropological, metaphysical, and theological. The theological calls for the most extended explanation, if for no other reason than that, under this head, Edwards also gives two accounts of the way, process, or manner in which infinite increase is expected to ensue after the end time of the work of redemption in heaven. The double question to which we turn is this: Why must and how can there be progress of the church in heaven within the everlasting wedding day of the Lamb?

-- 710 --

1. First, the anthropological ground for this hope. "That the glorified spirits shall grow in holiness and happiness to eternity," Edwards wrote when only twenty years old, "I argue from this foundation, that their number of ideas shall increase to eternity." They came to heaven with a finite number of experiences. The time will come in the "eternal duration" when the parts of that duration (suppose each equals a million ages) will be more in number than the ideas the saints brought with them to heaven. "Now we cannot suppose that they will ever entirely forget everything that has passed in heaven and in the universe, for a whole million million ages." This would be a very unreasonable supposition, because "'tis undoubted that they never will have forgot what passed in their life upon earth," what passed in "the first seventy years," i.e., the sins they were saved from, God's special mercies, their regeneration, particular providences in church and world, spiritual friendships, their own deaths. From their lives on earth the saints "retain innumerable multitudes of ideas" that shall never be "erased from out their minds." From these considerations Edwards draws the conclusion that "their knowledge will increase to eternity; and if their knowledge, doubtless their holiness." For the greater their knowledge of God and "the more they see of his excellency," "the more will they love him; and the more they love God, the more delight and happiness... will they have in him." Edwards cites Psalms 89:1–2 as Scripture evidence, of which the first part of ver. Psalms 89:2 is crucial: "For I have said, Mercy shall be built up for ever."9

This anthropological reason for the doctrine presupposes Edwards' understanding of the self as "consciousness to self,"1 or that "a sort of genius is with a man, that accompanies him" when he goes to heaven, "so that he has a conversation with himself, that is... with his own idea,"2 and with past graces remembered. It presupposes that consciousness to oneself of joys during the first seventy years is yet another moment of rejoicing in heaven, so that the whole is cumulative.

Two observations will suffice concerning this reason. First, how striking is the difference between Edwards' view of the span of a human memory from that of a twentieth-century philosopher who tried to say something sensible about life after death. John Hick's harmony of the

-- 711 --

Christian notion of conscious individual immortality with Eastern notions of nirvana rests upon his assumption that as the time span of self-consciousness lengthens, it must snap or collapse; that there must be some limit to the conscious self-identity of the soul; that the soul eventually passes over into a state of fullness in which any sort of individuality is lost or no longer matters.3 Second, how odd is the view of modernity that belief in an afterlife must underrate the importance of the realities of the lives people are now living. To the contrary, Edwards' teaching would seem to place an almost unbearable weight of glory upon "the first seventy years" of those who go to great happiness. This weight is at least as intolerable to massed millions of people in modern society as the opposite weight placed on the same time span of those who were believed to go to great misery.

2. Edwards also gives a metaphysical reason for the doctrine of eternal progress. I use the word metaphysical to distinguish this from the cluster of "theological" reasons that are most characteristic of Edwards. The propriety of speaking of more and more perfection in holiness and happiness decoupled from the history of redemption in the eternal duration of creatures (angelic or human) was, for Edwards, an entailment of their finitude. It is an error to think that the "constancy" of the heavenly world differs from the visible world in that "its state of things were as it were immovable and immutable." Heaven "is subject to no evil changes, yet 'tis subject to great changes and revolutions of a contrary nature." While the illustrations given in this brief entry4 are of revolutions corresponding to the turns of the wheels on earth, or "preparatory" to the changes to come in the last days, a different argument is also given for the plausibility of such expectancy among the souls of men made perfect, namely, "'Tis God only that is unchangeable. The whole universe consisting in upper and lower worlds is in a changing state." God alone is immutable, unchanging. All creatures are the opposite of that by virtue of their finitude. We ought not to suppose that in the end of the end time of redemption in heaven men become gods and that the good angels ("confirmed" only at that great consummation!) also become gods, i.e., become no longer creatures. Therefore we must not suppose that heaven is inherently an unchanging society. Again, in another entry whose title refers to heavenly progress that replicates events on earth, one reason given is: "For perfection in holiness, i.e., a sinless

-- 712 --

perfection, is not such in those that are finite but it admits of infinite degrees."5 The finitude of the saints and angels in heaven argues that sinless perfection is subject to change, which Edwards believed must mean change for the better to all eternity.

Edwards agrees with Augustine that time is the measure of the motion of creatures. For him this meant that time and the movement of creatures go on forever in some sense within the eternal duration of heaven. Of course, the movement conceivably could be forever going in circles (the ancient symbol of perfection) around the throne of glory singing God's praise, the revolution of creatures having different ranks of nearness or remoteness to him and different degrees of divine knowledge, love, and joy in their overflowing hearts. Clearly, that was no option for Edwards. As a vector from the movement of the throne in heaven with the wheels on earth, Edwards' notion of further progress was, quite naturally, linear, whether toward heights immeasurable or into divine depths unsearchable.

3. Edwards' distinguishing theological reasons are continuous with the argument of End of Creation. If the end of the first creation was to declare God's glory, so must be the end of the new creation. If communicating his fullness was God's end in any of his works, that communication must be as infinite as that fullness is. Heaven's progress might be finite if, but only if, God's riches are finite, joy in him exhaustible— or if he turns out to be of a reserved, even a stingy disposition. So Edwards argues infinite progress in heaven from the infinite greatness and goodness of the end God had in creation. "If anything of that nature [God's declarative glory] be of any importance or worth at all in the eyes of God on account of the fitness of the thing, or anything else, it will then follow that all of it that ever he will receive or all that he has in view is of infinite importance in his eyes. For the sum total of the glory God is to receive is infinite, for he will be glorified to all eternity; and those that shall render him his tribute of glory will to eternity be increasing in their knowledge of his glory, and so in the degree of their love and praise to eternity."6 In another entry marked for use, Edwards infers, from the infinite value and worthiness of God's glory being known to creatures for all eternity, that such knowledge will increase indefinitely. The "increasing knowledge of God in all

-- 713 --

elect creatures to all eternity is an existence, a reality, infinitely worthy to be in itself... If existence is more worthy than defect and nonentity, and if any created existence is in itself worthy to be, then this [increasing] knowledge of God and his glory is worthy to be."7

This may become clearer as we now turn from the Why must? to the How can? heaven forever be a progressive state, i.e., to the way or manner in which Edwards imaged the eternal increase of the knowledge and love of God and joy in him and in one another in heaven. For, as we will see, the account of how increase is to be expected gives us (or dramatically exhibits) the content and the contours of Edwards' vast eschatological vision.

There are a number of "Miscellanies" in which Edwards set forth two ways or manners (or, we may distinguish two ways) in which the soul's heavenly progress might be conceived to take place. This can be either by God and Christ opening a more extended view of or deeper insight into God's excellency, or by the enlargement of the soul's capacity to know, love, and enjoy God's excellency. In a crucial passage in which this distinction is made, we will see, it is the activity of God and Christ in opening more and more of the divine beauty, glory, love, and mercy that works to enlarge the creature's faculty or capacity for receiving these excellencies. Before coming to that, however, we need to note passages in which capacity serves to fix limits in gradation of perfection.

In the final sermon of the charity series, Edwards explained that different ranks among heaven's inhabitants (their different degrees of love, different capacities for love) are no denial of the Apostle's "that which is perfect is come," 1 Corinthians 13:10. "The love of the angels and saints to God and Christ is perfect in its kind, or with such a perfection as is proper to their nature, perfect with a sinless perfection, and perfect in that it is commensurate with the capacities of their natures."8 The perfection of that society is the removal from it of any "comparative spirit" of pride or envy. Its positive perfection is the fullness of love and enjoyment (proportioned to the amiableness or fitness of greater benevolence to souls who have more benevolence to Being) flowing from the lower to the higher saints, and the greater humility of higher to lower, among the storied mansions of heaven.

-- 714 --

This account of capacity was fully written out by Edwards in his twentieth year. Lest anyone believe that a society of unequals must foster a comparative spirit, Edwards begins this early entry by supposing the inhabitants to be equals. Nonetheless, each of these equal citizens will know that there were other "possible worlds" God might have chosen! "For if they were all equal, and all full of happiness, yet everyone would know that greater happiness is possible absolutely, and possible for them if God had enlarged their capacity; and why should not they, who are acted by pure reason, desire it as much as if it were actually enjoyed by some beings?" But the bare possibility of greater happiness need be no "damp" on the happiness of saintly persons of equal capacity; nor, since they are "acted by pure reason," would it be any damp that "it [greater happiness] is [actually] enjoyed by other beings." Thus the young Edwards of 1723, no less than the mature preacher of 1738, believed that "most certainly there is a pure, ardent even inconceivably vehement mutual love between the glorified saints; and this love is in proportion to the perfection and amiableness of the object loved," which admits of degrees. The young Edwards described a heaven of unequals as "everyone being completely satisfied and full of happiness, having as much as he is capable of enjoying or desiring, like a vessel thrown into the sea of happiness."9 The image of vessel for capacity recurs in several "Miscellanies."

So in these early entries Edwards understood heaven to be a world whose inhabitants have unequal and— shall our interpretation be— fixed but overflowing capacities? The 1738 sermon was in this respect not significantly different from a depiction of degrees of glory written ten years before:

Christ by his righteousness purchased for everyone perfect happiness; that is, he merited that their capacity should be filled with happiness. But this don't hinder but that the saints, being of various capacities, may have various degrees of happiness, and yet all their happiness be the fruit of Christ's purchase.

Edwards hesitated to say that Christ purchased different degrees of happiness. To speak properly,

he purchased eternal life, that is perfect happiness, or which is the same thing, that everyone's capacity should be filled. The saints are like so many vessels of different sizes cast into a sea of happiness, where

-- 715 --

each vessel is full: this is eternal life, for a man forever to have his capacity filled.1

This brings us to the crucial passage that distinguishes the "actual views" God gives from the "largeness" of a creature's capacity or faculty for enjoying those views.

What I mean by the largeness of capacity for happiness don't consist only in the strength and extent of the faculties but in the actual views which God gives— whereby the appetite of the souls is excited and extended and the enjoying faculty (if I may so speak) is as it were opened and prepared to receive such a degree and such a manner of delight and satisfaction.

In this entry, the degrees of capacity depend on three things: "the extent and strength of the faculties," "the degree of notional knowledge," and "the degree and manner of those spiritual views that God lets into their minds and the particular manifestations that he is pleased to make of himself, enlarging the appetite and opening the heart and extending the vessel, viz. the enjoying faculty; opening the mouth wide to receive the more."2

Then Edwards had to insure that increase in perfection entailed no improper craving for it, or disturbance of mind over such a prospect. Otherwise, the saints above would not be "just men" already "made perfect" in some true sense. Their rejoicing during the everlasting wedding day would be flawed by anticipation of more joy to come if there were any impatient craving for future joys now. Just as perfection requires that vessels of different sizes all be filled with all the joy they can hold, so there must be no privation or defect of the joy now in heaven because of uneasiness or anxiety over the possibility that the future increase could be frustrated. Just as the summum bonum must be not only the highest good but also a good that passes not away and cannot be lost against our wills and loves, so the infinite increase of good must be a surety. Otherwise, the hope that abides in heaven would not be a perfected hope; through every moment of the eternal duration the saints would live in sight of greater good that, against their wills and loves, might not come. Better to have their unchanging vessels filled, without that further conceit. So in the entry we are considering Edwards wrote not only, "Everyone shall have his craving filled," but added immediately,

-- 716 --

"i.e., in such a sense as to leave no uneasiness of craving, but not so as to leave no desire of increasing... [T]heir desires shall be no more than a suitable preparation for delight in their satisfaction."3 So we can say of hope that it is perfected desire.

Edwards ended his sermon series with the words, "And if ever you arrive in heaven, faith and love must be the wings which must carry you there." He had just spoken of how a "strong hope" casts out fear.4 So faith and hope and love must be the wings that carry you there. We may now also say, If ever you are to increase more and more in heaven's perfection, faith and hope and love will always be the wings of the soul that carry you ever deeper into the unfathomable mystery or higher and higher in exalted praise and delight in God's glory. Edwards did not share the view that in heaven faith disappears in sight (leaving only a formal faith) and hope ceases with its substantive realization (also leaving only its form), while love alone is the eternal activity of the soul enjoying all it sees of God and seeing all it wills or desires. Eternal progress may or may not fit very well with medieval notions of the summum bonum and the vision of God. However, Edwards' account of the progressive end of the happiness of heaven as God's end in creation, providence, and redemption is, I judge, coherent with what he says about the "Beatifical vision." These references are considered below. To anticipate, we may say here: the plenitude of God is infinite; God's fullness dwells forever in Jesus Christ; the God-man never ceases to be the Great Communicator. As the view Christ lets into the society of heaven increases, the capacities of the inhabitants continue to be made new. In the entire history of Christian theology, there is no more dynamic understanding of the relation of God to his creatures than that of Jonathan Edwards.

Had we only Miscell. no. 822 (c. 1740–42)— with its dynamic image of the view God and Christ "lets into" our minds enlarging the enjoying faculty— we might conjecture that it was the Great Awakening that shattered Edwards' vessels of unchanging perfection and opened the way to eternal progress within God's end in creation. Whatever development may have been occasioned by the Great Awakening, or by Edwards' later theological explorations, there is abundant evidence that eternal progress in heaven was never far from his thought. After all, the notion that the plenitude of God's grace, and his letting human beings in

-- 717 --

on his unsearchable riches, enlarges the human capacity to know, love, and rejoice in him is not a difficult notion to come by. Perhaps it required only Edwards' powerful massing of images to overcome the seeming rational contradiction in saying that perfect holiness may have progressive degrees.

Two additional early entries in the "Miscellanies" are worth consideration, these composed during Edwards' tutorship at Yale, when in his early twenties. In one he wrote: "Their sight shall reach further and further; and new things shall [be] plainly present to their minds, without any admixture of error."5 The second selection reminds us that it is not only God and Christ but other beings as well who are "all over lovely" in heaven, and it also tells us that creatures who are spirit-beings are also unsearchable if they are loved in the love of God:

How soon do earthly lovers come to an end of their discoveries of each other's beauty; how soon do they see all that is to be seen!... And how happy is that love, in which there is an eternal progress in all these things; wherein new beauties are continually discovered, and more and more loveliness, and in which we shall forever increase in beauty ourselves; where we shall be made capable of finding out and giving, and shall receive, more and more endearing expressions of love forever: our union will become more close, and communication more intimate.6

We need also to attend to an early entry evidencing Edwards' remarkable location of an active hope in heaven. He had developed quite early in life his understanding of a "sanctified hope" as an element of perfection in heaven. He needed such a pure hope even to depict the saints' anticipation of as yet future events promised in Scripture. Part of the happiness of separated souls before the resurrection, and of the resurrected saints awaiting the marriage of the Lamb, is hope of what is to come. For example, in order to show that the church in heaven is a "growing state" from resurrection to that wedding day, Edwards wrote:

Indeed, the spirits of just men made perfect will be perfectly free from sin and sorrow, will have inexpressible, inconceivable happiness and perfect contentment; but yet part of their happiness will consist in hope of what is to come. They will have as much happiness as they will desire in their present state, because they will choose to have the addition at that time and in that order which God has designed; it will be every

-- 718 --

way most pleasing and satisfying and contenting to them, that it should be so. Their having perfect happiness don't exclude all increase, nor does it exclude all hope; for we don't know but they will increase in happiness forever... [A]nd yet part of that happiness, part of that sweet rest and contenting joy, consists in the sight of what is future. They don't desire that addition should be now; they know that it will [be]... every way most desirable, that it should be in God's order.7

Edwards had only to transfer this notion of hope in heaven (elucidated when he was twenty-five years old) to fill in the endless progress sketched with geometric images at the end of End of Creation. But progress for the eternal duration is mentioned only hesitantly here: "for we don't know but they will increase in happiness forever."

Progress for the eternal duration began with the marriage of the Lamb. That event was the transitus to heaven as progress in seeing God and enjoying him forever, to Edwards' extraordinary understanding of the chief end of man. Among the "Miscellanies," a continuation of No. 952 under 954 is entitled HEAVEN MADE MORE GLORIOUS AFTER THE DAY OF JUDGEMENT. This is an exultant argument that the highest heaven, in which God dwells from all eternity, where his throne is situated, will be marvelously changed in preparation for the marriage of the Lamb. But Edwards was not able to limit the active role of Christ to that vast transformation. The Bridegroom continues for an everlasting wedding day to be God's way to his redeemed and glorified people.

All communicated glory to the creature must be by the Son of God who is the brightness or shining forth of his Father's glory... For all that God doth by Christ as the medium of communication between himself and the creature since Christ became God-man, or at least since as God-man he has been glorified and enthroned as Lord of the universe he doth by Christ as God-man, in whom it hath pleased the Father that all fullness should dwell... The old creation was by him, the highest heavens were created by him, for without him was not anything made that was made... If God committed to him the creation of the old world, much more would he commit to him the creation of the new; for this is his business, to renew all things.

Indeed, we can go so far as to say that progress in the perfect happiness and holiness of heaven is the vision of God, and that to provide that vision remains forever the undiminished role of the God-man.

-- 719 --

All that has been said so far about increasing views of God, enlarged capacities, and enjoying faculties of his people, I suggest, is grounded in three remarkable passages by Edwards on the "Beatifical vision." Edwards' Table to his "Miscellanies" lists two citations under "Beatifical vision," Miscell. nos. 678 and 777 (corol. 1). The first was probably composed shortly after the 1734–35 revival in Northampton, and the second in the year Charity was preached, or soon thereafter. The first of these references simply raises the question that was important for Edwards in contrast to medieval notions of the vision of God or the summum bonum: "Whether there be any visible appearance or glory that is the symbol of the divine presence in which God manifests himself in heaven besides the glorified body of Christ?"8 The second reference, corol. 1 of No. 777, reads in its entirety:

Hence that BEATIFICAL VISION that the saints have of God in heaven is beholding the manifestations that he makes of himself in the work of redemption. For that arguing of the being and perfections of God that may be a priori don't seem to be called "seeing God" in Scripture, but only that which is by manifestations God makes of himself in his Son. All other ways of knowing God are by seeing him in Christ the Redeemer, the image of the invisible God, and in his works, or the effects of his perfections in his redemption and the fruits of it (which effects are the principal manifestation or shining forth of his perfections). And in conversing with them by Christ, which conversation is chiefly those things done and manifested in this work, if we may judge by the subject of God's conversation with his church by his Word in this world. And so we may infer that the business and employment of the saints so far as it consists in contemplation, praise and conversation is mainly in contemplating the wonders of this work, in praising God for the displays of his glory and love therein, and in conversing about things appertaining to it.

Here was Edwards' answer to the question raised by the first reference to the "Beatifical vision." The passage concerns how the saints "see" God in the eternal duration of heaven. Because the Son once incarnate remains forever the God-man, the Father's elected disclosure of himself remains forever the same: through the Word made flesh. As there is no immediate seeing of God on earth, so also it is in heaven. Or rather: to see God

-- 720 --

immediately is to see him in Christ. The saints see God through the exalted and glorified one of us men in whom the Son was incarnate (the Father's sending and, by protological covenant, the Son's coming and acting in unison both ad intra and ad extra). That remains forever God's way to man, if it ever was. Thus earlier, in Miscell. no. 177, Edwards wrote: "[T]here is no creature can thus have an immediate sight of God but only Jesus Christ who is in the bosom of God." In this world we see God in his image; the saints above also "see him in his image which especially is his Son Jesus Christ," the image of the invisible God. They converse with him through his voluntary manifestations and significations of his mind— essentially, the Son. It remains true for the eternal duration that "no one hath seen God at any time but the only begotten Son who is in the bosom of the Father. He hath declared him, and no one knows the Father but the Son and he to whom the Son will reveal him." It remains forever true that "'Tis God's pleasure that Christ should be the light, the sun of heaven by which God should be seen and known there. For it pleases the Father that in him all fullness should dwell."

This particular understanding of the eternality of the incarnation— the eternality of the Son's mediatorial role— is, I judge, significantly different from the notion of the beatific vision of God in medieval theology. Thus, Dante spoke of the final vision granted him as a pilgrim in paradise:

I believe that, because of the keenness of the living ray which I endured, I should have been lost if my eyes had been turned from it. I remember that on this account I was the bolder to sustain it, until I united my gaze with the Infinite Goodness.

O abounding grace whereby I presumed to fix my look through the Eternal Light so far that all my sight was spent therein.

In its depth I saw ingathered, bound by love in one single volume, that which is dispersed in leaves throughout the universe: substance and accidents and their relations, as though fused together in such a way that what I tell is but a simple light. The universal form of this knot I believe that I saw, because, in telling this, I feel my joy increase...

In that Light one becomes such that it is impossible he should ever consent to turn himself from it for other sight; for the good, which is the object of the will, is all gathered in, and outside of it that is defective which is perfect there.9

Then Dante's powerful image of the Trinity is introduced.

Within the profound and shining subsistence of the lofty Light appeared to me three circles of three colors and one magnitude; and one seemed reflected by the other, as rainbow by rainbow, and the third seemed fire breathed forth equally from the one and the other.1

The innertrinitarian relations, however, are the mystery of the Light long sought, not mediatorial incarnation:

O Light Eternal, who alone abidest in Thyself, alone knowest Thyself, and, known to Thyself knowing, lovest and smilest on Thyself!2

Dante's curiosity is fixed on the second circle, the Son:

That circling which, thus begotten, appeared in Thee as reflected light, when my eye had dwelt on it for a time, seemed to me depicted with our image within itself and in its own color, wherefore my sight was entirely set upon it.3

Like a geometer, Dante wished to take the measure of that circle, "to see how the image [our image] conformed to the circle and how it has its place therein; but my own wings were not sufficient for that." His "lofty phantasy" failed, and Paradiso ends with: "but already my desire and my will were revolved, like a wheel that is evenly moved, by the Love which moves the sun and the other stars."4 Dante, a mortal, was briefly permitted to see the truth of the incarnation. Perhaps the saints above in their vision of God have their sight with unfailing, immortal vision set upon how the circle representing the eternal Son is "depicted with our image within itself and its color." We need not suppose that the revelation to them of the mystery of the incarnation was transitory. Perhaps that remains obliquely within their vision of the Light Eternal. Nevertheless, their united gaze is upon the Trinity, whom they see immediately. Or so I interpret Dante.

The point is clear enough in Aquinas, whose cue for understanding beatitude or the vision of God is 1 John 3:2, "We shall see him as he is." (This, of course, Edwards does not deny.) The classic locus for Thomas' discussion is Summa Theologica, Ia, 12, 1–10.

-- 722 --

The contention that beatitude cannot consist in the vision of God because such an object absolutely surpasses human capacities is ruled out by Aquinas on theological and philosophical grounds. It is contrary to faith: since we are assured by faith that God is our ultimate good, we must suppose that our ultimate happiness will consist in a vision of the essence of God which will completely fulfill our highest human capacities as spiritual beings, intellect and will. To deny this destiny is also contrary to what we can know about human nature: if the rational creature were incapable of attaining knowledge of the first cause of things, then its natural tendency to know the causes of things would in the end be doomed to frustration. It is true that God transcends all creaturely knowledge, but this rules out creaturely comprehension, not vision, of his essence. No created likeness can mediate this vision, though the human mind must receive an infusion of the grace of glory to permit the human being to enjoy the vision of God. Everything else which is seen, known, and loved by the blessed in heaven is seen, known, and loved in the vision of the divine essence. Nothing short of this vision can render human beings ultimately happy.

So far Edwards would agree, including in his agreement Aquinas' view that no created likeness— no "secondary beauty," which is only "some image"— can mediate the vision of God, and that there is need for an infusion of grace or divine love in the heart. A possible major difference lies elsewhere. Aquinas addresses the specific issue of the role of the humanity of Christ in the beatific vision in only two places (as far as I can discover), both in his commentary on the Sentences of Peter Lombard. In both cases, the issue arises in connection with other matters. (1) In the context of a discussion of "enjoyment," he wrote: "Since the humanity of Christ is not the ultimate end, the vision of it will not be enjoyment properly speaking, but a certain concomitant joy not beatitude as such."5 Then again, (2) when probing the motive for the incarnation, he wrote: "The complete beatitude of man lies in divinity itself which the intellect attains without mediation, from which glory redounds to the lower parts of the soul and to the body as well. In the beatific vision the humanity of Christ will be a concomitant joy in the victory of his passion. Thus it remains true that if man had not sinned, Christ would not have suffered."6 Thus, although Christ's humanity is

-- 723 --

an element in the beatific vision, it does not perform a central role in heaven in our enjoyment of the vision of God.

In comparison with Dante and Aquinas, Edwards gives a remarkably different account of the knowledge of God and joy in him opened to creatures in heaven. For Edwards, Jesus Christ, the incarnate God-man, continues to play an active and the central role in heaven's conversation even after that conversation has ceased to be about the great earthly events in the work of redemption, or about the heavenly events promised in Scripture to the church triumphant. Look now at Edwards' third and most extensive treatment of the "Beatifical vision,"7 with Thomas Aquinas in the background.

The earliest of our three passages on the "Beatifical vision" (the sermon on Romans 2:10, "Glory, honour and peace to every man that worketh good") outlines "the successive parts" of the happiness of the saints, as follows: "This vision of God is the heaven of heaven[s] and therefore I would speak of it a little particularly. And (1) I would consider the faculty that is the immediate subject of this vision. (2) The nature of the act of vision or seeing God. (3) The Object, and (4) Manner, and (5) Means (6) Effects." The first two of these points serve to remind us that human beatitude is in seeing God himself. No Christian theologian denies that the pure in heart shall see God (Matthew 5:8) or that "he that hath seen me hath seen the Father" (John 14:9)— although I judge Edwards' stress to be more on "uniting love" and "participation" than on vision, certainly by the time he wrote the two posthumously published dissertations. As to the faculty, "'tis an intellectual view. The Beatifical Vision of God is not a sight with the eyes of the body, but with the eye of the soul... 'tis in seeing God who is a spirit spiritually with the Eye of the soul." This must be so, if the angels and "separated souls" before the resurrection see God. But the saints in heaven in their glorified bodies see Christ also with "bodily eyes"; and they see God in heaven not otherwise than "by the Glorified body of Christ." This must be so, because there is no need for the glory of God (spoken of in Scripture) to assume some "aerial form or shape any more" since Christ has actually assumed an human body."

The seeing God or the Glorified Body of Christ is the most perfect way of

-- 724 --

seeing God with the bodily eyes that can be. For in seeing a real body that one of the Persons of the Trinity has assumed to be his body and that he dwells in forever as his own, in which the divine majesty and excellency as much as 'tis possible for it to appear in outward form or shape, the saints do actually see a divine person with bodily eyes in the same manner as we see one another.

Since the Word was made flesh and we beheld his glory full of grace and truth (John 1:14), "Christ does really subsist in a glorified body." This, observes Edwards, seems to have been one end that God had in view: that the saints "may see him not only in the understanding but in all ways of seeing that human nature is capable of. That we might see God as a divine person as we see one another."8 God in heaven still stoops to the creature's condition.

As for the act of vision (Edwards' second point), it will be "an immediate sight." By "immediate" he means no longer through a "glass darkly," i.e., through the Scriptures or by making use of stated ordinances, but "face to face" (1 Corinthians 13:12). Under the nature of the act of vision Edwards spoke briefly of topics we have noticed more fully above. He distinguishes the perfection of the view God opens to the saints, their capacities for sight, and of the enlargement of both (here, however, not increasingly).9 He also distinguishes between perfection or extensiveness of understanding and complete comprehension, to deny the latter— as did Aquinas— to any creature.1

As for the object of vision (Edwards' third point), "they shall see everything in God that gratifies love... They shall see as much love in God towards them as they desire. They neither will nor can crave for any more. This very manifestation that God will make of himself that will cause the

-- 725 --

Beatifical vision will be an act of love in God."2 Since an act of love in God causes the vision, we can understand why Edwards' stress was on union and not sight:

Love desires union. They shall therefore see this glorious God united to themselves and see themselves united to him... They shall see God gloriously present with them. God with them and God in them as they in God. Love desires the possession of its object. Therefore they shall see God as their own God when they behold this transcendent glory. God they shall see as their own... They shall see it [that glorious power and wisdom of God] as altogether engaged for them when they shall see the bounty of God's holiness... They shall see it to be all theirs.

The manner of the vision of God (Edwards' fourth point) is "as having communion with Christ therein." Here the distinctiveness of Edwards' understanding of final beatitude is most impressive. The saints

enjoy God as partaking with Christ of his enjoy[ment] of God, for they are united to him and are Glorified and made happy in the enjoyment of God as his members... They being in Christ shall partake of the love of God the Father to Christ, and as the Son knows the Father, so they shall partake with him in his sight of God as being as it were parts with him. As he is in the bosom of the Father so are they in the bosom of the Father. As he has immense joy in the love of the Father so have they every one of them in their measure the same joy in the love of the Father... Herein they shall enjoy God in a more exalted and excellent manner than man would have done if he had never fallen.

Heaven is but a continuation of God's answer to Christ's High Priestly Prayer in John 17— the end for which God created the world.

The means (fifth point), as on earth, so in heaven. As grace and divine love in the heart are the Spirit's indwelling in his own, proper nature, so "'tis the same Holy Spirit by which the Beatifical Vision is given of God in Heaven... All [holiness and light] is from God by his Holy Spirit as it is here. They shall have this Beatifical Vision of God because they will be full of God, filled with the Holy Spirit of God."

As for the effects (sixth point), Edwards exults in the soul inflamed with love, entirely satisfied with pleasure, yet most active.

The soul will not be an inactive spectator but shall be most active, shall be in the most ardent exercise of love towards the object seen. The soul shall be as it were all eye to behold and yet all act to love. All aflame of love, the soul shall be as full of love as it shall be of light, and of both. It shall be as full as it can hold. The understanding will be in its most perfect act in beholding and the will will be in its most perfect act in loving... The soul shall be in its place at all times, adoring at God's feet [perfectly humble] and yet embraced in the arms of his love.

In this sermon on Romans 2:10 (c. 1735), as in the final sermon of the 1738 series, Edwards chose not to develop his belief in progress for the eternal duration:

The sight of God shall satisfy the soul with pleasure. So great will the joy be that the soul will desire no Greater. It shall be full, as great as the large desires of the soul can stretch themselves to. So sweet shall it be that the soul will desire nothing sweeter. So pure and excellent will it be that the soul will desire nothing better.

Here (as in sermon fifteen) there is no nuance of the enjoying faculty's enlargement or of indefinite increase in the view of himself that God and Christ let into the soul.

Nevertheless, we have in Edwards a most remarkable Christological understanding of eternal life, born of his comprehension of this subject by "God's conversation with his church by his Word in this world."3

Most remarkably, "participation in the divine nature"— which is the key to understanding End of Creation— is in Edwards' account of the beatifical vision specified as participation in the innertrinitarian life of

-- 727 --

God, to partake with Christ in his sight of God, to have the same joy in love of the Father, etc.4 These eternal participations cannot be thought static, any more than the divine life can be. Hence the creature's share must be ever increasing in the knowledge, love, and enjoyment of God's knowledge, God's love, and God's enjoyment, while that share (because finite) never reaches an eternal moment when God and Christ let no more of the divine life into the soul or that soul cries Hold, enough!

After this exhibition of Edwards' doctrine of perpetual progress in heaven, his understanding of the hope that activates that world, and his dynamic Christological account of the beatifical vision of God, the reader may well ask, Is there anything analogous to it in the history of Christian eschatology? I suggest that a parallel with Gregory of Nyssa (c. 330– c. 394) is worth examination. According to Gregory, the soul grows by constant participation in the divine, which is without form or limit, and is therefore inexhaustible. So the perfection that the soul shares remains ever the same, and yet is always discovered by the soul to be no less transcendent. Edwards affirms a not dissimilar never-ending asymptotic progress, approaching ever nearer the Good, ever arriving, never arriving.5 Edwards' geometric image is flawed in that he believed, as did Gregory, that the greater the nearness of their approach, the greater is the saints' apprehension of distance from God's excellency.

God's inexhaustible plenitude, not only the finitude of creatures, is the principal ground for both theologians' expectation of indefinite increase. Yet finitude furnishes some reason. Gregory's view of the "new creation," never far from Edwards' thought, was that spiritual substance "is constantly being created, ever changing for the better in its growth in perfection; along these lines no limit can be envisaged, nor can its progressive growth in perfection be limited by any term."6 The term of the eternal progress in Edwards is a kind of theosis, an Eastern Orthodox teaching largely misunderstood in the West in much the same fashion as Edwards on the full extent of "participation in the divine nature" was misunderstood in the eighteenth century.7 The expressions and concepts Edwards used to explain infinite progress within perfect blessedness are to be found in Gregory. This, we saw, was because the

-- 728 --

view God and Christ let into the soul increases, and thereby creatures' capacities— their "enjoying faculties"— are enlarged evermore. This coheres with Gregory's teaching on God's end in creation:

The rational nature came into being for this purpose, that the wealth of divine goods might not be idle. Receptacles with the faculty of choice were constructed like vases by the wisdom that sustains all things in order that there would be some place capable of receiving these goods, a place that always becomes larger because of what is additionally poured into it... [T]he capability increases as it is nourished by the abundance of goods, and the nourishing agent abounds with the increased growth.8

At the point of the reciprocity between the divine good and the human capacity there is perhaps a primary difference between Gregory and Edwards, for Gregory celebrates the nisus of the soul toward the Unreachable. A kind of demiurgical desire propels the soul; and also the soul's "prior accomplishments" renews "its intensity for the flight." The pleroma of desire enlarges the capacity, and not alone the splendor, of the vision. "Activity directed toward virtue causes its capacity to grow through exertion; this kind of activity does not slacken its intensity by the effort, but increases it."9 By contrast, for Edwards all is of grace, both the view let into the soul and the enlargement of the vessel. Christ is the dynamic activator of every increase, as on earth, so in heaven. So the

-- 729 --

inhabitants of heaven actuated by hope "don't desire that addition should be now"; instead, they will find it "every way most pleasing and satisfying and contenting" that "they will choose"— i.e., love— "to have the addition at that time and in that order which God has designed."1 Their hope of increase is not restless. Nevertheless, the increasing of desire may recall JE's assertion that from an infinite height God views the creature "as rising higher and higher through that infinite duration, and that not with constantly diminishing (but perhaps an increasing) celerity."2

In Latin Christianity, it was Augustine's account of eternal life that prevailed. His interpretation of the many mansions (John 14:2) allowed for degrees of perfection in the afterlife. Those who remained virgin for Christ's sake and the gospel had superior dwelling places prepared for them (though this was not the only gradation to be replicated in heaven: a sober housewife was, in significant respects, better than a drunken nun!). Stripped of the preference given dedicated virginity and concentrated entirely on truly virtuous love, Edwards' array of images of heaven (in Charity, Sermon Fifteen, and on the beatifical vision in the sermon on Romans 2:10) is, in my judgment, a rendering like that of St. Augustine and of Western Christendom generally. In sinless perfection there may be degrees, though Augustine's degrees did not necessarily entail growth. But that was all along contained in the beatifical vision according to Edwards, confirmed as his belief throughout his "Miscellanies," and not solely by the three references we have just examined.

To understand the theological background of Edwards' remarkable vision of progress in and into the beatifical vision, however, it is not necessary to go so far back as Gregory or to Eastern Orthodoxy. Original that he was, Edwards may, indeed, have invented his own theological kinship with Orthodox theology, created out of the same flowing together of biblical and Platonic themes. Light on his apprehension of the end toward which Christ moves his people also comes, and more immediately, from the teachings on the "communication of qualities" in Reformation Christology and from perichoresis in trinitarian theology throughout the centuries of Latin Christianity— since the great creeds of the church were formulated in Greek from the original encounter of the Bible with Greek world views.

First, then, the communication of divine and human qualities in Christ. Edwards' theology of the incarnation— of the "two natures" of Christ— is crucial to understanding him on the centrality of Christ in what I shall here call "beatifical progress" in heaven. It is not enough to speak of the eternal role of the God-man in forever manifesting the "knowledge of God's," the "love of God's," the "joy of God's"3 to his redeemed creatures in heaven. Nor is it enough to keep steadfastly in mind the words of the High Priestly Prayer for comprehending End of Creation: "that they all may be one; as thou, Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they also may be one in us" (John 17:21). These two— the eschatological vision of God and the union that was in Christ— are themselves bonded together. The end must be seen from the beginning, and the beginning from the end, if readers of Edwards are to understand fully the meaning for him of "partaking of the divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4).

The foundation for understanding the entirety of End of Creation is laid not only in the going forth of the Son of God into the far country of a human nature but also in the return of the Son of Man to the Father's house, taking that human nature (and creatures of the same nature) with him into the innertrinitarian life of God. Christ is Alpha, he is Omega; he is both. There is "emanation," and there is "remanation," as Edwards wrote.4 Properly understood, this means that Edwards' incarnational Christology is essential to his understanding of Christ as eternally the Mediator of the increase of knowledge, love, and joy in God.

The swiftest and best way to exhibit Edwards' theology of the incarnation is by reference to his spoken Christology, not to any theological speculations of his on the two natures. His sermon "The Excellency of Christ"5 (published the same year as Charity was preached) skillfully deploys the images of the lion and the lamb (Revelation 5:5–6) to develop the theme that the excellency of Christ, his worthiness to be adored, consists not in his possessing divine excellencies (lion-likeness) or in his possessing surpassing human excellencies (lamb-likeness). His supreme worthiness consists rather in the admirable "conjunction" of diverse elements, in the conjunction and indeed the mutual enhancement of divine and human attributes in one person.6 Even theologically informed readers

-- 731 --

may mistakenly understand this sermon to express no more than a standard orthodox Christology (two "natures" never to be either confused or separated) unless and until they reflect upon the preacher's powerful use of "communication of the attributes" (divine and human) as a further rule for interpreting the two natures. Therein lies the sermon's power as spoken Christology.7

For an illustration, consider the sermon's use of metaphors and ascriptions of names8 Its use of instruction tells us why the names given in

-- 732 --

Scripture are so wonderfully intertwined, why in Isaiah 9:6, for example, "the same person should be a Son, born and given," and yet be "everlasting Father, without beginning or end." Its improvement, or use for inducement "to love the Lord Jesus Christ, and choose him for your friend and portion," rehearses that admirable meeting of diverse elements in him by, among other things, the following appeal: "[How] do his condescension and compassion endear his majesty, power and dominion, and render those attributes pleasant, that would otherwise be only terrible!" There is a marvelous "mixtion" of excellences, human and divine:

Christ has no more excellency in his person, since his incarnation, than he had before; for divine excellency is infinite, and cannot be added to. Yet his excellencies are additional manifestations of his glory and excellency to us... Though his human excellencies are but communications and reflections of his divine; and though this light, as reflected, falls infinitely short of the divine fountain of light in its immediate glory; yet the reflection shines not without its proper advantages, as presented to our view and affection. The glory of Christ in the qualifications of his human nature, appears to us in excellencies that are our own kind... But as both divine and human excellencies meet together in Christ, they set off and recommend each other to us. It tends to endear the divine majesty and holiness of Christ to us, that these are attributes of one in our nature, one of us, who is become our brother, and is the meekest and humblest of men... And on the other hand, how much more glorious and surprising do the meekness, the humility, obedience, resignation, and other human excellencies of Christ appear when we consider that they are in so great a person, as the eternal Son of God, the Lord of heaven and earth! The same exhibition of the "communication of attributes" in the names for the divine and the human nature is repeated in the third from final entry in the "Miscellanies." Miscell. no. 1358. DIVINITY OF CHRIST is a lengthy and eloquent argument that everything everywhere in Scripture ascribed to "the one only supreme God," to Jehovah, is also ascribed, without exception and without reservation or differentiation, to Jesus Christ the God-man. Be it creation, be it upholding or preserving the natural or the intelligent creation, be it governing, be it the old or the new creation, be it justification, regeneration, redemption (the same with the work of the new creation), be it overcoming Satan, or destroying the world, or judgment— all these works are most peculiarly spoken of as the work of the supreme God and in the most special manner as also the work of Jesus Christ. His names bespeak the exercise of the qualities of eternity, immortality, and power; "our Lord Jesus Christ" is called King of kings and Lord of lords (1 Timothy 6:4); also, the titles ascribed to the virgin's son in Isaiah 9:6 include "the Mighty God, the everlasting Father."

Notably, this entry is the one that argues philosophically that "the giving the world a being at the first no more differs from preserving it through all successive moments, than the giving the last moment differs from the giving a supported being this moment."9 One could go so far as to say that this sophisticated reasoning was designed to prove the divinity of the baby Jesus exercising divine power, upholding the world from the manger. For following that identification of "upholding" the world in creation with "giving a being this moment," Edwards wrote:

But if these things are so, what shall we think of the upholding and government of the world, while Christ was in his humble state? and while an infant, when he had less knowledge than afterwards, when it is said he increased in wisdom and stature, and had far less strength than he had afterwards? when we are told that he was wearied with his journey, wearied and his strength in a measure spent, only while governing the motions of his own body? Who upheld and governed the world at that time?

Doubtless it will be said, Edwards continues, that "the Father took the world out of the hands of the Son at that time, to uphold and govern it, and return it into his hands again at his exaltation." Edwards finds that supposition incredible: that there be such different authors of the world's

-- 734 --

"being and all its properties, natural principles and motions and alterations and events, both in bodies and all created minds, for one, three or four and thirty years from what it had ever before or since? Have we any hint of such a thing?" Thus Edwards rejected the age-old kenotic theology that came to flourish later, in the nineteenth century. That theology held that the eternal Word had to divest himself of his divine attributes in order to assume human weariness, grow in wisdom and stature, or utter the cry of dereliction from the cross. For our purposes, Edwards' Christological affirmation is clear: the communication (and not only the union) of divine and human attributes in the person of Christ solved all such difficulties in the mind's endeavor to grasp and hold the truth of the incarnation. Indeed, this was the "inducement" to come to Christ and become one with him. The practical upshot was that when one "closes" with Christ, one "closes" with the Most High God, Ruler of the universe, that the incarnation is not reversible, and that Jesus Christ is forever the source of knowledge of God, love and joy in God, yet to be bestowed.

But why in particular is it important to bring into consideration "The Excellency of Christ" to draw together Edwards' account of progress throughout eternal life? Partly because of its publication in the same year (1738) the charity sermons were preached. Mainly, however, because this understanding of the dynamic communication of divine and human attributes in the person Jesus Christ helps decisively to interpret the end for which God created the world, from Alpha to Omega, and not only the final peroration of that dissertation on progress in eternal life, or the beatifical vision alone. At the same time, the sermon helps us understand, once again, how the Triune Identity (when interpreted by the rule known as perichoresis/circumincessio)1 is the paradigm for "uniting

consent" and virtuous other-love (agape) in Edwards' theological ethics. Moreover, when "the society of the blessed Trinity" is brought into view as the End, we shall see that JE's geometric image of asymptotically converging lines is defective also by reason of the externality of the approach it suggests.

The linchpin holding together and in place those two wheels of God's providence— which move, as in heaven, so on earth (eschatology), and as on earth, so in heaven (incarnational Christology)— is the following remarkable fact. In 1738, in conclusion on the excellencies of Christ, Edwards said the same thing— almost word for word— in analysis of the manner of men's "participation in the divine nature" as he had said two years earlier in the

excursus on the beatifical vision when preaching on Romans 2:10. The beatifical vision is a "having communion with Christ therein"; this means "partaking with Christ of his enjoyment of God," "partaking with him in his sight of God," as the Son knows the Father, and having "in their measure the same joy in the love of the Father." The relation moves (circumincessio, I suggest) in the other direction also: "[T]hey being in Christ shall partake of the love of God the Father to Christ."2 The participation is a partaking of the three persons' very own participation in one another within the divine life.

There is nothing unusual, of course, in a preacher's repetition of the same themes and words in sermons separated by about two years' time. The point is to show two significant elaborations of the notion of "participation" that must be controlling in any understanding of the posthumous dissertations. The point also clinches the eternal connection between Christology and eschatology in Edwards. There is a fundamental relation never to be severed between the "communication of attributes" in Jesus Christ and the perichoresis (circumincessio) of the Triune Identity. Only he who was in the beginning with God, and was God (John 1:1), and who is God-man, can as such take his people with him "into the society of the blessed Trinity." "Progressive beatification"— in which, we know, Edwards always believed— seems implicit, while not explicitly stated, in the inestimable riches of participation, as formulated in JE's excursus on the beatifical vision, and again in the ending of his sermon on the excellencies of Christ:

When believers get to heaven, Christ will conform them to himself; as he is set down in his Father's throne, so they will sit down with him on his throne, and shall in their measure be made like him... [The saints' conversation with Christ in heaven shall be even more intimate than on earth] for in heaven that vital union shall be perfect, which is exceeding imperfect here... For hereby the saints' relation to God becomes much nearer; they are children of God in a higher manner than otherwise could be. For being members of God's own Son, they are in a sort partakers of his relation to the Father: they are not only sons of God by regeneration, but by a kind of communion in the Sonship of the eternal Son... So we being members of the Son, are partakers in our measure of the Father's love to the Son, and complacence in him [John 16:27 and John 17:13, John 17:23, review me]... So again, we being united to a divine person, as his members, can have a more intimate union and intercourse with God the Father, who is only in the divine nature, than otherwise could be. Christ who is a divine person, by taking on him our nature... is brought nigh to us; whereby we have advantage for the full enjoyment of him. And, on the other hand, we, by being in Christ a divine person, do as it were ascend up to God, through the infinite distance and have hereby advantage for the full enjoyment of him also. [Ed. italics]

Then, with quotation from John 17:21–23, the sermon ends with a summary both moral and metaphysical-theological: "Christ has brought it to pass, that those whom the Father has given him should be brought into the household of God; that he and his Father, and his people, should be as one society, one family; that the church should be as it were admitted into the society of the blessed Trinity."3

We have no evidence that Edwards ever preached on the text 1 Corinthians 15:28, "And when all things shall be subdued under him, then shall the Son also himself be subject unto him that put all things under him, that God may be all in all."4 In any case, his interpretation of this passage could not have been that the saints pass beyond the Son in their beatifical vision. His thirty-sermon series on the history of redemption, preached during the year after the charity series, comes to its climax in 1 Corinthians 15:24— in three paragraphs just before Edwards' Application of the Whole. First, there is retrospective reference to the covenant protologically: "Now shall all the promises made to Christ by God the Father before the foundation of the world, the promises of the covenant of redemption, be fully accomplished. And Christ shall now perfectly have obtained the joy that was set before him, for which he undertook those great sufferings he underwent in his humiliation." Then, in the final paragraph before the Application, he refers to the eschatological completion of that covenant, which leads to ver. 1 Corinthians 15:24 (not ver. 1 Corinthians 15:28): "And as Christ when he first entered upon the Work of Redemption, after the fall of man, had the kingdom committed to him of the Father, and took on himself the administration of the affairs of the universe, to manage all so as to subserve the purposes of this affair; so now when that work being finished, he will

His next words are: "Not that Christ shall cease to reign or have a kingdom after this," citing Luke 1:33 and Daniel 7:14 on Christ's everlasting dominion. Jesus Christ, as known on earth, now with the saints in glorified body, will never cease to "cleave" to his church in the union that was then accomplished. 7. The most frequently used analytical tool for elaborating the union of the two natures of Christ was the communicatio ideomatum ("communication of attributes"). Evidently the shaping influence on the Christology JE preached in "The Excellency of Christ" was this rule of interpretation which, in differing versions, was shared by Lutheran and Reformed theologians. Lutherans, in their zeal to affirm the reality of the union, said that one nature directly communicated with or interpenetrated another. Calvinists, in their zeal to uphold the free grace of God as the ground of the union, stressed the movement from the divine, assuming the human nature into itself; and they held that the communication of the two natures was in and to the person of Christ. In him was the union, and in him the communication. Calvinists suspected Lutherans of obscuring or confusing the integrity of each nature, and of logical absurdity in mingling natures; Lutherans doubted the Calvinists' acceptance of an actual communication of real properties. The crucial point of the controversy was the Lutheran teaching that the body of Christ, now far away in heaven after the ascension, possesses the divine property of ubiquity, so as to be present substantially, according to scriptural promise, in, with, and under the elements of the eucharist. Beneath this dispute, however, was a common belief in a communication of attributes.
As we have seen, JE studied and recommended "the great Turretine" (Affections, p. 289, n. 4; pp. 742–43, below). Francis Turretin's Institutio Theologiae Elencticae (3 vols., Geneva, 1679–85) is, therefore, of interest on the present point. Topic XIII, Q. VIII asks, "Were certain Properties of the Divine Nature formally communicated to the human Nature of Christ by the Personal Union?" To which he answers, "We deny against the Lutherans." But then he says that there is "a communication of attributes and of the properties of each nature to the Person"; and explains, "The Communication of Attributes, concerning which we now dispute, is an effect of the Union, by which the properties of both natures become common to the Person." He then defends (against the Lutherans), "This communication is not only verbal, but is rightly called real, not indeed with respect to the Natures, as if the properties of the one nature were really communicated to the other; But with respect to the Person, which consists of two natures really united and claims the properties of both for itself. For although the union of the two natures with each other is real, it is not necessary, that the properties of the natures all should be communicated to each other in turn; it suffices that they be communicated to the whole subsisting substance on account of that union." (François Turrettini [Francis Turretin], Institutio Theologiae Elencticae. [New York and Pittsburgh, Robert Carter, 1847]. 1. Perichoresis was sometimes used as the "rule" for interpreting the interrelation of the two natures of Christ, but its true home was in trinitarian theology. Two Latin translations were used. In the Middle Ages, the notion was of a spatial togetherness of the three persons of the Trinity, a dwelling in one another; all three "inexist" one another (as Barth says, Church Dogmatics, I/1, pp. 424–25, 456), they exist or have their being only in common. So the reference in medieval times was always to the eternal "session" of the Trinity. Circuminsessio expressed this notion or rule of trinitarian reflection. Circumincessio was an earlier understanding of perichoresis (going back at least as far as John of Damascus (c. 675– c. 749], probably earlier). Its meaning was that the three persons permeate one another mutually by, as it were, moving around, one into another; or, as Barth says, "passing into one another" in "a kind of circular course" (ibid.). In another metaphor that has been used (appropriate in reading JE, I suggest), the joyous innertrinitarian life of God is a kind of dance. The Latin root is a legal term meaning to cede, yield, grant, give over; yielding and leading in turn are the movements of a dance. The suggestion of temporal sequence was replaced in the Latin church by the spatial metaphor of "session."
If, instead of a Reformed understanding of the communication of properties, one wanted to frame JE on the excellencies of Christ in terms of perichoresis, the more dynamic of these two rules of interpretation suggests itself, just as circumincessio seems the better summary for him of the innertrinitarian society. In any case, the root of perichoresis means "orchestrated movement." JE avoids the temptation to affirm, of the person of Christ, that there was "more of God" than was manifest in his divine nature (and avoids the objection to "Nature to Nature" communication) by understanding "nature" chiefly as activity. Participation in the divine life or the divine activity is JE's answer to the objection raised against his use of 2 Peter 1:4 in Affections. See Related Correspondence, above. ↩

Nowhere in his writings does Barth develop systematically the idea of heaven as a progressive state. Yet we must remember that Vol. V of the Church Dogmatics, on the doctrine of redemption, where one would have expected the full statement of Barth's eschatology, was never written. We do not know how he might have developed the exchange of properties in the history of the incarnate Christ (the themes of IV/1 and IV/2, the Lord as Servant and the Servant as Lord) if placed in the context of God's end in redemption. Nevertheless, there is much in Barth's later theology that parallels Edwards' themes: (a) the inexhaustible richness of the living, triune God, whose being-in-love is freely extended to his creatures; (b) the inexpungeable significance of Christ for human salvation as the eternal mediator in and through whom we ever know and love God; (c) salvation as participation in the triune life of God, which is far more than a restitutio ad integrum of our humanity prior to the Fall; (d) the prevenience of God's grace now and forever in all his relations with humanity, opening and empowering human response in joy, gratitude, and obedience; (e) the communal as well as personal reality of the new creation here and hereafter; (f) the new life in communion with God and fellow creatures not as the extinguishing but as the establishment and fulfillment of our true humanity as finite creatures.
