APPENDIX ONE: JOSEPH BELLAMY'S COPY OF THE CHARITY SERMONS

IN his preface, Tryon Edwards stated that after Edwards' death his sermons on 1 Corinthians 13 were "selected for publication by Dr. Hopkins and Dr. Bellamy; and by the latter were in part copied out and prepared for the press, when for some reason he was interrupted in their preparation, so that now for the first time they are given to the public."1 The recent discovery and identification of fragments of Bellamy's part-copy confirms TE's assertion that Bellamy began transcribing the charity sermons for publication. TE did not say how he knew this, nor how extensive a partial copy Bellamy made.

How shall we explain Tryon Edwards' knowledge that Bellamy began a copy of the charity sermons? In attempting an answer to this question, we begin with Tryon's relation to Bellamy's papers. He had access to these papers in the preparation of a "Memoir" of Bellamy published in 1858,2 six years after his own edition of Charity. This might explain how Tryon came upon Bellamy's part-copy, if it be reasonable to assume that he was engaged for several years in preparing the memoir, or that Charity's publication was delayed. But the span of years between TE's preface to Charity and his subsequent "Memoir" renders this supposition concerning his knowledge of Bellamy's eighteenth-century part-copy quite implausible.

Plausible explanations come to mind if we turn our attention to the history of Edwards' papers. Following Bellamy and Hopkins, Jonathan Edwards, Jr., was responsible for the collection for a long time, and edited from it. Tryon was the grandson of JE, Jr., and the son of Jonathan Walter Edwards, who held the collection for a brief period after the death of his father. JE, Jr. died several years before TE (1809–94) was born. Nevertheless, it is not unreasonable to suppose that an oral tradition

-- 644 --

about the papers' history was handed down to TE along with the collection.

Bellamy and Hopkins might have left in the collection the letters that passed between them concerning their project of editing some of the MSS. This correspondence could have been retained among the papers and been handed on in succession to Tryon. This conjecture (Thomas A. Schafer's) is based on the fact that there were many letters addressed to Jonathan Edwards in the collection when it came into Tryon's possession, practically all of which have disappeared. As executor, Tryon himself might have removed letters he judged extraneous. (If so, how letters from Grosart addressed to TE, and several pages of the Treatise on Grace, came into the Andover coll. is a mystery.)

Furthermore, nothing excludes the possibility that the part-copy itself was in the collection when TE became executor. If the source of TE's knowledge of Bellamy's transcription was neither oral tradition nor correspondence in the collection, but the copy itself, we could further speculate that TE himself transferred the part-copy to the Bellamy papers when he was working on the "Memoir" and before he maneuvered Edwards' papers into the libraries at Yale and Andover.

But let us take up the story at its beginning, a century earlier. Recall the no-if-ands-or-buts letter Hopkins wrote to Bellamy concerning some sermons the latter had undertaken to transcribe.3 We need now to raise questions about those sermons, even though this will lead only to a series of relatively plausible suppositions. Hopkins' words— "transcribe some of the sermons you mention without any ifs or ands"— are consistent with a high esteem on Hopkins' part for the sermons Bellamy had begun to transcribe, and with Tryon's assertion that Bellamy partly copied the charity sermons for possible publication. Such were my conjectures about the identity of these sermons: first Bellamy and then Hopkins noted the special worth of the 1738 sermon series; Bellamy began to copy them.

Inquiry into the respective apprenticeships of Bellamy and Hopkins in Northampton tended to confirm my surmise that, among Edwards' many sermons, papers, and notebooks, Bellamy's editorial attention had been drawn to the charity sermons. The following detailed reconstruction of the early years of these two editors has as its upshot that the young Bellamy may have heard the first three, and only three, charity sermons delivered.

Samuel Hopkins' first stay in Northampton lasted from December

-- 645 --

1741 through March 1742. He returned to his home in Waterbury, Connecticut, and was licensed to preach in April 1742. Instead of taking a pastorate immediately, however, he decided to return to Edwards' home— known by then as one of the "schools of the prophets." Hopkins' second period in Northampton was from the spring of 1742 until May or June of 1743. During this period he served as interim pastor of the congregation in Simsbury, Connecticut, but his time was apparently mostly spent under Edwards' tutelage.4 Thus Hopkins' time in Northampton overlapped with the Great Awakening (1740–42).

Bellamy was the older of the two, and he also came earlier as an apprentice to Edwards. It is commonly said that he studied with Edwards in 1738 (nearer in time to the revival in Northampton, 1734–35). If Bellamy's year in Northampton was 1738, he in all probability heard JE preach the charity sermons in their entirety. No wonder then that, following JE's death, Bellamy's eyes lighted upon that particular set of sermon booklets, and he remembered their quality. But S. E. Dwight is the source of that date, and he only implies that the year was 1738.5

Glenn Paul Anderson, however, says the year must have been mid-1736 to mid-1737.6 Anderson's chronology of events in Bellamy's life is as follows: 1736–37, with Edwards in Northampton; licensed to preach, May 1737;7 preached occasionally in his home church, Cheshire, Connecticut, 1737–38;8 settled in Worcester, Massachusetts, where he preached regularly from June to October 1738; and was settled in Bethlehem, Connecticut, in November 1738, where he was ordained in 1740.9

On its face Anderson's chronology seems to remove Bellamy from

-- 646 --

Northampton in the year the charity sermons were preached. But the fact that Bellamy was licensed to preach in 1737 does not mean that was the end of his apprenticeship with Edwards. Like Hopkins, Bellamy may have planned to remain under Edwards' tutelage after licensure. Nor does the fact that there is record of his preaching from time to time into the early months of 1738 at his home church remove him from Northampton during that period. Bellamy's permanent removal from Northampton began with his settlement in Worcester, where he preached every Sunday from June to October 1738.

This means that, by Anderson's account, Bellamy could not have heard the entire charity series. But his chronology allows us to suppose that Bellamy heard Edwards deliver the first three charity sermons. The Andover copy dates sermon one sometime in April and sermon two in May of 1738. Although sermon three is undated, we have reason to conjecture only that Bellamy did not hear the remainder of the series from sermon four (dated June 1738) onward. Significantly, the recently discovered fragments of Bellamy's part-copy are pieces of these first three sermons.

Before 1982, no extant Bellamy copy was known. For that matter, we had no definitive proof that TE was correct in saying that Bellamy had begun to copy the charity sermons. Then Mark Valeri located, and he and I identified, three fragments of sermons one through three in Bellamy's hand. Valeri, a doctoral student in the Department of Religion at Princeton, was doing the research for his dissertation on Joseph Bellamy.1 In spring 1982, he was reading in the largest collection of Bellamy manuscripts, at Hartford Seminary, Hartford, Connecticut. There, in "Doc. Box. No. 189, Folder No. 2942 Joseph Bellamy papers (sermons)," Valeri found three fragments of sermons in Bellamy's hand, classified as his sermons, on texts from 1 Corinthians 13. The three items, with Hartford's numerical designation and description, were:

81466 a sermon n. d. 4 p. (on I Cor. XIII: 1 Corinthians 8:1, 1 Corinthians 8:2, 1 Corinthians 8:3) (incomplete)

81467 a sermon n. d. 4 p. (on "Love") (incomplete)

81468 Sermons; (1) last part of Sermon No. II (on God's grace; incomplete) (2) Sermon No. III (on I Cor. XIIL3; incomplete); 4p.

Upon Valeri's returning to Princeton with photocopies of these and other Bellamy papers, he and I recognized almost at once that these were fragments of Bellamy's long-lost copy of Edwards' charity sermons.

-- 647 --

Dr. Richard B. Eberhart, then Administrator, Educational Resources Center, Hartford Seminary, tells me that the seminary received its Bellamy papers as a gift from Bellamy's great-granddaughter, Miss Elizabeth Bellamy Loomis of Greenfield, Mass., in 1893. He presumes that the papers had been in the family before that.2

I suggested above that Bellamy's copy or part-copy might have been placed among the Edwards papers, where Tryon came upon it when he became executor. If so, we might also picture him using Bellamy's part-copy as a "pony" or "trot" in the transcription of the earlier sermons in the series. This surmise in no way discredits, nor is it meant to discredit, TE's claim that he edited from the original sermon booklets. But the conjecture would serve to explain (if more is needed) a number of remarkable agreements between TE and Bellamy that are in disagreement with Andover (noted in the examples below). However, in general, both the discrepancies and the agreements among our three sources are adequately comprehended from the fact that each transcribed and edited as he copied. The Andover copyist doubtless was given rules for editing while transcribing. Of the three, TE was by far the most "liberal" redactor.3

Before commenting on the texts, I should raise the question why Bellamy did not finish his copy of the sermons. TE says he was interrupted. A more reasonable conjecture is that he stopped. This supposition can be unfolded as follows. As a young apprentice under Edwards' tutelage, Bellamy was properly impressed with the charity series (or the first three sermons) when he heard them delivered in 1738. Hence, after Edwards' death, he chose these sermons for possible publication, while also transcribing True Virtue. But— now more than twenty-five years older— Bellamy's interest in the project waned rather more rapidly than did that of the younger Hopkins. Why might this have been so? For a combination of two reasons, I suggest. (1) Having recently entered the

-- 648 --

theological arena with his own systematic theology,4 Bellamy was deeply engaged in his own life's work, and could not be diverted from this for any considerable length of time by his debt of filial piety to Edwards. But also (2) the more he comprehended the sermons as a mature scholar, the less they were to his liking.

In the theological battles of their own day, the New Divinity men (Bellamy, Hopkins, Emmons) identified themselves as heirs of the master theologian of the eighteenth century. Their claims served mainly to impair the continuing influence of Edwards in the intellectual history of America in the national period and in the nineteenth century. Three foci of Bellamy's system of divinity were surely not Edwardsean: his legalism, his concept of disinterested love, and his computation of virtue in terms of happiness in the whole consequences.5 The law of God was not Edwards' emphasis in theology. And, while the expression "disinterested love" can be found in Edwards' writings,6 there could not be a more interestedlove than his (whether by this is meant the will/love of one's own

-- 649 --

happiness on the part of any intelligent willing being, or such a being's specific determination to love to God and to fellow creatures). It is quite reasonable to suppose that sooner or later the charity sermons— and especially these sermons among Edwards' writings— would disturb Bellamy; and threaten his fond belief, and that of other New Divinity men, that they were only thinking Edwards' most intimate thoughts after him.7

The first four-page Bellamy fragment proved to be the beginning of Edwards' sermon one; the second four-page fragment, the ending of sermon one; and the third four-page fragment, the ending of sermon two and the beginning of sermon three. Bellamy numbered his pages consecutively throughout. Thus, page 1 of the first fragment was his facing page; then pp. 2 (verso), 3 (recto), and 4 (verso) began the expected use of odd numbers for recto, even numbers for verso. Bellamy's pagination of the second fragment, the four-page ending of sermon one, however, introduces a mistake: 17, 17, 18, 19. The first 17 is correct, since it is a recto page. I conclude, therefore, that the missing pages are 5 through 16; and that there were 20 pages in Bellamy's copy to the end of sermon one.

We have some reason to assume that Bellamy continued to use the even-odd number sequences (18, 19, etc.) begun by his repetition of 17 on verso, for when he came to the pages we now have in the third fragment, the first of the four pages is 44, and this is on a recto. That

-- 650 --

would be the outcome if, on the missing pages, Bellamy continued the unusual even-odd sequence introduced earlier. Then Bellamy made another mistake. He repeated 44 on the next verso, which corrected the first mistake and brought him back to odd-even for the two final pages (i.e., 45, 46), which are recto-verso. I figure the pages of Bellamy's copy to total 48 plus at least one more page, because the fragment ends with a catchword on the right at the bottom. The significance of these details is, I suppose, that Bellamy numbered consecutively throughout, and we know from this that his copy was a full one as far as he went— which was at least one more, now missing page!

In footnotes to the text of the charity sermons, I have indicated the beginning and ending of Bellamy's fragmentary text, and also something of the importance of the discovery of his copy in the preparation of the Yale edition. The latter requires further explanation. The problem is how best to construe the now lost original sermons. Just below, the text of Bellamy's part-copy is published in its entirety for the first time.8 This provides a consecutive reading. But Bellamy, Andover, and Tryon all three copied and edited from the original booklets. Therefore I have selected four passages of particular significance to serve as examples of editorial problems in construing the originals behind our sources, or as examples of what can be learned from Bellamy's copy— so far as it extends. These are cross-sections of my text, Bellamy, Andover, and Tryon, printed in parallel columns to aid readers in making their own comparisons line by line and in forming their own judgments. Each example is preceded by an Editor's Comment in which I point out signal disagreements, analyze the problems faced in editing the passage, and indicate where I rely on Bellamy in constructing a text. Sometimes he helped in major respects; sometimes in deciphering a word that in Edwards' hand Andover and Tryon found difficult to read. Students of Edwards can go beyond these explanations into the style of our available sources. The synopses also afford, I believe, interesting exercises in editing.

IN these words may be observed, (1.) Something spoken of, as of special importance, and as peculiarly essential, in Christians, which the Apostle calls CHARITY. CHARITY is abundantly insisted on in the New Testament by Christ and his Apostles, and indeed there is no virtue so much insisted on by them. But then the word Charity, as 'tis used in the New Testament, is of much larger signification than as it is vulgarly used now in Common Discourse. What Persons very often mean by Charity, in their Common Conversation, is a Disposition to hope and think the best of others, and to put a good construction on their words and behaviour, and sometimes it is used for a disposition to give to the Poor. But these things are only particular branches or fruits of that great virtue of Charity, which is so much insisted on in the New Testament. The word properly signifies Love, or that disposition or affection whereby one is dear to another. The word (agape) in the original, which is translated Charity, might as well have been rendered Love, for that is the proper English of it. So that by Charity in the New Testament is meant the very same as Christian Love. and altho' it is frequently used for Love to men, yet sometimes it is used to signify not only Love to men, but also love to God. So it is manifestly used by this Apostle in this Epistle, for so he explains himself. Chap. VIII. 1 Corinthians 8:1. Knowledge puffeth up, but Charity Edifieth. in these words knowledge & Charity are compared together; and Charity is preferred before Knowledge; because knowledge puffeth up, but Charity Edifieth. and then in the two next verses he explains first how knowledge vainly puffs up. if any man think that he knoweth any thing, he knoweth nothing yet as he ought to know. and then Secondly, how Charity Edifieth. but if any man love God, the same is known of him. So that what he calls Charity in the first verse, he calls loving God in the third: for he is evidently speaking of the same thing, and doubtless he means the same by Charity in this XIII.th Chap, as he there does in the VIII.th for he is here comparing the same two things

From the third sentence of Bellamy's copy, "The more we consider it, the more wonderful and inexpressible will it appear" onward, his text has a distinctive style in comparison with our other two versions. Andover and Tryon read much more smoothly to our ears than Bellamy. There is a jerkiness, or sudden movement from one statement to another, in Bellamy's copy. Couple this with the three successive gaps in the Andover copy following the references to the Virgin Mary, etc., and readers can set themselves a test in construing what was the nature of this page in Edwards' sermon booklet.

In his booklets Edwards often did not fully write out the sermon as delivered. Instead, at certain places he wrote a phrase here and a phrase there, an incomplete sentence, or a short complete sentence, at irregular intervals on the page, some beginning to the left, some to the right hand of the page. This, we may suppose, assisted the preacher in extemporizing. Our three copyists and editors seem to be struggling with such a page.

At another point, Andover simply copied down similar irregular notes concerning the sin against the Holy Ghost. I reconstructed that page from JE's biblical notes (sermon two, pp. 164–66 above). Here the copyist— we may conjecture— was confronted with a similar page. He tried to make the text readable consecutively, but failed at the three gaps, and gave up altogether when he came to "how little this lies with." The reason for this decision— which I hope will be persuasive— was my failure to find a way to make sense of Andover while at the same time respecting the integrity of his text at these particular blank spaces in his copy. To respect the integrity of the copy-text, I needed not to change his words to elide with mine (or with words taken from the Bellamy or Tryon parallels).

Consider the first space. Bellamy's is a splendid Edwardsean sentence: "considered in themselves, separately from the saving grace of God, are nothing compared with this." Bellamy's "this" (and, I surmise, Edwards') must have seemed dangling to both Andover and Tryon. Their versions extend the sentence to mention "saving grace in the heart." But Bellamy had stated that earlier: "considered in themselves, separately from the saving grace of God"— to which his "this" refers. Instead of switching to Bellamy for this sentence only and back again to Andover, my decision was to incorporate the entire relatively brief and lively Bellamy passage.

Moreover, Andover has "hope" also later in his sentence. But that would be an abrupt dismissal of one of my sources— the one there was ample reason to choose as copy-text for this edition, since we have only pieces of Bellamy's copy and none of Edwards' booklets.

The title of sermon three is of greatest interest. Titles are taken from doctrines, and we do not know how Bellamy worded the Doctrine of this sermon, if he copied that far. Our only clue is the title he chose (to be compared with Tryon's): "Nothing can make up for the want of Sincerity"— the brevity of which might have been determined by want of space. The words at the top of the final page, 46, are "Nothing can make up for" (a running head that doubtless was on the missing pages that followed). Still, Bellamy's title is remarkable for its simplicity, and for offering no elaboration on "Sincerity," which could be all he meant to write. The Andover copyist entered no title (as Edwards did not). His statement of the Doctrine, however, was: "All, which men can do, and all, which they can suffer, will not make up for the want of sincerity in the heart." If that was Edwards' wording (and also Bellamy's), then Bellamy composed a more forceful short title by changing "All" to "Nothing." In editing this sermon I adopt Bellamy's title, with the addition of Andover's "in the heart," in the belief that this is not an elaboration upon "sincerity." At least it is not an elaboration to be compared with Tryon's Doctrine: "for want of sincere Christian love in the heart." Edwards would not have needed those additional qualifications of sincerity (see Intro., p. 61, n. 8). Otherwise Tryon's statement of the Doctrine is rather like Andover's, viz., "That all that men can do, and all that they can suffer, can never make up for..." Tryon's variation upon that for his title— stressing Edwards' (and St. Paul's) examples of performances and sufferings in extremis— is somewhat unfortunate.

6. Cf. Charity, p. 264 at n. 2: "there is no other-love so much above a selfish principle as Christian love is, there is no love that is so free and disinterested." This quotation from Sermon Seven on a selfish spirit points especially to freedom, to the doctrine to be "willing cheerfully and freely to do good to others"— one of the two dispositions of charity commended in Sermon Four— not to disinterest. No intelligent willing being can will or love disinterestedly. Such love was not among Edwards' themes, either in its New Divinity or its contemporary sense. The notion of "disinterested love" enabled Hopkins to write, as Edwards did not, a "Dialogue between a Calvinist and a Semi-Calvinist," showing that a true Christian should be willing, if God so wills it, to be damned for the glory of God. See The Works of Samuel Hopkins, D. D. (3 vols., Boston, 1854), 3, 143–57. In his mature years Hopkins went in quite different directions, in both substance and terminology, from those of his mentor. He used the Edwardsean language of "benevolence or good will to being in general." But this "term of art" for love to God in True Virtue he promptly turned into a characteristic of the Deity, an axiom from which to reason as follows: "This is love of pure, disinterested benevolence, to creatures infinitely odious and guilty... Hence it follows with certainty, that disinterested benevolence is primary and essential in the divine moral character... And it follows from this also, that the love to God which is required, is the love of disinterested benevolence" (The System of Doctrines, Contained in Divine Revelation, Explained and Defended... in Two Volumes (Boston, 1811), 1, 291. Cf. pp. 52–61; and 465 ff., "On Disinterested Affection," distinguishing "self-love and a desire or love of happiness" [!] from "disinterested benevolence"). Hopkins presumed to know enough about the divine wisdom to write (as JE would not) a treatise entitled Sin, Thro' Divine interposition, an Advantage to the Universe (Boston, 1759), in Three Sermons from Rom. iii. Romans 3:5–8 (Boston, 1773). His older associate Bellamy computed (in 1758) the happiness of the generations, whether destined for heaven or hell, so as to prove the advantage! The need for such theological investigations logically follows from the premise that "disinterested benevolence" is a quasi-philosophical "characteristic of the Deity." ↩
