APPENDIX IV
INFUSED VIRTUES IN EDWARDSEAN AND CALVINISTIC CONTEXT

ANYONE who reads Edwards on 1 Corinthians 13 (even when stripped of the language of "infusion"),1 who reads him on the "gracious" affections, and who reads him on true virtue must instantly see that he teaches a doctrine of infused righteousness, and not only a doctrine of Christ's righteousness imputed to us. This he variously expressed as divine love and its fruits in virtues implanted in heart and life by the indwelling Spirit, as a new "simple idea" impressed upon the soul,2 as uniting consent or a propensity to unition with the Being of beings and our fellow human beings. Quite evidently, these qualities are virtues "endued" and inhering in the souls of men; they are neither "common" external influences of the Spirit, nor the garments of imputed righteousness alone, nor only a new status of being forgiven of sin.

From early entries in the "Miscellanies" through the period of the 1738 charity sermons to the end of his life (as True Virtue makes manifest), Edwards' account of the Christian moral life is one of progressive sanctification firmly grounded in a doctrine of infused moral virtues. Saving grace is "a quality inherent in... the subject of it," making "a man's heart and nature excellent." So "the Spirit becomes an indwelling vital principle in the soul, and the subject becomes a spiritual being."3 This is what Edwards means by being remade in the "spiritual image of God" as distinct from having been made in the "natural image of God," i.e., belonging by natural birth to the "moral part" of the creation. The Spirit indwelling, "grace infused," is the fountain of "love infused" in the heart; and love infused is the fountain of a variety of infused moral dispositions.

-- 740 --

Edwards stated succinctly the point to be proved in an early Miscellany: "[I]t is evident to a metaphysical demonstration, that one of these things is true of that man that is become good: either there was a time wherein if he had died he would neither have gone to heaven nor hell, that is, neither to great happiness nor great misery; or else, the future state is not according to man's goodness or badness here; or else, there are infused virtues."4 The thesis of a related entry is: "Those that deny infusion by the Holy Spirit, must necessarily deny the Spirit to do anything at all." Whatever the "something" is that the Spirit does in the soul, this we call infusion: "[H]e causeth some motion, or affection, or apprehension to be in the soul, that at the same time would not be there without him."5

Moreover, Edwards argues in this entry that "God's Spirit... doth so much as he doth, or he causes in the soul so much as he causeth, let that be how little soever. So much as is purely the effect of his immediate motion, that is the effect of his immediate motion, let that be what it will; and so much is infused, how little soever that be. This is self-evident." Suppose, Edwards continues, that the Spirit of God only assists man's natural powers, according to the views of those who say the Holy Spirit only "assists the man in acquiring the habit." What does this mean? It means perhaps that the habit is "acquired rather sooner than it would be otherwise," or that it has a strength it otherwise would not have, or the natural power is assisted "to act more livelily and vigorously than otherwise." In such a circumstance, Edwards concludes, "that liveliness and vigorousness must be infused, which is a habit and therefore an infused habit; it is grace, and therefore infused grace." Therefore, among those who don't say the Holy Spirit does nothing at all, the dispute is only how much is infused. One party "says, a great deal; the other says, but little."

In another early entry Edwards refutes the position of those who say that a man acquires true virtue by frequent exercises of it. This, he argues, is a contradiction. "For tell me [how] a man that has no true grace within him shall begin to exercise it: before he begins to exercise it, he must have some of it. How shall [he] act virtuously the first time? How came he by that virtue which he then acted?" A man cannot get a virtuous habit by the acting or exercise of it before he has it. Edwards is willing to grant that if a man acts virtuously, after a while it will become habitual to him. The question concerns who was the prime mover in the act "when the man first began to act from a truly virtuous principle."

This cannot be "by means of his using of himself to it" in that time when he had never before acted from a virtuous principle.6

Edwards' insistent question is: "Supposing a person most profligate and vicious in his life," who afterwards changes course and reforms, "how this man became better than he was before, in his first beginning to reform; how came he by that virtue from which he acted when he first began to reform?" If you say that he did not at first act virtue, that he began to reform from some other spring or principle of action, the question remains: "[H]ow came he by his virtue by which he first did act from a virtuous principle?" To a common answer to that question Edwards replied: "If you say that even the worst have some spark of virtue remaining in them, and by acting and exercising of them he strengthens them; if we allow it to be so, the case is the same." How came the man to begin to reform today and not yesterday— or tomorrow? How came he to act so "livelily" and vigorously when he did? Did he strengthen the strength or liveliness of his virtue in order to begin to reform when he did, and to begin with the degree of impulsion he put into willing that self-reformation? So, among those who don't say the Holy Spirit does nothing, the dispute is over how much is infused. One party says, a great deal; the other says, but little.

This he variously expressed as divine love and its fruits in virtues implanted in heart and life by the indwelling Spirit, as a new "simple idea" impressed upon the soul,2, p. 205. Cf. Freedom of the Will, p. 43, n. 5. as uniting consent or a propensity to unition with the Being of beings and our fellow human beings. Quite evidently, these qualities are virtues "endued" and inhering in the souls of men; they are neither "common" external influences of the Spirit, nor the garments of imputed righteousness alone, nor only a new status of being forgiven of sin.

From early entries in the "Miscellanies" through the period of the 1738 charity sermons to the end of his life (as True Virtue makes manifest), Edwards' account of the Christian moral life is one of progressive sanctification firmly grounded in a doctrine of infused moral virtues. Saving grace is "a quality inherent in... the subject of it," making "a man's heart and nature excellent." So "the Spirit becomes an indwelling vital principle in the soul, and the subject becomes a spiritual being."3, pp. 157–58. Ed. italics. Cf. JE's extended discussion of the meaning of spiritual in Affections, pp. 201–08. This is what Edwards means by being remade in the "spiritual image of God" as distinct from having been made in the "natural image of God," i.e., belonging by natural birth to the "moral part" of the creation. The Spirit indwelling, "grace infused," is the fountain of "love infused" in the heart; and love infused is the fountain of a variety of infused moral dispositions.

Suppose, Edwards continues, that the Spirit of God only assists man's natural powers, according to the views of those who say the Holy Spirit only "assists the man in acquiring the habit." What does this mean? It means perhaps that the habit is "acquired rather sooner than it would be otherwise," or that it has a strength it otherwise would not have, or the natural power is assisted "to act more livelily and vigorously than otherwise." In such a circumstance, Edwards concludes, "that liveliness and vigorousness must be infused, which is a habit and therefore an infused habit; it is grace, and therefore infused grace." Therefore, among those who don't say the Holy Spirit does nothing at all, the dispute is only how much is infused. One party "says, a great deal; the other says, but little."

In another early entry Edwards refutes the position of those who say that a man acquires true virtue by frequent exercises of it. This, he argues, is a contradiction. "For tell me [how] a man that has no true grace within him shall begin to exercise it: before he begins to exercise it, he must have some of it. How shall [he] act virtuously the first time? How came he by that virtue which he then acted?" A man cannot get a virtuous habit by the acting or exercise of it before he has it. Edwards is willing to grant that if a man acts virtuously, after a while it will become habitual to him. The question concerns who was the prime mover in the act "when the man first began to act from a truly virtuous principle."

This cannot be "by means of his using of himself to it" in that time when he had never before acted from a virtuous principle.6. 723–24). JE used this argument many times thereafter, in different contexts. See Freedom of the Will, Intro., pp. 29–34.

Edwards' insistent question is: "Supposing a person most profligate and vicious in his life," who afterwards changes course and reforms, "how this man became better than he was before, in his first beginning to reform; how came he by that virtue from which he acted when he first began to reform?" If you say that he did not at first act virtue, that he began to reform from some other spring or principle of action, the question remains: "[H]ow came he by his virtue by which he first did act from a virtuous principle?" To a common answer to that question Edwards replied: "If you say that even the worst have some spark of virtue remaining in them, and by acting and exercising of them he strengthens them; if we allow it to be so, the case is the same." How came the man to begin to reform today and not yesterday— or tomorrow? How came he to act so "livelily" and vigorously when he did? Did he strengthen the strength or liveliness of his virtue in order to begin to reform when he did, and to begin with the degree of impulsion he put into willing that self-reformation? Edwards would certainly not have agreed with Mastricht that the infused "spiritual" life— the several dispositions and virtues quickening the regenerate man— obtain their names according to "the several parts and faculties of him." I begin with a small but significant point. In what Calvin says about the "ancient doctors" to distinguish his method from theirs, he anticipated and granted the appropriateness of Edwards' careful delineation of a panoply of fruits of the Spirit— habitual dispositions or virtues in heart and life— in the charity sermons. Calvin wrote:

In composing exhortations on but a single virtue, the ancient doctors, as we see, became very prolix. Yet in this they waste no words. For when a man sets out to commend any virtue in his discourse, abundance of material drives him to a style of such fullness that he seems not to treat it properly unless he speaks at length. But I do not intend to develop, here, the instruction in living that I am now about to offer to the point of describing individual virtues at length, and of digressing into exhortations. Such may be sought in others' writings, especially from the homilies of the fathers. To show the godly man how he may be directed to a rightly ordered life, and briefly to set down some

Calvin himself set forth one rule only, that men may not be left to wander about in their zeal for righteousness, namely, "that we must be made holy because our God is holy... that, infused with his holiness, we may follow whither he calls."1 Edwards delivered eighteenth-century "homilies of the fathers," prolix yet wasting no words.

Indeed, Edwards' innovation, within the Reformed tradition of infused righteousness, was not that he delineated an array of the virtues of charity. He drew his precepts from I. Cor. 1 Corinthians 13, and in the distinguishing signs of the gracious affections from St. Paul's list of the fruits of the Spirit (Galatians 5:22–23) and from elsewhere in Scripture, as did the fathers in their homilies. So Calvin remarks, "As philosophers have fixed limits of the right and the honorable, when they derive individual duties and the whole company of virtues, so Scripture is not without its own order in this matter, but holds to a most beautiful dispensation, and one much more certain than all the philosophical ones."2 Nor was Edwards' innovation solely in that he more carefully delineated the infused virtues, conceptualized and arranged them more "methodically"— heedless of Calvin's warning that the Spirit of God did not adhere so exactly or continuously to a methodical plan (because the Spirit taught more without affectation than the philosophers!).3 His innovation within the Reformed tradition, I suggest, was rather his full account of natural principles. His principles are the source of several, at least, of the dispositions and virtues that true virtue or love to God operating alone infuses in heart and life. Both dispositions and virtues deserve, by the common consent of mankind in its use of moral terms, the same names, i.e., justice, kindness, compassion.4 and discussed more fully in the final chapter of that dissertation. In his account of the totality of the moral life Edwards articulated the humanism often ascribed to Calvin in contrast to Luther.

In his discussion of regeneration, repentance has special importance. The "sum of the gospel," Calvin writes, consists in two things: repentance and forgiveness of sins. "That is, newness of life and free reconciliation," he explains. Note that repentance does not mean sorrow; it is paired with, and it does not precede, forgiveness. Both are "conferred" on us, and both repentance (newness of life) and free reconciliation (justification) begin at the same point. But here again Calvin chooses to discuss repentance first. The immediate transition is "from faith to repentance." Then we come to the following passage:

For when this topic is rightly understood it will better appear how man is justified by faith alone, and simple pardon; nevertheless actual holiness of life, so to speak, is not separated from free imputation of righteousness. Now it ought to be a fact beyond controversy that repentance not only constantly follows faith, but is also born of faith [since both pardon and newness of life are offered in the preaching of the gospel].6

That comes to pass when the Spirit of God so imbues our souls, steeped in his holiness, with both new thoughts and feelings, that they can be rightly considered new."9 "Repentance" is defined exactly as Edwards did in the Seventh Distinguishing Sign of the gracious affections.1 The resemblance between Calvin and Edwards on repentance and faith is even more remarkable in the concatenation sermon. "[S]aving repentance and faith are implied in one another," as Edwards wrote:

They are both one conversion of the soul from sin to God through Christ. This act of the soul in turning from sin to God through Christ, as it respects the [object] from which the turning is, viz. sin, is called repentance. And as it respects the [object] to which, and the mediation by which [[it turns]], is called faith. But it is the same motion of the soul, as when a man turns or flies from darkness to the light. It is the same motion though it may be called by different names, as it respects the darkness fled from, and the light fled to; with respect to one, it is called fleeing from, and the other, receiving, or embracing.2

This passage may serve for Edwards on duplex gratia. For him as well as for Calvin, "pardon" and "newness of life" are always together, and from the same source, our "sharing in Christ."

Indeed, Calvin's exposition of repentance (newness of life) entails so thorough and so constant a turning to God that it embraces the whole of regeneration, so far as this is ever conferred in this life. "Therefore, in a word," he writes, "I interpret repentance as regeneration, whose sole end is to restore in us the image of God that had been disfigured and all but obliterated through Adam's transgression." This restoration "does not take place in one moment or one day or one year." Through "continual and sometimes even slow advances God wipes out... the corruptions of the flesh... consecrates... to himself as temples renewing all their minds to true purity that they may practice repentance throughout their lives and know that this warfare will end only at death."3

-- 749 --

Calvin's entire discussion under the head of regeneration begins, as do Edwards' charity sermons, with the indwelling Holy Spirit, the "secret energy" by which he "breathes divine life" into us.4 "As long as Christ remains outside us," all that he suffered (to purchase our pardon) remains useless to us.5 "As if we ought to think of Christ, standing afar off and not rather dwelling in us! For we await salvation from him, not because he appears to us afar off, but because he makes us, ingrafted into his body, participants not only in all his benefits but in himself... But since Christ has been so imparted to you with all his benefits that all his things are made yours."6 No student of Edwards can read those words without thinking of many themes in the charity sermons: the Spirit working "divine love" in the heart; communicating himself not only by external influence or "adventitiously" but in his own "proper" or essential nature; our becoming "partakers of the divine nature"; recreated in the "amiable image of Christ"; he having become ours.

The terms "faith," "righteousness," "repentance," "regeneration," "sanctification," "holiness" are used with overlapping if not shifting meanings in the Reformed tradition. Nevertheless, one thing is clear: free reconciliation and newness of life begin together at the same point and continue together throughout life. On the one hand Calvin writes "concerning our righteousness [reckoned to us] and sanctification," that "although we may distinguish them, Christ contains both in himself." To attain favor or pardon in God's sight, "you must first possess Christ; but you cannot possess him without being made partaker of his sanctification, because he cannot be divided into pieces." These benefits are bestowed "at the same time, the one never without the other." Thus we are "justified not without works yet not through works, since in our sharing in Christ, which justifies us, sanctification is just as much included as [imputed] righteousness."7

On the other hand, a righteousness not their own must always continue to be just as much included as sanctification if the salvation of believers and their works is sure. Why must this be so? Because the whole life of believers must, as we have seen, be a life of repentance, a turning to God, because the story of redemption through the regenerative work of the indwelling Spirit (infused righteousness) is never complete in this life. "After forgiveness of sins is set forth, the good works that now follow

-- 750 --

are appraised otherwise than on their own merit [as was the doer of them, in free reconciliation]. For everything in them is covered by Christ's perfection, every blemish or spot is cleansed away by his purity in order not to be brought in question at the divine judgment." So the good works done by believers still need to be "accounted" or "reckoned" righteous, if only the pure in heart are to see God.8

So it made sense to Calvin— and it was an embassy to be published daily to the hearts of men— that "man is so justified by faith that not only is he himself righteous but his works are also accounted righteous above their worth";9 that "a work begins to be acceptable only when it is undertaken with pardon"; that "by faith not only we ourselves but our works as well are justified."1 Having laid stress, and continuing to stress, moral newness of life, Calvin demands (by his standard of being steeped in holiness) to be shown one virtue or work that can claim "any right to be raised up either to destroy or becloud justification of faith."2 "Let one of Adam's children come forward with such uprightness. If there is no one, they must either perish out of God's sight or flee to the shelter of his mercy."3 So justification by faith remains, for believers, the main hinge on which religion turns.

Now, of course, we do not know that Edwards was steeped in the works of John Calvin.4 In fact, no Puritan ever believed that final truth had broken forth from the Scriptures in the writings of that man of God in Geneva. Still, no one can read Calvin, or the foregoing summary of his views, on the two forms of righteousness without noting the deep family resemblance between them and Edwards' views.
