APPENDIX V
DEFYING AND ASSISTING THE SPIRIT

BECAUSE this place in Scripture [Hebrews 6:4–6] is an occasion of much stumbling and difficulty to many, I would here be a little particular in explaining it." With these words Jonathan Edwards inserted a mini-sermon on the "unpardonable sin" into Charity, sermon two, pp. 163–66 above.Except for this digression which I call a mini-sermon, JE's second sermon is largely an exercise in comparative excellencies. That is, JE rhetorically praises the exceedingly great privilege granted to those who in former times had the extraordinary gifts, in order to evoke rejoicing over how much more excellent is the gift of saving grace or love in heart and life. One must turn to the fourteenth sermon for his more nuanced treatment of ordinary/extraordinary and common/uncommon gifts. See Intro., pp. 67–68, 70–71. The biblical passage reads: "For it is impossible for those, who were once enlightened, and have tasted of the heavenly gift, and were made partakers of the Holy Ghost, and have tasted the good work of God, and the powers of the world to come, if they shall fall away, to renew them again to repentance; seeing they crucify to themselves the Son of God afresh, and put him to open shame." In the early church's development of penance for grave sin after baptism, rigorists appealed to these words from the book of Hebrews against those who were willing to admit lapsed Christians again to communion, as St. Paul was (2 Corinthians 2:6–10) in the case of the Corinthian who first claimed "liberty" to live in incest but then repented. The entire passage from Hebrews was understood to refer to the truly converted, who had the "ordinary" gifts of saving grace and faith and were baptized, but who thereafter committed the deadly sins of murder, adultery, or apostasy.

Edwards understood the passage to refer to the possession of the extraordinary gifts by the ungodly, i.e., by only "professing" Christians. To fathom its meaning he read Hebrews 6:4–6 in the light of Acts 19:2–6 (the doctrine of baptisms), Matthew 13:18–23 with Luke 8:18 (the parable of the sower— which he correctly understood as a parable of the soils), and Numbers 22 (the story of Balaam's disobedience). He interpreted the fiery

-- 752 --

threats of the related Hebrews passage (Hebrews 10:25–26, beginning with the words "forsaking the assembling of ourselves together") in terms of an Old Testament "type," the presumptuous, willful sabbath breaker in Numbers 15:32–36. The source in oral tradition of this standard Puritan sermon on Hebrews 6:4–6 and the sin against the Holy Ghost was John Calvin.Institutes of the Christian Religion, III, iii, 21–24(McNeill, 1, 615–20). A truly inquisitive mind will consult this passage at once.

This place alone in the Andover copy required extensive editorial reconstruction. In all probability the Andover copy accurately represents the page(s) in Edwards' booklet. It resembles later sketchy sermons where delivery filled in the blanks. I judge that here also Edwards spoke largely from memory, as ordinarily he filled in the lines left for Scripture verses. Hence, more than a few notes to the text of sermon two were needed to indicate the source and reasons for the text I have constructed; and also to indicate Tryon Edwards' nineteenth-century cancellation and misrepresentation of the argument. Not to burden the pages of my text further, I here comment briefly on the theological problem Edwards addressed, i.e., how to commit the unpardonable sin in Northampton, or how not to be unduly distressed when hearing those words from Hebrews.

In Edwards' view, there had to be some foundation laid in order for one to commit the unpardonable sin. For requisite to this sin "a man, when convinced in conscience" sets himself "with a free and full will to reproach, or otherwise openly and contumaciously to malign the Holy Ghost in his office, or with respect to his gracious operations."Miscell. no. 380 (c. 1729). What sort of conviction in conscience was this? Did those who in former times sinned against the Holy Spirit and had "extraordinary gifts" have the requisite conviction? Certainly they had no new sense of the heart, no saving grace. Hence their condemnation was aggravated, the standard sermon said. But Edwards believed that the Pharisees were in fact convinced that Jesus' works were done by the power of the Spirit. In them was conviction as to the truth. He cited in evidence the fact that they did not condemn the watch for going to sleep while guarding the tomb of Christ, but instead instantly bribed them to keep the resurrection secret! They were "guilty of something of the nature of apostasy," even while they stirred up the people to crucify Christ "under that notion, that what he did was by the help of Beelzebub."Miscell. no. 475 (c. 1731).

-- 753 --

How then could the sin against the Holy Spirit be committed in Northampton, long after the age of miracles? Jonathan Edwards evidently wished to comfort and assure over-scrupulous souls as well as warn others of this dreadful possibility. Hence in his "Notes on the Bible"Dwight, Works, 9, 545–46. he identified the apostates of Hebrews 6:4–6 with those of Hebrews 10:25 who "forsake" the assemblies of Christians. So now in gospel times, to become a visible saint, to hear the Word of God preached, to attend upon his ordinances, to have God's preparatory work begun in the heart, to experience common convictions, common humiliations, and common religious affections, and therefore to begin to apprehend that there is a Savior or even to glimpse the secondary beauty of his garments, to have the sort of "faith" that passes away (sermon fourteen) and then openly turn back and no longer seek earnestly the more excellent way of divine love in the heart: this may be what some men do by positioning themselves contumaciously and with malignant malice against God's holiness and love that elicited their joining visible assemblies and participating in the common ordinary gifts. To reassure those over-scrupulous souls Edwards also taught that "those that are willing [earnestly] to use the means of grace, have not committed the unpardonable sin."Miscell. no. 406 (c. 1729–30).

A person, therefore, might turn, knowingly and with a "full will," against the "common ordinary" works of the Spirit, not only against him in his "common extraordinary" effects. Thus Edwards wrote: "In order to the sin against the Holy Ghost, persons may appear in avowed, malicious opposition and contumacy against the Holy Ghost in his work and office, and as communicated and as acting in them in his ordinary or extraordinary influences and operations."Miscell. no. 703. Ed. italics. The former refers to common illuminations, common convictions, etc. This, too, was to do "despite to the Spirit of grace" (Hebrews 10:29). Such ordinary operations "acting in them" must fall under the Spirit's acting upon them in contrast to dwelling in them in his own proper nature.See Charity, Sermon Two, p. 152, n. 3. Indeed, defiance of the Spirit in his common ordinary works was the only sin against the Holy Spirit possible in Northampton— since the extraordinary gifts of inspiration or of miracles were not to be expected there, then, or ever again.

This is also my reading of Miscell. no. 707.Miscell. nos. 703 and 707 were composed only a short time before JE preached the charity sermons. There Edwards says that in order to commit the unpardonable sin "there must be something that

-- 754 --

is of the nature of conviction that may be called a receiving the knowledge of the truth as Hebrews 10:26." The qualifying words "something that is of the nature of" and "may be called" are significant. More than wavering doubt, less than real conviction or knowledge of the truth: this state of the soul is sufficient to make it possible for "professing" Christians willfully to defy the work of the Holy Spirit among them. The "light" they go against, "[t]his light by being so spoken of seems to be the highest sort of light that natural men ever attain to or are capable of in a natural state, and it is probably not attained without great inward convictions of the Spirit of God, those that the Apostle has respect to in the 6th of Hebrews." Thus there are "degrees of light persons must have," degrees of inward convictions that are not saving or properly spiritual. Finally, in support of these readings of mine, consider the fact that the will is as is gracious affections in the heart; no one willfully and with a full heart goes against that uncommon work of the indwelling Spirit.Freedom of the Will, pp. 17, 144, 217, 222. Despite these evidences from Edwards' notebooks of his adherence to the standard sermon inserted in Charity, sermon two, his was not the way the Hebrews passage was understood in the contest between rigorists and accommodationists during the development of the practice of penance in the early church. Indeed, Edwards was mistaken if he believed that the author of Hebrews was a Calvinist who held that "saints" could never fall from grace, or that once Christ was theirs, they could never cease to be his nor be guilty of defying him face to face.

The problem with which we have been wrestling parallels, in reverse, how we are to understand "preparation" and the use of "means" in Puritan preaching and practice. Whether one can assist grace (and in what sense) parallels whether (and how on earth) one can resist grace unpardonably, in direct disrespect of the Spirit's operation. On the question to which we now turn, there is little explanatory value in simply saying that Edwards preached faith like a Reformed theologian and (especially in the Application to his sermons) preached works and striving toward perfection like an Arminian. More needs to be said than that about "the heart prepared."Cf. Norman Pettit, The Heart Prepared: Grace and Conversion in Puritan Spiritual Life (New Haven, Yale Univ. Press, 1966).

One of Edwards' sermons in the Andover collection is on the text, "But covet earnestly the best gifts: and yet show I unto you a more excellent way," 1 Corinthians 12:31. This is the transitional verse introductory to ch. 1 Corinthians 13. Because of the text's proximity to the charity texts and because

-- 755 --

the sermon's topic— preparation— is also in different fashion about the Christian moral life, a brief exposition of it should be included in this volume of ethical writings.The copy of this sermon is in the Andover coll., Franklin Trask Library, Andover-Newton Theological School, Newton Centre, Massachusetts. The hand is that of the second of Sereno E. Dwight's two copyists. Throughout Dwight has revised this sermon for possible publication. His handwriting does not overlay that of the copyist of the charity sermons. This deepens the mystery of why Dwight did not publish the charity sermons, since the sermon on 1 Corinthians 12:31 is sketchy, like the 1748 sermon on 1 Corinthians 13:8–13 on extraordinary gifts, whose argument is detailed in my footnotes to Charity. Probably neither sermon will ever be published.

The Doctrine Edwards draws from the text is "Christians should earnestly seek the best experiences." That earnest search, we shall see, is through multiple graded preparatory experiences. The sermon mentions the "common/saving" distinction crisscrossing the ordinary/ extraordinary distinction in the charity series. But Edwards promptly dispatches the "common extraordinary" gifts (the whole of ch. 1 Corinthians 12): "As to those miraculous influences of the Spirit that were experienced in the apostolical age, we are not concerned with them; they have long since failed, as the Apostle foretold, 1 Corinthians 13:8." These words are set down immediately after the statement of the brief Doctrine. Edwards is not concerned with anything that he had argued at length in Charity, sermons two and fourteen, although the same themes are recast in a preparatory mode. The sermon's encouragement to seek earnestly the best in influences and fruits of the Spirit among the "common" gifts must mean among the "ordinary" ones— since there are no more any "extraordinary" ones among which to seek. So the text suggests more than a movement away from the extraordinary gifts of ch. 1 Corinthians 12. And the sermon urges not simply a transition to one thing only, charity and its virtuous dispositions (ch. 1 Corinthians 13). Instead, there are two sets of degrees of excellence to be taken into account.

First, there are degrees of excellence among "ordinary" gifts "common" to the godly and ungodly alike. The Christian should earnestly seek the best of these common gifts. Second, there are degrees among saving graces. We learned from the charity series that all Christian graces are "concatenated together" in a unity of virtues not "distributively" distinct. But there was no attempt to indicate features that evidence degrees of excellence, except as love inhabits or causes them. The preacher is now concerned to show his congregation how to mark different degrees among the "particular Christian graces" considered in the concatenation sermon, and to urge them to seek earnestly the best of these also. Moreover, to seek earnestly the best among the common gifts is itself a

-- 756 --

way to seek earnestly the best among the saving graces. The sermon is about these two sets of degrees, and the nutrient relation of the first to the second. Plainly, to keep one's heart prepared for the descent of the dove is not easy; it also requires a rather complicated set of directions.

Among "common" gifts (convictions, etc.) that enliven the hearts of natural men, some are better than others; some are greater in degree; some greater in depth (these have a much better tendency than convictions small or light); some are more inward, and lead men more into their hearts than others; some have more resemblance to what is truly spiritual than others, some work by sorrow, some work by joy and delight. I am using Jonathan Edwards' language. Christians should seek earnestly the highest and deepest of these common ordinary gifts. Moreover, to do this is one way to seek earnestly the most excellent and saving gift, grace itself, for these distinctions are set forth under the first point of Doctrine, which reads: "Professing Christians should not content themselves with those religious experiences that are common, but should seek saving experience." Evidently hearts may be prepared to receive saving grace and indwelling love "mediately" or "indirectly" by seeking the best religious experiences that are among the common works of the Spirit. Before concluding under this head, Edwards undertakes to show "what are not sure characters of the most excellent experiences." These are not those attended with the most violent affections, tears, passions; not those that the subject at the time entertains the highest opinion of; not those that dispose persons to talking most about their experiences. Those are no certain signs that one covets earnestly the best experiences.

The second point of the doctrine (also the second preaching occasion) moves to positive signs of how to prepare the heart: "I would now endeavor to show what are the best sort of experiences." We may pass over many details and come at once to the noteworthy fact that Edwards' discourse under this point maps the territory of saving and particular graces (not "common").

In general, the excellence of saving experiences is to be measured by their nature, their degree, and their purity. On the essential nature of saving graces, the sermon notes, "The other graces of faith and hope are excellent principles as having something of the image of God in them, but charity is a more excellent grace than they, as the image of God does most essentially consist in it," 1 Corinthians 13:13. In degree, some graces have greater discovery of the glory of God than others. Again, one should seek stronger faith over weaker. On the test of purity we have an interesting reversal: as a guide to preparation of the heart, a mixture of natural

-- 757 --

principles with spiritual is some defect, while in True Virtue pure benevolence mixes and mingles its formative power with natural principles to direct them to the best.

Concerning "particular characters" that are distinguishing of degrees of excellence, a Christian should seek those experiences that are more attended with the greatest exercises of humility; those attended with the greatest self-denial;The copy continues: "This as the Rev. Mr. Whitefield says in one of his sermons is the grand secret of Christianity, the great secret of being a real Christian seems to lie very much here"— edited out by Dwight. This reference to a published sermon of Whitefield is one reason for dating JE's sermon on 1 Corinthians 12:31 later than the charity series. those that have the greatest effect on "the abiding temper of the soul" (some "common" experiences notably don't last long); and those that bring forth the most excellent fruits in practice. These are familiar themes used now by Edwards to chart preparatory pathways. If, for example, practice does not exactly make perfect, to seek earnestly experiences that are known to bear fruit in Christian practice is one way to have hearts prepared for the disposition freely to be kind to others that, itself, is directly and immediately a fruit of charity.

Before concluding this second preaching unit, Edwards gives "the reasons [his second point of doctrine] why Christians should earnestly court and seek those experiences that are the best and most excellent kind." There is reason to seek experiences most to the glory of God, most secure to the soul, most pure and satisfying evidences of a good estate, most lasting in the comfort afforded, that make men most serviceable to others, and that shall have the most excellent reward.

Edwards' Application "by way of direction" (his third preaching occasion) was "to direct what to do in order to an obtaining of the most excellent experiences." The five points under this "use" are worth outlining because they manifest the extraordinary probing nature of Puritan self-examination and self-understanding. (1) "Labor to grow in acquaintance with your own heart." "The greater knowledge any have of themselves, the more excellent spiritual experiences are their hearts prepared for." This knowledge is the end of the "common" work of the Spirit upon natural men! "So it is needful that persons should descend low into their own hearts." "Therefore, let Christians be advised as they would obtain excellent experiences, narrowly to observe, and watch over, and study their own hearts." (2) "Labor to abound in the most excellent practices. So to abound is "the way to have holy experiences; and doubtless to abound in the best and most excellent practices, is the way to have the best experiences" (Ed. italics). Among particular practices

-- 758 --

to be pursued are: (a) spiritual discoveries promised to disciples who obey Christ's commandments, John 14:21, John 14:23; (b) meekness of behavior, which especially leads to excellent experiences; (c) practices "attended with labor and suffering in the cause of Christ, and in the way of duty."This language is clearly that of Sermon Eleven of the charity series; this, along with the reference to Whitefield and the sketchiness of the sermon, demonstrates its later date. "Therefore," Edwards concludes, "let me advise all such as desire to have the most excellent experiences, to labor to abound in the most excellent practices." (3) "Endeavor to make progress in holiness in something of a constant, steady manner. If you do so, you will have more and more excellent experiences." "Your experiences will become more and more spiritual, having less mixture of what is common and natural" (Ed. italics). Don't be "standing water." (4) Inquire what fruits the experiences you were most "taken" with have brought forth. Have they given a "greater strength to your mind in running the Christian race?" (5) "Pray to God that you may never have any kind or degree of hope or comfort, but what is attended by these three effects, viz. humility, reverence and obedience." Better be without the sort of comfort that puffs up, or that does not engage you to reverence and serve God.

The sermon as delivered must have been an eloquent discourse on "the heart prepared." Reading it, one thinks of Augustine and Calvin on knowledge of God and of the soul.

The movement is the reverse of the work of the Spirit in the charity sermons. There, the indwelling Spirit communicates himself properly, in his own nature, divine love inherent in the subject; and the Spirit influences extrinsically through "common ordinary convictions" and "common" extraordinary gifts (the latter absent since the apostolic age). The later sermon reverses that movement. Professing Christians are urged to seek earnestly the best of the common ordinary gifts (going deep into one's own heart, etc.) and to seek earnestly the best saving experience; and the best of the former prepares the heart to receive the better saving graces (experiences of the highest excellence). All this is as much about the Christian moral life as the Spirit infusing virtuous dispositions.

No Puritans believed that they had any influence upon what Barth might have called the going forth of the Son of God into the far country of the human heart, or upon the return of the Son of Man to the Father's house (Edwards' Alpha and Omega).Karl Barth, Church Dogmatics, IV/2, §59, 1; IV/2, §64, 2. But Puritans did believe they could and should align their hearts' striving with the redemption in course."Assisting" the Spirit is misleading language if it suggests a freedom of contingent cooperation enabling God to grace the soul. "Preparation" the better word can also mislead to the notion that the soul can be or necessarily need be made capable of being graced. Terrence Erdt goes further in his ingenious argument that art as an instance of what JE termed secondary beauty "can perform a vital religious function by enabling the saint to conceive, and subsequently receive or revive, the particular emotional sensation that constitutes the religious experience— which Edwards referred to as the sense of the heart" (Jonathan Edwards: Art and the Sense of the Heart [Amherst, Univ. of Massachusetts Press, 1980], p. xi. Final italics are Erdt's. Cf. pp. 50, 61). The intricacy of Erdt's argument may escape me, since he twice quotes (pp. 50, 58; cf. 31, 48–49) the decisive passage from True Virtue that goes against his own thesis: the analogy God has made to himself, i.e., secondary beauty, whether material or immaterial, has "a tendency to assist those whose hearts are [already] under the influence of a truly virtuous temper, to dispose them to the exercises of divine love, and enliven in them a sense of spiritual beauty" (Ed. italics. Intro., p. 36, True Virtue, p. 565 above). In any case, I suggest that much of Erdt's exposition can better be understood as "preparation" (e.g., pp. 45–46, 56, 63). Insofar as he appeals to natural types, one needs to remember that the antitype of Christ alone unveils them: only a mind saturated in biblical typology will have a ravishing sense of the glory of God in trees and fields. The author's fine comparison of JE's "aesthetics" with those of his Puritan predecessors (ibid., pp. 64–77; I would say "rhetoric"), and JE's interest in romantic novels for promoting piety and virtue (ibid., pp. 78–82) do not support the proposition that seeking earnestly the best secondary beauty (art) fosters a new sense of the heart directly or immediately, or even that JE would have prompted people to experiences of the best secondary beauty (material or immaterial) as a way to prepare their hearts.

-- 759 --

Having once tasted the heavenly gifts of "common" convictions, "common" religious affections, "common" faith, and so on, having heard some echo in the mind of the truth and reality of divine things, having once in the assemblies at the meetinghouse been moved a little by the Spirit to adhere to the gospel proclaimed to them, it was possible to realign one's soul's striving again toward worldly-mindedness, contumaciously to defy those Spirit operations, to bring the heart into disrepair. Puritans saw no contradiction here. The wind still bloweth where he listeth. Indeed, Edwards' mini-sermon on the sin against the Holy Spirit was for "preparation"; its use was of "warning" as well as of "consolation."
