EDITOR'S INTRODUCTION

-- 1 --

IN the year 1738 Jonathan Edwards preached a series of sermons on 1 Corinthians 13. This sermon series is printed first in this volume of his ethical writings because it came first chronologically, but more because it is an extraordinary, systematic treatise on the Christian moral life. By contrast, the two philosophical-theological dissertations completing this volume are sustained essays on particular topics.1 The series is printed here rather than among the volumes of sermons in the Yale edition because these sermons gained a historical identity of their own when, more than a hundred years after they were preached, Tryon Edwards published them as a series of "Lectures" under the title Charity and Its Fruits.2 The text printed here is a nineteenth-century copy of Edwards' sermon notebooks (which Tryon probably discarded). The copy is in the Edwards collection at Andover-Newton Theological Seminary.

When Edwards preached his sermons in praise of charity, he was in his thirty-fifth year, about midway through his ministry at Northampton. The first revival (1734–36) was but a vivid memory, its fervor dampened. "Surprising works" of the Holy Spirit were again to descend in 1740–43 (this revival, known as the Great Awakening, encompassed the Middle and Southern colonies and the itinerancy of George Whitefield). Edwards was busy recording, explaining, analyzing, and defending the phenomena of the Northampton revival. A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God (begun as a letter to Benjamin Coleman in 1735) was first published in London in 1737, the year before the

-- 2 --

charity sermons. Two years later, The Distinguishing Marks of the Work of the Spirit of God was delivered as a commencement address at Yale College on September 10, 1741. Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival was published in Boston in 1743. Throughout this period Edwards carried on a voluminous correspondence with both critics and supporters of the revivals.3

In the midst of these events and controversies, Edwards preached three remarkable series of sermons. The first of these was his hymn to charity in 1738, printed here. The following year he used thirty pulpit occasions to preach a lengthy sermon-discourse on Isaiah 51:8, which (at least after the fact) he planned to develop into a treatise on the work of redemption.4 The third was a series on 1 Peter 1:8, preached probably in 1742 and 1743. Edwards rewrote and published this last in 1746 as A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections,5 the finest fruit of the revival controversy, generally regarded as a major work in the psychology of religion. Affections can also be read as moral theology; and when so read, Charity is clearly superior to it.

Of these three important series of sermons, Charity came closest in time to the fervor and ferment of the first revival in Northampton under Edwards' preaching. Students of Edwards need to take into account these discourses to the glory of divine love— when, for example, interpreting Distinguishing Marks or Some Thoughts or the section on "Distinguishing Signs of Truly Gracious and Holy Affections," Pt. III of Affections. The seeds and more than the seeds of these attempts to sort out the "raised" affections are already in Charity.

Therefore, the sermons on 1 Corinthians 13 may be interpreted (a) as a finely woven systematic treatise on the Christian moral life, (b) as one that follows the story line of the work of redemption, and (c) as one of Edwards' major accounts, composed in the context of the revivals, of gracious affections (divine love) in heart and life. The first two of these three ways of reading the charity sermons— true virtue and its virtues— are summarily restated in Edwards' abbreviated account of a right holy life in True Virtue.6 The third reading is amply indicated in my footnotes to

-- 3 --

the sermons. In these notes I correlate "fruits" of charity with Edwards' account of one or another of the "marks" or "signs." The connection between the first "ethical writing" in this volume and Edwards' writings on the Great Awakening is often one of verbally identical expression, not merely similar meaning.

It is a major fault in any scholar to treat the charity sermons as a surd among Edwards' works. A student's first effort must be to understand Edwards whole, in the integrity of all parts of his writings on theological subjects. For some such reason, I believe, H. Richard Niebuhr chose the works to be included in the present volume (which he had intended to edit), despite the decades separating the 1738 sermons from the posthumously published works.

The title page of Tryon Edwards' nineteenth-century edition of the charity sermons states that he edited them from the original manuscripts. In addition, Tryon's Preface7 claims that the 1738 sermons were "apparently designed by himself for publication." Mere association with Edwards' "discourses" the following year on the history of redemption— which it is known, he intended should be published— is not a very good reason for that supposition. Tryon gave as another reason the fact that the sermon booklets were "written out in full." If Tryon meant that the booklets he edited (and probably discarded from the collection) had Scripture verses fully written out, he had better reason for supposing that Jonathan Edwards planned to publish the series. In footnotes to the text8 I draw attention to the skilled way in which it uses moral terms drawn from Scripture. It strains belief that a copyist located from scriptural verses and lists of spiritual gifts the exact precept or disposition of heart Edwards had in mind, and so unerringly filled in the blank lines that ordinarily follow Scripture citations in Edwards' sermon booklets. If writing out biblical verses indicates Edwards' design to publish, then we have the unanswerable question why he did not do so— since, as they stand, the series is already one connected discourse.

Tryon Edwards' Preface further states that after Jonathan Edwards' death the charity sermons were "selected for publication by Dr. Hopkins and Dr. Bellamy; and by the latter were in part copied out and prepared for the press, when for some reason he was interrupted in their publication." How could Tryon know this, so many decades after the event?

There is an indeterminate reference to sermons in a letter from Samuel Hopkins to Joseph Bellamy concerning their joint publishing project that by some stretch of the imagination might be a reference to the partial copy of the charity sermons by Bellamy, which later, in the nineteenth century, Tryon somehow knew about. From Great Barrington, Massachusetts, on January 4, 1764, Hopkins wrote Bellamy concerning the transcription of some sermons:

I should be glad if you would transcribe some of the sermons you mention without any ifs or ands. I and my brother have transcribed 10 sermons which I hope to send to Boston soon, but these with the life and the 6 Mr. Searl has transcribed, will not be enough to make one Vol. 16 sermons will be about 200 pages... Most of the sermons have blanks. Where I think it not possible or proper to fill them up, I draw a ___, of which the reader must be notified in an advertisement.9

-- 4 --

Hopkins' Memoir on Jonathan Edwards was first published in 1765 with eighteen "Sermons on Various Important Subjects."1 His preface to the Memoir is dated August 20, 1764; his preface to the eighteen sermons printed with it, April 25, 1765. Likely, only two sermons had to be added to the sixteen Hopkins mentioned in the letter quoted above. Hopkins was not waiting to receive sermons transcribed by Bellamy to be added to his volume.2 He was simply urging Bellamy to speed up his transcription of another set of sermons altogether, by pressing him to "transcribe some of the sermons you mention without any ifs or ands." These expressions are consistent with Hopkins' having knowledge of the special importance of the sermons Bellamy had begun to transcribe. Was Hopkins referring to the 1738 charity sermons, which (according to Tryon Edwards'

-- 5 --

Preface) both men selected for publication and Bellamy began to copy? All one can say is that Hopkins' language is consistent with that supposition; perhaps for this reason, he pressed Bellamy to go forward without delay with his transcription. These conjectures led, in 1982, by remote and chance connections, to the discovery and correct identification of three four-page fragments of Joseph Bellamy's part-copy, classified as Bellamy sermons in the collection in the Hartford Seminary Library. The story continues, the three fragments are printed, and some passages from them are compared with Tryon's edition and with the text of the present edition, in Appendix I below.

The second and third of the three works comprising this volume are Two Dissertations. I. Concerning the End for Which God Created the World. II. On the Nature of True Virtue.3 I ought rather to have said, the second of two works comprising this volume, for Edwards intended these dissertations to be published together. The one is the mirror image of the other; the "end" for which God created the world must be the "end" of a truly virtuous and holy life. The present volume reproduces the unity and the sequence of the component parts of the original edition of Two Dissertations. The use of that short title might help avoid some misinterpretations of Edwards' ethics.

In recent years the second of the two dissertations has frequently been printed and circulated alone under the title The Nature of True Virtue.4 It

-- 6 --

is one of the most widely known of Edwards' works, if not indeed the most well known. No admirer of the thought and writings of Edwards can regret the attention paid to any single one of his major works. Still, interpretations of True Virtue made without regard to its connection with End of Creation have fostered inadequate and even quite mistaken understandings of his ethical writings.5

-- 7 --

Placing Charity first in this volume of Edwards' Ethical Writings is obviously justified, because the sermon series was the first of the three ethical writings the author produced. However, one might still question whether End of Creation should precede True Virtue, or the other way round. The fact that they were originally printed with End of Creation first does not quite settle the issue, since we do not know how Edwards left them tied together. In footnotes to the text below, I argue for the priority of End of Creation from the fact that (as Austin pointed out) in True Virtue there are two bracketed references (in subsequent editions turned into footnotes) to End of Creation, as if to a former companion treatise. These notes specifically say "former." Yet the question might continue to be debated on the basis of external evidence, for these references could have been inserted by the editor or the printer in 1765. One reference to End of Creation as the former treatise, however, seems to me to settle the matter. There, the reference is more than a mere citation or cross-reference that credibly might have been inserted by a later hand. Instead, Edwards' argument is greatly foreshortened at that point in the text of True Virtue, so much so that to comprehend it would require rereading the argument of End of Creation. Edwards expected his readers already to have gone through End of Creation; and, with both dissertations in hand, to be able to refer to it, and reread, when first reading True Virtue. This internal evidence seems to me compelling.6 The reader now has the two dissertations before him in the sequence in which Edwards intended them to be understood— plus the prospect of beginning with Edwards' first and major account of a truly virtuous and holy life, for which objective moral end God created the world.

When I assumed responsibility for the volume of ethical writing in the Yale edition, Thomas A. Schafer transcribed for me "The Nature of True Virtue" from Edwards' massive unpublished "Book on Controversies."7 This has been invaluable to me and, I believe, also for readers of the present volume and future interpreters of Edwards. There are three

-- 8 --

brief notes to himself at the beginning of this section of the "Book on Controversies"; these are integral to the main body of it. One of these notes reads, "No true virtue without supreme love to god and making God our supreme end. See Miscell. 1208 B. 8." That reference is to Miscell. no. 1208. END OF CREATION. GLORY OF GOD. Nature of REDEMPTION. SATISFACTION OF CHRIST. Nature of TRUE VIRTUE AND RELIGION. Schafer dates this entry not later than mid— 1753, and he dates the "Controversies"— draft of True Virtue later that year or early 1754.8 This means that by 1753–54 Edwards had written out the entirety of Two Dissertations in far more than outline form and frequently with verbally identical elaboration of arguments. These are actually penultimate drafts of End of Creation and of True Virtue, page after page marked, when later used, by lines along the left margin. Only the topic "satisfaction" was eliminated from Miscell. no. 1208.9

-- 9 --

Since the entire argument of Two Dissertations is fully rendered in these earlier versions, footnotes are frequently required to identify the connections and to offer readers the opportunity to compare passages from Edwards' penultimate version with his final revision. This I also undertake to do at significant points in Edwards' lifelong wrestle with these themes in the "Miscellanies."1 But there is little that needs to be said after the years 1753–54 as background for understanding Two Dissertations. Indeed, by then Edwards had all his great, late works firmly in mind, spelled out in notebooks, and woven together in an integrated system of thought.

His puzzlement was how he would allocate them to volumes and in what order. Another note at the beginning of the "Controversies"— draft, for example, reads: "Observe how whatever modern philosophers call natural moral taste is a different thing from virtue and the love of virtue. Treat of this either here or in the Discourse on Original Sin according as which is published first. See on Orig. Sin p. 97. a." That reference to a draft of Original Sin earlier in the "Controversies" notebook (where there is a cross-reference to the note just quoted) reads: "Perhaps under the head of original sin treat of MORAL TASTE."2 (This note is cancelled by a vertical line about an inch from the left margin— a

-- 10 --

line that may have been drawn when Edwards decided to ready Original Sin for publication.3)

Indeed, Edwards' notes concerning method of publication indicate that he considered issuing the various treatises that neared completion together in the last several years of his life in a work that we might call "Three Dissertations," or "Four." In the middle of MS p. 102 of "Original Sin" in the "Book on Controversies" he wrote (this note is also cross-referenced and cancelled):

METHOD. Perhaps in more volumes than one a treatise the first part concerning the nature of true virtue, and in this treat of God's end in creating the world, and in the next part concerning original sin an inquiry into the truth of the doctrine, both as to the corruption of nature and imputation [...] and its total corruption, and then concerning infused habits and concerning saving grace's differing from common grace in nature and kind, and concerning that natural taste that natural men [have] to show that there is nothing of true virtue in it.

In fact, all these works— together with Edwards' constant probing of these and other topics in the "Miscellanies"— were expected to be parts of his "Rational Account."

Does the foregoing passage contain reference to a treatise on grace, in addition to true virtue, God's end, and original sin? If so, was this the Treatise on Grace that Grosart published in the nineteenth century from among papers given him by Tryon Edwards?4 These questions have no certain answers. But from several leaves found in the Andover collection,5 Thomas A. Schafer estimates the original MS on grace to have been written earlier, between 1739 and 1743. This span of time was shortly after Edwards delivered the charity sermons. So the reader will want to pay attention to the structural similarity between Edwards on grace and Edwards on divine love in the heart.6

Edwards finished Original Sin and sent it off to the press. That left God's end and true virtue, of the topics mentioned as publishable together one way or another. In Original Sin, published shortly after his death, Edwards wrote:

-- 11 --

As to the arguments made use of by many late writers, from the universal moral sense, and the reasons they offer from experience, and observation of the nature of mankind, to shew that we are born into the world with principles of virtue; with a natural prevailing relish, approbation, and love of righteousness, truth and goodness and whatever tends to the public welfare... a native bent in mankind to mutual benevolence, tender compassion, etc. those who have had such objections against the doctrine of original sin, thrown in their way, and desire to see them particularly considered, I ask leave to refer them to a Treatise on the Nature of True Virtue, lying prepared for the press, which may ere long be exhibited to view.7

Thus the decision was made not to include in Original Sin the reflections and argument on "moral taste" that Edwards had accumulated over a lifetime in his "Miscellanies." Nevertheless, the last work Edwards sent to press was itself directed against philosophers who affirmed man's natural worthiness to receive God's favor, or even that our natural goodness is in some way the measure of God's goodness or of his worthiness to be worshiped.

Throughout his entire life Edwards returned again and again to such topics as moral taste, God's end in creation, the history of redemption, original sin, the nature of true virtue and a right holy life, gracious affections, infused virtue, divine love, the Trinity; and these interrelated themes, as it were, crowded together into the treatises he wrote during his years at Stockbridge. Any decision Edwards might have made, or did make, concerning publication required tearing apart the integrated exposition and argument he had time and again set down in notebooks. The multiple titles he gave to numerous entries in the "Miscellanies" are sufficient alone to show that (in order to have a mind rightly prepared to read the present volume) it is a grave error, now or ever, to separate Edwards' philosophy from his theology, or his moral philosophy from his theological ethics.

One thing more needs to be noted from the "Controversies"— draft of True Virtue. Toward the end Edwards wrote another note to himself, which reads "METHOD. Show how all the virtues are derived from pure benevolence."8 This one-line paragraph was cancelled when done. But the plan was executed in only one page of True Virtue, in Edwards' terse list of several virtues derived from pure benevolence alone: a virtuous

-- 12 --

pity, virtuous gratitude, a virtuous love of justice, a virtuous sense of desert, a virtuous conscientiousness (sanctified conscience), a virtuous love of parents and children, "yea, and a virtuous love between the sexes."9 Any promise Edwards might have made to elaborate fully upon how all the virtues derive from pure benevolence was adequately executed in the corpus of Edwards' writings only in the charity sermons preached nearly twenty years before he wrote that note.1

I have now discharged the main part of the first duty of an editor, namely, to tell the reader what he has in his hands.2 Let this suffice also to explain why in the following sections of this introduction I undertake a systematic analysis of important themes in Edwards' ethical writings, with only light stress on the times and circumstance of their composition. One needs first of all to comprehend Edwards' ethics whole and entire and appreciate the integrity of internally related concepts which run through the three (or two) works printed here.3

I have chosen the alternative of systematic analysis of selected themes chiefly because the power and the coherence of Edwards' ethical writings demand such treatment. One studies the times and backgrounds of some men in order to understand them. Others have such rare greatness that one studies them in order to understand their times, or even to comprehend the deepest meaning of the intellectual and other influences that were effectual upon them. Jonathan Edwards was such an original.

Self-Love, Love of Happiness, Love to God and to Neighbor

In one of the "Miscellanies" Edwards asks the question whether each and every one of the desires of human hearts has become exorbitant as a

-- 13 --

consequence of the Fall. He answers no— because love of happiness (which necessarily means love of one's own happiness) cannot be too much. A cynic always wins the debate over whether all people are selfish by failing to distinguish between a blameworthy selfishness and the general human endowment to love happiness. Yet a love of happiness is evidently different from always finding that happiness in a narrow, confined love of self as if the self were all— in Ibsen's apt description of Peer Gynt, living in "a cask of self, stopped with a bung of self."

Contemporary ethicists often break apart these two meanings of self-love by speaking of the grammatically "ambiguous genitive" case in present-day English language usage in such expressions as "the love of God" or "the love of self." The love of God can mean either God's love to us or our love to God. The love of self can mean the self's love to itself only (or to an extended social ego), or it may mean the self's love of whatever the self loves, whether that is its love to itself as object, or its love to God or to neighbor, or its love of learning, beauty, sensual pleasure, or anything else.

Edwards does not employ this distinction between the objective and subjective genitive. Instead, he plays out the same analysis in terms of what can only be called the "ambiguous pronoun." A man's love of his own happiness, he writes, is "ambiguous... as the pronoun ‘his own’ is equivocal" and can be taken in "two very different senses." A self's love of its own happiness limited to love to self is narrow, confined. But a self's love of its own happiness may simply mean the self's love of whatever is "grateful4 and pleasing," more often than not found in other objects than the self sole.

Love of one's own happiness is "universal" in all, the same degree in all. To resolve the will and action of moral agents into nefarious self-love on the grounds that the propensity of will and action is our own happiness was for Edwards nonsense. That amounts to saying that there is something censurable in the fact that moral agents have wills, and differ from "stones and trees, which love nothing and hate nothing." Within the definition of self-love as love of one's own happiness, to put the

-- 14 --

question "whether all our love... don't arise from self-love" is an "impropriety and absurdity." For this is only to say,

self-love is a man's liking, and being suited and pleased in that which he likes, and which pleases him; or that 'tis a man's loving what he loves. For whatever a man loves, that thing is grateful and pleasing to him, whether that be his own peculiar happiness, or the happiness of others. And if this be all that they mean by self-love, no wonder they suppose that all love may be resolved into self-love. For it is undoubtedly true that whatever a man loves, his love may be resolved into his loving what he loves— if that be proper speaking. If by self-love is meant nothing else but a man's loving what is grateful or pleasing to him, and being averse to what is disagreeable, this is calling that "self-love" which is only a general capacity of loving, or hating; or a capacity of being either pleased or displeased: which is the same thing as a man's having a faculty of will.

The notion that men can fail to love their own happiness in whatever they find objectively "grateful" or pleasing amounts to the absurdity that they "incline to nothing and will nothing." This is one main theme running through Edwards' ethics: I have just been quoting from his treatment of it in True Virtue.5

Such "self-love taken in the most extensive sense" was already Edwards' analysis twenty-three years before True Virtue when he wrote Miscell. no. 530. LOVE TO GOD. SELF-LOVE (c. 1731–32), at twenty-nine years of age.6 Again, some seventeen years before True Virtue was written,

-- 15 --

Edwards made the same point preaching on a text that uses the ambiguous pronoun, charity "seeketh not her own" (one of his famous 1738 series on 1 Corinthians 13). That cannot possibly mean that a Christian spirit is contrary to all self-love, contrary to all love of one's own happiness. Love of one's own happiness is as necessary to man's nature "as a faculty of will is." To destroy that would be "to destroy the humanity." Thus in the seventh sermon of the charity series, Edwards signals the importance of this question by affirming that the point "needs to be well stated; for the resolution of many scruples and doubts, which persons have, depends upon it."7 The first step is to fix firmly in mind that what is censurable in self-love does not consist in love of our own happiness "being absolutely considered in too high a degree." Man cannot love happiness too much, if such love is viewed simpliciter. Such love is not "a thing liable to diminution or increase." It is "alike in all" because it belongs to the "nature of all intelligent beings" as necessary to their nature "as a faculty of will is." Love of one's happiness is without degree.

Such self-love our first parents had before the Fall, in high degree, in no degree too great; or better to say, simply in no degree, since it was a thing not to be compared. "Saints and sinners" in this life "love happiness alike, and have the same unalterable propensity to seek and desire happiness." Finally, in the redemption of creatures on earth, it would be an absurdity to suppose that happiness increases as the love of it decreases upon the infusion of love to God; or that the holy saints above are happy, but their love of happiness diminished as their holiness increased. The saints and angels in heaven love their own happiness. If this were not so, heaven "would be no happiness to them; for that which anyone does not love, he can enjoy no happiness in."

Here we have a correlation of the nature of the will with love, and of love with happiness, that cannot be other than "one's own" while it is willed and loved and enjoyed— a correlation matched only by that in Augustine.8 Since will and love are alike in all, the meaning of inordinate

-- 16 --

self-love, to which a Christian spirit is contrary, cannot lie in too much love of one's happiness.

So far, we have one "principle" founded in human hearts. Edwards used the word "principle" in the sense of the Latin principium or the Greek arché. The word "principle" means a source or beginning or spring of disposition and action. But it also means the direction, shape, or contours of human hearts and lives, as in the root of our word "archetype," or the arché or formative power of Plato's ideas, such as justice or beauty, or triangularity. So when the first verse of St. John's Gospel reads "In the beginning was the Word," that in Greek is en arché and in Latin inprincipio. The verse not only points to the source and beginning of all things, without whom "was not anything made that was made." These verses also tell us something about the channel in which the whole creation runs, its shape, meaning, and direction.

A universal and ineradicable will and love of happiness, Edwards says in his sermon, makes the world go round. But that propensity of "intelligent willing perceiving" beings explains nothing specific. It does not explain why we find one particular object of love "grateful and pleasing" and not another. It does not explain the reason why such and such things become an agent's happiness, or why we look on others as ourselves, and so on their happiness as our own. Consider also Edwards' exclamation, "What can more properly be called love to any being, or any thing, than to place one's happiness in that thing?"9 and his use of "placing" happiness to depict the goodness of Christ and of the saints in the charity sermons. An agent's general capacity to place his love of happiness anywhere or in any object, somewhere or in some object, and to find bis, own happiness therein, "can never be a reason why men's love is placed in such and such objects" and not in others. A "capacity of enjoying happiness, cannot be the reason why such and such things

-- 17 --

become his happiness."1 The difference between Hitler and you and me, and between Mother Teresa and you and me, lies not in our common love of happiness, but rather in what each of us looks on as that in which our own happiness consists. We need other principles to explain why we creatures move the world the way we do.

Two questions then: What accounts for the fact that love to God, and glorifying him and praising him forever, becomes a human heart's delight? What accounts for the fact that a moral agent lights upon the happiness of another human being as that in which love of his own happiness is gratified? Edwards' answer to this twofold question (which is the question asked of us by the twofold love commandment) is that only some "compounded self-love" can be any further explanatory— that is, love of happiness compounded with some other principle laid in human hearts and lives. This notion of principles conjunctively explanatory of particular loves and particular joys was best expressed in Miscell. no. 530. Compounded self-love, Edwards wrote,

arises from the necessary nature of a perceiving and willing being, whereby he loves his own pleasure or delight; but not from this alone, but it supposes also another principle, that determines the exercise of this principle, and makes that to become its object which otherwise cannot: a certain principle uniting this person with another, that causes the good of another to be its good. The first arises simply from his own being whereby that which agrees immediately and directly with his own being, is his good; the second arises also from a principle uniting him to another being, whereby the good of that other being does in a sort become his own.2

Once more the mouth of the cynic must be stopped. The cynic is liable to say that love of our own happiness can be stretched to explain, by itself, every particular love. We secondarily and circuitously love another person because we first and primarily love our own specific happiness. All other-love is for our own sakes.3

-- 18 --

This may be the case in the world as it is, because of a principle of sin acting conjunctively with willing beings' love of their own happiness. Indeed, love to self or to some extensive "private system" is the principle treated at length in True Virtue, Ch. IV, and in sermon seven. Still, another principle in the heart was, for Edwards, not a metaphysical impossibility: there is no contradiction in assuming that a human being first loves another and then finds or places his own happiness in the other's happiness, looks on that other's happiness as his own.4

Indeed, he argued in True Virtue that such a cynical location of a selfish system in the nature of the will (rather than in its corruption) is nonsense. That account makes the effect (our own happiness) "the cause of that of which it is the effect." It makes "our happiness, consisting in the happiness of the person beloved... the cause of our love to that person." Plainly the truth must be the other way round: "our love to the [other] person is the cause of our delighting, or [as an effect] being happy in his happiness. How comes our happiness to consist [particularly] in the happiness of such [persons] as we love, but our hearts being

-- 19 --

first united to them in affection, so that as it were, we look on them as ourselves, and so on their happiness as our own?"5

So the question is, How comes the universal nature of a willing perceiving being, loving its own happiness, to be compounded with a principle of unitive love or truly virtuous benevolence and unalloyed goodwill toward another? In the familiar language of Scripture, How come love to God and love to neighbor to be what our happiness consists in? How can we creatures, who are on earth and not in heaven, place our happiness in God's glory and look on another person's happiness as our very own? Only if, in truth, we are first made one with them in "pure" or "absolute" benevolence, Edwards answers.

We are approaching, with increasing celerity, the very center of Christian theology. On the relation to God and his creatures, first let us ask Edwards, in the simplest terms, Whence come any knowledge of God and any love of God?

In the twentieth century Karl Barth answered, "Only God can reveal knowledge of God." That statement, on simple inspection, clearly must be true. No one would think of denying it but for the fact that man is (as Plato said) that most religious of animals and (as Calvin taught) the human mind a veritable factory of approximate ideas about the ultimate nature of things.

Jonathan Edwards in the eighteenth century said why only God can reveal knowledge of God: because God's knowledge of himself is the only knowledge of God there is. I cannot get to know you, i.e., I cannot get to know anything about you that is of any importance to you or to me, unless you communicate yourself to me in words without dissimulation or in actions that are authentically yours. These remain your words and actions, though they resonate in my heart and life. If the word from you that I hear is not and does not remain yours in reality, or if my hearing is only some echo of your word, I will never know more than something between us, nor know any more than something about you, but never you. In self-disclosure, the word sent forth and the hearing of it are one. In like manner, knowledge of God cannot be knowledge about God or knowledge of him from a distance.6

-- 20 --

So if there is knowledge of God communicated to any human understanding, this is God's very own knowledge of himself, for that happens to be the only knowledge of God there is in heaven or on earth. If there is any love to God enkindled in any human will, this must be God's own love spread abroad in human hearts. If there is in human affections any happiness, joy, and delight in God, this must be God's own felicity communicated to us. These are the divine principles needed to "further inform" natural principles. When mingled and compounded with love of our own happiness, they direct and channel that love into love to God in particular, so that our greatest good and happiness consists in his glory.

We have now come upon expressions that look like "ambiguous genitives," but according to Edwards only seem so: the knowledge of God, the love of God. The two possible meanings of these expressions are not equivocal. Indeed, there are not two meanings. As a datum in our language, of course, "the knowledge of God" can mean either our knowledge of God or God's knowledge of himself; but according to Edwards these two are but one and the same thing. If there is any such thing as a creature's knowledge of God, this must be the creature's participation in God's knowledge of himself. "The love of God" grammatically can mean either our love of God or God's own love in himself; but according to Edwards, these are but one and the same thing. If there is any such thing as a creature's love to God, this must be an infusion of God's own love.

The simple fact that in past times English-speaking people used both of and s in genitives bears out the foregoing interpretation of Edwards. They wrote and said, "the house of my father's" for "my father's house." Similarly, in Miscell. no. 1084 Edwards wrote that John 17:267 expressed "two ways of God's flowing forth and being communicated... viz. manifesting God's name and communicating his love; or in other words Christ being in the creature in the name, idea or knowledge

-- 21 --

of God being in them, and the Holy Spirit's being in them in the love of God's being in them." Edwards himself punctuated that last line. He awkwardly (it seems to us) wrote, "the love of God's being in them," where ordinarily he would write "love to God" or "divine love" dwelling in the heart. I suggest that his rare use here of the ambiguous genitive "the love of God," which is our usual practice today, followed by a possessive s ("the love of God's being in them") reinforces the conclusion that, for Edwards, if an intelligent, willing perceiving creature is disposed and determined to love to God, this must be from partaking of the divine love. Edwards might also have punctuated the preceding phrase to read "Christ being in the creature in the name, idea or knowledge of God's being in them." No creature has a natural capacity to actualize in itself knowledge of or love to God; that is the common teaching of classical Christian theology. In evangelical Christianity in America this was, of course, and still should be, the meaning of being "born again."

Unique to Edwards is only the philosophical setting of this understanding of God and his creatures. Thus Edwards believed that "perceiving being only, is properly being"; minds, spirits only, are properly beings; to speak of material things as "beings" is to speak improperly. God "communicates himself properly only to spirits; and they only are capable of being proper images of his excellency, for they only are properly beings... Yet he communicates a sort of shadow or glimpse of his excellence to bodies which... are but the shadows of being, and not real beings."8 In one of his notes on "The Mind," Edwards asks us to make the following supposition: "Suppose that we received none of the sensible qualities of light, colors, etc. from the resisting parts of space (we will suppose it possible for resistance to be without them), and they were to appearance clear and pure, and all that we could possibly observe was only and merely resistance." Suppose, in other words, that we are hummingbirds bumping into the clear glass of a window pane. Then all we would observe of "solidity" would be "that certain parts of space... resist anything coming within them." We would never bump into matter. All we would experience would be "impenetrability"; and all we need to suppose to account for the bump-up-againstness is some immediate action that prevents coming within various parts of space. We are stopped here and now, and not elsewhere or later. According to Edwards, God's immediate action accounts for the resistance bumped into whenever we encounter solid, impenetrable bodies. No matter there. Of course, it is

-- 22 --

natural for men to suppose that there is "some latent substance, or something that is altogether hid," which upholds every moment the solidity of created bodies. "All therefore agree that there is something that is there, and upholds these properties; and it is most true, there undoubtedly is. But men are wont to content themselves in saying merely that it is something; but that ‘something’ is he by whom all things consist."9

This is the ontology into which Edwards set his understanding of God and his creatures. He is nothing if not ontological and theological or biblical at the same time. Here we have a sort of "scale of being." As surely as an archangel is further removed from nonentity than is a worm or a flea,1 so spiritual beings— the only proper beings— are further removed from nonentity than bodies. To this we can now add that "understanding and will are the highest kind of created existence," higher than merely perceiving being. Then to that we can add: understanding and will in exercise are higher in actuality than those faculties dormant. Finally "and certainly the most excellent actual knowledge and will that can be in the creature is the knowledge and the love of God.2

A being to whom has been imparted knowledge of God and love to God is the being of a creature furthest removed from nonentity. This was Edwards' interpretation of St. Paul's "if any man be in Christ, he is a new creature" (2 Corinthians 5:17).

The increasing knowledge of God in all elect creatures to all eternity is an existence, a reality, infinitely worthy to be in itself... If existence is more worthy than defect and nonentity, and if any created existence is in itself worthy to be, then this knowledge of God and his glory is worthy to be. The existence of the created universe consists as much in it as in anything, yea, it is one of the highest, most real and substantial parts of all created existence; most remote from nonentity and defect."3

Thus, the "new creation" is the name, idea or knowledge of God's, being in human understanding; the love of God's, being in human wills, and the joy of God's, being in human affection. This was the original ultimate end God had in the creation of the world.

So Edwards in the eighteenth century joins Barth in the twentieth in

-- 23 --

the belief that it is not God whose existence needs proof, but rather the existence of anything other than God, any world at all, that needs explaining. Edwards' cosmic teleology was ultimately Christological; the work of redemption accounted for the history of the universe at large. God's end in the natural world was the end of the moral world, the end of the moral order was the end of the good part of the moral world, the end of the good part of the moral world was the end of the saints at their best, venting their souls in praise of God, and the end of the best part of the moral world at their best was what Jesus Christ sought as his last or ultimate end, in his "great request" in what we call his High Priestly prayer: "that they may have my joy fulfilled in themselves"; "that they all may be one; as thou Father, art in me, and I in thee, that they may be one in us... that they may be one, even as we are one" (John 17:13, John 17:21–22).4 That was Edwards' ultimate answer to the question, Whence came knowledge of God to any human understanding, and whence any love to God? Since the being of that knowledge, love, and joy is existence most worthy of being, most remote from nonentity, the same was his answer to the perplexing question— if this be proper speaking— why on earth there is any being outside of God himself.

"Divine love" in the heart also goes out to our fellow creatures. Characteristically, Edwards always spells out "Do unto others as you would that others do to you" by adding "If you were in their circumstances and they in yours." That is, he spells out the golden rule to mean "swapping places" in order to tell what we should do. Moses' command, "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself" also puts you in your neighbor's place, and he in yours.

Modern moral philosophy calls this the "reversibility" test, crucial in golden rule arguments generative of specific moral conclusions, or verdicts concerning right or wrong. Since philosophers talk that way, it looks as if there can be a truly virtuous love of neighbor without divine love in the heart. Before letting ourselves be culture-bound to that view, however, consider the extraordinary conclusions Edwards drew from "swapping places." In his sermon on "Charity suffereth long and is kind," Edwards commented as follows on Jesus' parable, Luke 10:25–37: "if the Samaritan was neighbor to the Jew, his enemy, so the Jew was neighbor to the Samaritan; because neighborhood is a mutual relation equally predicable of both those between whom there is such a

-- 24 --

relation."5 So Moses' "as thyself" entails love of alien or enemy neighbors no less than those who are friendly. In the application of this sermon, Edwards reformulates "neighborliness" (in terms of golden rule reversibility) in words that in their extremity must include freely doing good to enemies no less than to friends, strangers or the poor. "If others have a hearty good will to us," he wrote, "and there is a great deal of kindness, and they are ready to help us when we stand in need of help, and to that end are free to do for us, to labor for us, to give to us, to suffer for us, and help bear our burdens, and share with us in our calamities, are free and cheerful in it, and seem to be open-hearted and liberal in it, this we approve of when we are the objects; this we highly commend; ... we are not apt to think that they exceed their duty."6 While yet an enemy we would not think such beneficence exceeded the duties of neighborhood if we were the object helped. "Swapping places" requires that if I am hostile and an implacable enemy to someone, yet am in serious need of help, I ask myself whether I would approve or disapprove of aid kindly given me by my implacable enemy; and go now and do likewise to him.

Now that is truly extraordinary. Edwards assumes that people, whose only obvious "mutual relation equally predicable" is that of stranger, alien, venomous hostility, may also become sharers in an equally predicable mutual relation of neighborhood; and that then anyone can know quite well the right disposition and action due to a hateful enemy by imagining that we are in his place and he in ours when we see him suffering under great calamity. If we suffered similarly, we would not say he "exceeded his duty" by helping us. But the language of "duty" or obligation may be questioned here.

Not many of us are likely to believe that love of enemies (real enemies, not ideal or imaginary ones) is a duty. Indeed, it is reasonable to argue that the expression "the duties of charity" is a contradiction in terms. In the Roman Catholic tradition of ethics, "counsels of perfection" placed less strain on ordinary, reasonable people's conception of their bottom-line obligations. And in Lutheran and Reformed traditions, the first function of the example of Christ, the perfection of righteousness he taught, the "love one another as I have loved you" of John 13:34, was to cast people down in their own eyes so that they realize they have no health in them, and can do nothing but seek a Savior. Yet, as we have just seen, Edwards' shared neighborhood consists of moral roles, relations, and duties having extraordinarily elevated standards of bottomline

-- 25 --

expectations, which he tries to show are entirely reasonable. His demonstration consists in a use of the reversibility test, which ordinarily aims at attaining an unbiased derivation to others of the self's natural inclinations and preferences. On its face that reasoning fails to reach the moral dispositions and actions Edwards commends. The reverse of love of self is not naturally love of enemies. How are we to explain the remarkable meaning Edwards finds in the "as thyself of the Mosaic law? Does what he says really follow from a rational use of reversibility or golden rule arguments?

In his sermon on "seeketh not her own," Edwards wrestles with why Christ called "love one another as I have loved you" a new commandment in "rule and motive." This was a problem since he believed the love, the matter of the command, is the same as the old "as thyself"; the "substance" is the same but with "a new enforcement and light annexed," a "new rule and motive." What he says under these heads is all very puzzling. It explains nothing. No answer can be given to those who demand a "rational" explanation of how the rule of loving others as Christ has loved us only "more clearly" shows our obligation in respect to our neighbor. "In a further degree" hardly describes the remarkable meanings Edwards draws from the "swapping places" that Moses and modern moral philosophy command.7 Reason alone teaches no such thing to be the meaning of virtuous benevolence.

Typology is the key to understanding Edwards on the sum of the moral law, Old and New. Typology, or figural interpretation, was the way Puritan exegetes held on to the narrative unity and truth of the whole of Scripture, without either fundamentalist literalism or medieval allegorization. The story of Jonah, for example, was a marvelous type; its real but veiled meaning was unveiled and made manifest in the antitype, the entombment of Jesus Christ— who, risen from the dead, also reveals in the Great Commission (Mark 16:15) the true meaning and scope of preaching God's forgiveness to the Ninevites. The blessed land of Canaan, passing over Jordan into the promised land, Zion, Jerusalem were all remarkable types whose meaning will be fully manifest only in the future the New Testament promises God's people.

In like manner, the old commandment contains the new under a veil; the new is the old unveiled and made clear. Love of neighbor "as thyself" is a typical adumbration of the antitypical love to one another "as I have loved you." Therefore, I suggest, "swapping places" (with the remarkable meaning Edwards finds in that interchange) is a type

-- 26 --

whereby we substitute ourselves in the stead of others as Jesus Christ stood in ours. Or rather, substituting Jesus Christ in the stead of others (when he was in prison, etc. [Matthew 25:35–40], when he went down from Jerusalem to Jericho or up to Golgotha) "derives to others" our own position when we were dead by the roadside and the Good Samaritan freely shared with us in our own calamities. We are not apt then to think excessive duty was done them. I can conceive of no other grounds than these for deriving to myself from my implacable enemy succor for me when he catches me in distress, as the standard for what I his implacable enemy should be and do towards him in disposition and in action in his calamities. Of course, there is the example of David who, after Saul became his "enemy continually," yet spared Saul's life when Saul was resting in a cave from pursuing him. But in this and other respects David was for Edwards a type of Christ.8 Thus the gospel meaning of "pure benevolence" was contained in the old rule, "as thyself"; and thus only is it discoverable by reversibility or golden rule arguments.

Given Puritan typology, it should surprise no one that Christ substituting himself for us gives the meaning to golden rule arguments; that the doctrine that Christ looked on our happiness as his own, placed his happiness in ours, placed his glory in our glorification with him gives the meaning of pure benevolence to our neighbor; or that looking on others' happiness as our own, placing or finding our own happiness in theirs is the manifest meaning of the "as thyself." For the matter of the old law, the meaning of swapping places, types out Jesus' taking our place. Truly virtuous love to neighbor, if there is any such goodwill in human hearts, must be love "so great as to be justly looked upon as a thorough union with them"; so great as the friendship of Christ for his people, which was a love "justly looked upon as making himself one with them, as is thoroughly assuming them into union with himself."9 John 17:21–22 is the manifest, fully unveiled meaning of goodwill among men.

By this measure, we who do not reveal ourselves to one another without dissimulation and are not united in affection can hardly have virtuous and holy goodwill among us. It begins to look as if no one in this life has a native capacity for the first act of truly virtuous benevolence to our neighbors— as was said of love to God. A perfect conjunction of love of our own happiness consisting in their happiness must be the effect (and

-- 27 --

not the cause, nor we the cause) of our first being united with them.1 There must be an assumption of their happiness into the will and love of our own happiness, as Christ assumed our own nature into his. Mostly, love of our happiness and of theirs runs on two tracks; or sometimes so, sometimes not; sometimes converging, sometimes diverging. Our wills and loves to happiness are compatibly similar— homoiousios, I am tempted to say. Still our own happiness does not consist in theirs. There remains an iota between us; my happiness is not homoousios with theirs, placed in theirs by our first being united in perfect affection.

In the Christian life, according to Edwards, the old creature has passed away, yet not perfectly; all things are made new, yet none perfectly.

Consent to Being

The second dissertation analyzes the topic announced by its title only in the first two chapters. The first of these uses the metaphysical language of "being"; the second translates that into theological language. Decisive for understanding Edwards' ethics is, first of all, the way in which one "unpacks" the extraordinarily compressed exposition of chapter one. Edwards begins with preliminary definitions and initial steps designed to bring his readers into agreement with him concerning the likely location of true virtue. These opening pages, written in nontechnical language, extend down to Edwards' introduction of his distinction between the primary (first) object and the secondary object of virtuous benevolence (propensity) of heart. At that point Edwards also introduces his contrived "term of art"— "‘absolute Benevolence’ (if I may so call it)"— and here rigorous analysis and argument begins.2 The paragraphs immediately preceding this point serve only as a ground-clearing elimination of both love of complacence and love of benevolence, as ordinarily understood, from eligibility to be crowned true virtue.

These introductory pages also include the statement, "True virtue most essentially consists in benevolence to Being in general." But that is not Edwards' true starting point. For he immediately signals: "Or perhaps to speak more accurately, it is that consent, propensity and union of heart to Being in general, that is immediately exercised in a general good

-- 28 --

will."3 That "more accurate speaking" is put fully to work in his later exact definition of terms as he means them, and his later complex logical argument. This, I suggest, serves to qualify his use of the shorthand expression "general benevolence" throughout the dissertation (and his introductory distinction between general and particular beauty, which is preliminary in any case).

Edwards has signaled another thing in these earlier pages. He tightens the circle around the sort of beauty and excellency that everyone will agree true virtue consists in to include only the moral beauty of the "heart" (the disposition and will) of mankind. Having thus stipulated that only intelligent willing beings are "capable subjects" of true virtue, Edwards then announces that by "Being in general" he thereby means "intelligent Being in general."4 The reader should note this description of the only capable object of true benevolence, for it prepares the ground for the second chapter's translation of the first into theological language. It also anticipates Edwards' logical argument in this first chapter, which begins, not with general benevolence, but with "consent, propensity and union" of the heart of a capable subject to the only capable object of true virtue.

Edwards' move is from the particular ("beings' uniting love to being") to the general, not the other way round. Consider the relationship between his "first" and "second" objects of virtuous benevolence. First, there is benevolence to Being. Second, benevolence to benevolent Being. In the course of explaining this, a certain priority, even a causal priority, is ascribed to Being— Being itself, Being simply considered, or Being itself, simply considered— over Being in general as the capable object of consent, propensity and union of heart. The difference between the first cluster of expressions and "Being in general" must have some significance. In asking what that significance may be, consider the sequence in the two following, crucial elaborations in Ch. I: (1) "propensity and union of heart to Being simply considered; exciting ‘absolute Benevolence’ (if I may so call it) to Being in general"; and (2) "pure benevolence

-- 29 --

in its first exercise is nothing else but being's uniting,5 consent, or propensity to Being; appearing true and pure by its extending to Being in general, and inclining to the general highest good, and to each being, whose welfare is consistent with the highest general good."6 Edwards' basic premise is the definition he gives to a truly virtuous relation of being to Being. From that he moves to generality. Precisely the same sequence he expresses in terms of "implication" in Ch. VI, where he tests instinctual affections (and finds them wanting, whether exercised to

-- 30 --

some only of our fellow creatures or "towards men in general") by "a principle of virtue" which has been "proved to be union of heart to Being simply considered; which implies a disposition to benevolence to Being in general."7 This reading of Edwards is confirmed, it seems to me, by the interchangeability of "beings" and "persons" in his discussion in Ch. VII of the remarkable and misleading resemblance between a truly virtuous love and love that has nothing of "pure" benevolence in it.

The movement from the first to the second object of virtuous benevolence (and the logic of most of the numbered paragraphs in Ch. I) is at least closely coordinated with the movement from love to Being simply considered to love to Being in general. Edwards comes last to "general beauty" or what is "beautiful in a comprehensive view as it is in itself, and as related to everything that it stands in connection with,"8 adumbrated but not proved in his introductory pages.

We may ask, How does one get to such general beauty or general excellency? The great stride toward extension or universalization is taken by moving to the second object of virtuous love, which is benevolence to benevolent being. In this secondary context, it is fitting to speak of "general benevolence." Such general benevolence is now fully demonstrated to mean the heart's relish and cordial union with the supremely benevolent supreme Being. In context with this second object, benevolent Being itself (Edwards' appropriate metaphysical referent to God) and "Being in general" are interchangeable. "Pure" or "absolute" benevolence to Being in general concisely encapsulates the complex "proportion compounded of the greatness of the benevolent being, or the degree of being and the degree of benevolence,"9 which is Edwards' criterion or test for love's rectitude. In relation to the greatest Being's benevolence to all being, and only in propensity to union with him, is the soul "related to everything it stands connected with" or does it become the capable

-- 31 --

subject of moral beauty in the most comprehensive view of it. Norman Fiering is exactly right when he says that "Edwards meant by ‘being in general’ the transcendent God plus His ordered creation."1 But that was the conclusion of his argument in chapter one, not an axiom with which to begin. In the movement of his thought, Edwards comes last to "Being in general" and to full warrant for the use of "general benevolence" to abbreviate his more exact definition of terms.

Chapter II of True Virtue translates the foregoing metaphysical language into biblical theological language. The reference to "created beings" in its title, and the expression in its first sentence of love to "the Being of beings," make it clear that "Being in general" does not point simply to all-encompassing being, nor does virtuous love to God direct us to a Whole of parts that are lesser wholes, etc., down to particular loves. Readers who have followed my interpretation so far will not be surprised to find that Edwards anchors the evidentiality of substituting "love to God" for true, absolute, or general benevolence in both the "first objective ground" (benevolence to Being) and the "secondary objective ground" (benevolence to benevolent Being). I need only draw attention here to the biblical character of Edwards' love to God (his glory in human understanding, his fullness in our "esteem" and our joys) and of his "benevolence to created beings" (loving for them with them their knowledge of God, and their loving union and joy in him). The following is a typical paragraph:

By these things it appears that a truly virtuous mind, being as it were under the sovereign dominion of love to God, does above all things seek the glory of God, and makes this his supreme, governing, and ultimate end: consisting in the expression of God's perfections in their proper effects, and in the manifestation of God's glory to created understandings, and the communications of the infinite fullness of God to the creature; in the creature's highest esteem of God, love to God, and joy in God, and in the proper exercises and expressions of these. And so far as a virtuous mind exercises true virtue in benevolence to created beings, it chiefly seeks the good of the creature, consisting in its knowledge or view of God's glory and beauty, its union with God, and conformity to him, love to him, and joy in him. And that temper or disposition of heart, that consent, union, or propensity of mind to Being in general, which appears chiefly in such exercises, is virtue, truly so called; or in other words, true grace and

-- 32 --

real holiness. And no other disposition or affection but this is of the nature of true virtue.2

That was Edwards' rendering of the two love commandments. At least, it will seem so to a reader not bemused by the metaphysical language of being, and not driven to try to prove that, shortly before his death, America's greatest theological mind loosened his bonds to biblical ethics when writing on true virtue. That is the grave mistake fostered by separating the second from the first dissertation, where are to be found Edwards' scriptural evidences and theological arguments. Still, an implied contrast between metaphysical and biblical theological language entails no necessary conflict for Edwards. Before the modern period, classical Christian theology did not play these options against one another. Theological language was not set over against metaphysical language, nor biblical against theological.

Some interesting similarities and differences may, in conclusion, be noted between Edwards and, for example, the theological language and analysis of Thomas Aquinas. In Aquinas ipsum esse functions as a reference or name for God, for all practical purposes. This is Edwards' "Being itself." Both theologians orient the human being upon the summum ens (supreme being) or ens primum, and not only upon a summum bonum (highest good). Interpreters of Edwards can hardly demonstrate that the "new sense of the heart"— his "voluntarism"— excluded conviction of the truth and reality of divine things; or even that the heart leads the head. Yet the traditional telos of the intellect to being and of the will to good is not exactly replicated. In his thought, greater being (an archangel in comparison with a flea) has greater worth, even before consulting the "secondary object," the consenting or the benevolence to being on the part of that greater or lesser being. This seems rather like saying that the summum ens is worthy to be, and to be loved, before consulting bonum as one of the "transcendental" attributes of ipsum esse (Being itself). Entity itself is worthy of being. In this, Edwards distinguishes more than Aquinas did between greatness and goodness, even in holding them together in God, and in depicting the restored image of God in the virtuous love of the saints. Therefore, in devising his philosophical equivalent of a principle of divine love in the heart going out towards God and towards neighbor,3 Edwards found it necessary to define true virtue (or "primary" beauty) as consent, propensity, and

-- 33 --

union of heart proportionate to the degree of greatness of the being loved in combination with the degree of benevolence or goodness of that being. Finally, "Being simply considered" may introduce a divergence: Aquinas' ens simpliciter (being wherever found) is always used in contrast to secundum quid (modality of being). However, the fact that Aquinas never uses ens simpliciter (Edwards' "Being simply considered"?) interchangeably with summum ens or ipsum esse should not detract too greatly from the degree of similarity between these philosophical theologies.

The Splendor of Common Morality

It is a mistake to treat Edwards' moral philosophy apart from his moral theology, his theological ethics. That would be to separate the merely distinguishable. It is a mistake also to separate the four principles that comprise his account of common or natural morality. These elements, sources, or principia are (1) our sense of the secondary beauty in moral dispositions and relations, (2) self-love extended by association of ideas, (3) natural conscience, and (4) instinctual kind affections: pity and familial affection. Yet another mistake would be to reduce these principles to one only, e.g., self-love understood to be utterly nefarious; and then to understand the other principles as simply and overwhelmingly subject to the inroads of nefarious self-love.

Edwards' well-known thesis, of course, is that there is nothing of the nature of true virtue in the morality arising from these four principles; such natural dispositions are "entirely diverse" from truly virtuous goodwill. Still he is equally concerned to show that, working together or separately, these four principles adequately account for a common morality which, both in its nature and its effects, greatly "resembles" and is "agreeable" to true virtue and readily mistaken for it.

So for Edwards ordinary morality was a rather splendid thing. Here we need not and should not appeal to the grace or the gifts of the Holy Spirit that are "common" to the godly and ungodly alike: common illuminations, common awakenings, common inspiration, common convictions, common religious experience, common humiliations, etc. These restraints or aids accompany the redemption God is working in every age from the Fall to the end of the world. Edwards was not one to use prevenient grace, restraining grace, or common grace to account for the natural moral competence of fallen humanity, and to avoid the belief that we good people are only "miserable sinners" and not totally deprived of true virtue.

-- 34 --

True Virtue should first be read as Edwards' ethics of creation. Indeed, if anyone wants to know the meaning of the "natural image" of God in intelligent willing creatures, in distinction from the lost and to-be-restored "spiritual image" of God, I know no better place to send the questioner than to the second dissertation. While these are "entirely diverse" and there is nothing of the nature of the spiritual image in the natural, God has so made our "frames" that we instinctually, silently, insensibly are moved— our dispositions and actions are directed and shaped— by those four principles. Christianity is not built on the ruin of reason, nor on the ruin of morality within the competence of ungraced moral agents.

In addition to principles planted in our natures from creation, God's "laws of nature" or his "moral constitutions" complete Edwards' account of common morality. These also are components of an ethics of creation. A law of nature refers to God's governance of the creation, its moral part no less than what we today call "nature." Laws governing physical nature or intelligent moral beings are immediate arbitrary constitutions. "When I call this an arbitrary constitution," Edwards wrote, "I mean that it depends on nothing but the divine will; which divine will depends on nothing but the divine wisdom."4 In the moral order or in the physical order, a "new effect is consequent on the former, only by the established laws, and settled course of nature: which is allowed to be nothing but the continued immediate efficiency of God, according to a constitution that he has been pleased to establish." So "God's preserving created things in being is perfectly equivalent to a continued creation, or to his creating those things out of nothing at each moment of their existence."5 As the world is not a machine set going by a perfect watchmaker, neither is it a system of orbiting and moving creatures whose designed courses the Space Director must constantly correct, thus "preserving" planets in being, preventing collisions or cosmic grid-lock. "So that truly the giving the world a being at the first no more differs from preserving it through all successive moments, than the giving the last moment differs from the giving a supported being this moment."6 We may also say that the giving the moral world a being this moment, lawfully and beautifully connected with a previous moment, differs in no significant respect from giving the moral world a being the first moment. The creation of an intelligent willing being the first moment was

-- 35 --

not without the moral world that God, in his wisdom, was pleased to create and continues to constitute. So Edwards' natural laws and moral constitutions are ordinances of creation. No reference need yet be made to God's providential rule and overruling, since the immediate telos of general or special providence is the redemption, as is expressly the case with prevenient or restraining grace "common" to the godly and the ungodly alike.

Edwards' radical doctrine of creation-every-moment needs to be brought to mind whenever one is attempting to understand his view of principles and moral constitutions, whether "natural" or "divine." Thus, apart from God's laws of nature, the beautiful harmonies of sight and sound, the consent of minds, fitting moral relations of any sort, the virtues of the pagans or of the regenerate would have no continuity or duration or any being at all. With respect to the four principles of common morality, a moral order by God's immediate "arbitrary" constitution figures prominently in Edwards' account of secondary beauty in insuring that the effects of self-love are an extensive and rather admirable love for others and in instinctual kind affections (which serve not only to preserve the world but also to give us pleasure and comfort on the earth). Without God's laws of nature, there would be no common notions of desert or justice from our sense of the secondary beauty in fit or proportionate moral relations, or in conscience. Self-love would incurve on itself at once, with no propensity to include other-love, nor any sort of consent to another. Calamities would have destroyed all human creatures long since (without pity) and men and women, parents and children, would have no continuance at all of any affection or comfort on the earth.

SECONDARY BEAUTY. Edwards observed beauty in the symmetry of buildings, in melodies, in the infinite number of equalities in a rose, a countenance, or the solar system. But this inferior sort of beauty or excellency was also in immaterial things. An orderly working together in society is beautiful. So is wisdom "consisting in the united tendency of thoughts... to one general purpose." An adaptedness of things one to another— in, e.g., the proper returns of love— is the beauty of natural justice. There is also "a becoming gratitude."7

"The cause why secondary beauty is grateful to men is only a law of nature, which God has fixed, or an instinct he has given to mankind."8 He

-- 36 --

made the answerableness in the natural world and in our common moral world beautiful because therein is "some image of the true, spiritual original beauty," consisting in "the union of minds or spiritual beings in a mutual propensity and affection of heart... And it pleases God to observe analogy in his works... and especially to establish inferior things in an analogy to superior."9 The whole creation (natural and moral), and this first element of ordinary morality, is an analogy of "cordial union" with God.

By using the term analogy Edwards does not suggest ascent from a taste for well-known inferior beauty to a taste for primary beauty or excellency. Comparison with Barth's analogy of faith or analogy of relationship may not be entirely apt, but certainly no comparison is to be made with the analogy of being. For when Edwards speculates why God was pleased to observe analogy in all his works, he thinks of no influence of secondary beauty toward bringing about true spiritual beauty in human hearts or moral relations. Nevertheless, the image— secondary beauty, whether material or immaterial— has "a tendency to assist those whose hearts are [already] under the influence of a truly virtuous temper, to dispose them to the exercises of divine love, and enliven in them a sense of spiritual beauty."1 Cordial consent or pure love must first be in order to be enlivened by the beauties abroad in the natural and moral worlds. Very much this same relation of asymmetrical, dependent reciprocity is again expressed by Edwards when he briefly brings morality from divine principles and from natural principles together at the end of Ch. VII, in a sentence that calls to mind the "instinctual kind affections": "a principle of general benevolence softens and sweetens the mind, and makes it more susceptible of the proper influence and exercise of the gentler natural instincts."2

The extent and the kind of duties to which our sense of secondary beauty inclines are quite remarkable. This inferior sort of beauty is the ground of "most of the duties incumbent on us" in justice, the "duties of friendship and good neighborhood," and "all duties whatsoever, considered as required by God, and as branches of our duty to him." So "religion" was for Edwards, as for Aquinas and many others, a species of justice. And finally, since true religion has been made known, the answerableness of acts to their occasion, or rendering God his due, also encompasses all duties "considered as what are to be performed with

-- 37 --

a regard to Christ, as acts of obedience to his precepts, and as testimonies of respect to him, and of our regard to what he has done for us, the virtues and temper of mind he has exercised towards us, and the benefits we have or hope for therefrom."3

But the goodness of these beauties, even the "becoming" beauty of gratitude, is entirely "diverse" from the beauty of holiness, and there is nothing of the nature of true virtue in them. Men may be disposed to and capable of "great performances" and "great sufferings"4 in which there is an inherent moral beauty, but this is only secondary if these dispositions and performances are not from a propensity to unition of heart with God and relish for the primary beauty of holiness. A taste for love to God is as different from a taste for the beauty of justice to God as it is from a taste for harmony, honey, or the smell of a rose.

This brings us to the proof Edwards offers for this diversity— an argument used in the case of each of the four cooperating principles of common morality. If primary and secondary beauty were not diverse, "men's sense of secondary beauty would increase in proportion to their virtue," and truly virtuous love would increase in proportion to men's capacity for secondary beauty. But experience teaches that spiritual beauty and secondary beauty are independent variables. A good ear for music, a good eye for painting or for architecture, appreciation of beautiful systems in physics or philosophy, eligibility for the Metropolitan Opera Company, good citizenship, virtuosity in what we (not Edwards) call golden rule morality, the administration of justice or the harmonious arrangement of political society evidently do not vary directly with true virtue.

Edwards sometimes uses a stronger form of this argument, which gives instances of the entire separation of apparent from true virtue. Serpents can be charmed by the beauty of a tune! Whatever we may think of classing secondary beauty in moral matters with the beauty of melodies to serpents, I see no reason to deny that, for example, beneficence may be inherently a very beautiful thing, fitting real benefits to real occasions, even without true benevolence.

SELF-LOVE. By "association of ideas... vastly heightened by education,"5 self-love is a principle or spring of disposition and action productive of an extensive love to others. A genuine "love to some others [and to many others] may truly be the effect of self-love." Self-love, of

-- 38 --

course, has no power by itself to do this. Still, anyone experienced in loving himself well will have a sort of other-love. Such an association of the idea of others' good with our own is because of a law of nature God has fixed. There is no "metaphysical necessity" that from regard to our own interest we should love those that love us; the contrary would imply "no contradiction."6 It could be that— however we exert ourselves to promote our own interest by promoting the interest of some others— we could never do so that way. It could be that self-love spins only on the spot, that each and every moral agent remains alone, with no mutual benefits flowing from other-love and no sense that this is so, that love to self and of private interest are of the self sole, that the self's loves are becalmed at sea, governed by no moral laws and therefore associated with no others, going out to none. Just so, the gravity that draws parts of our globe toward its center might be different from the gravity that draws our globe to other planets. In both instances, we have to look to see. And when we look to see, we see that self-love (even censurable selfishness) is very often commonly and best gratified by love to others. God's moral constitution sustains and bends purely private personal self-love to extension into larger communities of common interest. No one rightly reads Edwards if he does not understand that for this, too, the Creator is to be praised.

We not only learn to love other specific persons (and common causes) from love of their love to us, which is a love derived to them from an experienced love to self. In the course of individual moral development we also learn to love certain "qualities and characters" abstracted from the particular persons who originally taught our love to self to love those characteristics in others. It takes no "great degree of metaphysical refining" to see that this is the reason men approve of others they hear of at a distance, or read of in history, or in a romance, or see represented on the stage from whom they expect no profit or advantage; and why they

-- 39 --

disapprove of other moral characters from which they expect no injury. Our initial approval of the "character" was because from experience "its nature and tendency falls in with the nature and tendency of self-love." So from association of ideas first imprinted and fixed in the mind by experienced deadly danger or experienced pleasure, a child learns to respond to the picture of a snake, painted fruit, or the figure of a bird, without any expectation of being bitten, or tasting or hearing these representations.7

This approval of "qualities and characters" extends to "meekness, peaceableness, benevolence, charity, generosity, justice, and the social virtues in general." Edwards goes so far as to say that from love to self can be derived by fixed association of ideas a sort of love of enemies, if in their "quality and character" we discern a stoutness or a high degree of strength to overcome obstacles which in our own moral development we learn to value in others who are friendly to us.8

Justice, or desert, are among those qualities. This virtue of persons and in moral relations, we saw, also springs from the secondary beauty of uniformity and proportion. Since one of these principles is never without the other, Edwards does not undertake to determine whether extended self-love or the secondary beauty of moral and social relations better accounts for justice in common morality. I judge that the excellency of symmetry in moral relations exerts the stronger influence. But Edwards does not decide this question. Instead, he compounds the effects of these two first principles in yet another "association of ideas." Our "approbation of justice and desert, arising from a sense of the beauty of natural agreement and proportion" strengthens by "a kind of reflex... influence" men's claims to justice and desert, and concordant exercises of their own agency, from self-love. Association of ideas brings the first two sources of common morality together. So "some vices may become in a degree odious by the influence of self-love, through an habitual connection of ideas of contempt with it; contempt being what self-love abhors." Concurrently, the same idea of odiousness attaches to qualities and characters because of their incongruity or natural ugliness or deformity— the abstracted contrary of secondary beauty in moral matters and relations of which we have a sense to disapprove.9

Thus anger and gratitude are justly deserved manifestations of self-love "when others' hearts oppose us, and they from their hearts do us

-- 40 --

hurt" or "when they love us, and do us good." At the same time, gratitude flows from our "sense of a beauty in moral things," which Edwards calls "a kind of moral sense"1 (reserving this designation, unqualified, for conscience); and resentment from our sense of incongruity in moral matters. There is a "natural agreement, proportion, and adjustment" between others' dispositions and actions and, respectively, their suffering "in like manner as we have suffered" or our gratefully loving them and doing them good. Two different pairs of things are here joined together in a morally experienced self-love: (1) self-love and fixed attitudes toward "qualities and characters" of persons, and (2) self-love and "a kind of reflex and indirect influence" of "a sense of the beauty of natural agreement and proportion." Either or both together cause men to approve justice or desert; and as well, to approve ordinary benevolence and disapprove malice.2

To this he adds a final explanation: the "almost irrefragable association, in innumerable instances, of ideas which have no connection any other way than by education... greatly strengthening that association, or connection, which persons are led into by other means."3 That is, education transmits the moral notions men were led to, even after the strong, fixed connection that was experienced has been forgotten. Children are morally educated in the virtues in a tradition of ordinary morality, who no way have experienced their connection with self-love. A tradition commending virtues that originally arose from morally experienced self-love, with its esteem of qualities and characters from association, will continue in moral education long after memory of that connection is lost. This is precisely the "lapsed link" theory used by British utilitarians to explain why people persist in the belief that virtue and vice— and judgments about right and wrong actions— are not to be explained by their usefulness in obtaining beneficial and avoiding evil consequences.4

-- 41 --

Finally, Edwards gives two examples to show that gratitude and anger arising from self-love do not vary directly with public benevolence. These are independent variables. The case of the conspiratorial crew and the gang of robbers provides evidence that there is no virtuous benevolence in the love to others, with its becoming gratitude and anger, that may be derived to them from self-love. Nevertheless, love to self as a source of moral disposition can account for gratitude and angers that are fitting and those that are not; and that is important, morally, without true virtue.5

CONSCIENCE. The element in Edwards' account of conscience that is original with True Virtue reaches back to pick up a theme in Edwards' first Miscellany on the Trinity. This entry argues that, since any idea "perfectly like a thing, is that thing" and "wants nothing that is in the thing, substance nor nothing else," the Son, who is the "express and

-- 42 --

perfect image of God, is God's idea of his own essence." There is "some image" of this in human self-knowledge. Thus, Edwards continued:

Man is as it were two, as some of the great wits of this age have observed: a sort of genius is with a man, that accompanies him and attends him wherever he goes; so that a man has a conversation with himself, that is, he has a conversation with his own idea. So that if his idea be excellent, he will take delight and happiness in conferring and communing with it; he takes complacency in himself, he applauds himself; and wicked men accuse themselves and fight with themselves as if they were two. And man is truly happy then, and only then, when these two agree, and they delight in themselves, and in their own idea and image, as God delights in his.6

In like manner, in True Virtue conscience consists in "a disposition in man to be uneasy in a consciousness of being inconsistent with himself and, as it were, against himself in his own actions." Ease of conscience is consistency with self: integrity. There is a sort of "self-love, or self-union"7 that is with man, that accompanies him wherever he goes. The reflex action of conscience is at least one element in what Edwards means by intelligent willing creatures being in the "natural image" of God. In conscience there is some image of the primary beauty of both the paradigm union and consent between the Father and the Son, and of truly virtuous consent to "persons and beings."

Immediately after introducing the notion of self-union, Edwards writes that disturbance of conscience "appears particularly in the inclination of the mind to be uneasy in the conscience of doing to others what he should be angry with them for doing to him, if they were in his case, and he in theirs." Thus common morality so far as it arises from conscience encompasses what we today call golden rule arguments, or the reversibility or "swapping places" test for whether the action we are contemplating is moral or immoral. This is the constant element in Edwards' many notes on natural conscience written over a lifetime. The new stress in True Virtue is on the contradiction within the self, which disapproves when one is "conscious to oneself"8 of having done as one would not be done by, and which arises spontaneously, insensibly, etc., in the mind. The spontaneous reflex of ease or dis-ease may be the reason Edwards is willing to have his understanding of conscience called the

-- 43 --

"moral sense." The reader should recall, however, that a sense of secondary beauty also spontaneously registers the harmony or proportion of answerable actions and moral relations, without our having any knowledge of nature's laws. In any case, conscience is not an intuition. It is not Francis Hutcheson's "moral sense" that registers approbation upon an apprehension of benevolence wherever it may be found, in oneself or in others, similar in manner of operation to the other senses we have of the world. For Edwards, conscience is, itself, a relation. It is golden rule morality writ small in the self's consciousness to self.

To do that to another which we should be angry with him for doing to us... if we were exactly in the same case, is to disagree with ourselves, and contradict ourselves. It would be for ourselves both to choose and adhere to, and yet to refuse and utterly reject, as it were, the very same thing. No wonder this is contrary to nature... and inward war with a man's self.9

So in some sense conscience prescribes reversibility (not, in the first place, to make universal). If an agent does not abide by this, if his disposition and action go against natural integrity, an uneasiness of conscience warns him that wrong has been done, in that he claims such and such disposition or action to be moral and yet would not prescribe that it be directed by others toward himself in similar circumstances. This is a practical contradiction— disunion with self— in a moral agent since he is by nature "conscious to" his own will and action. Edwards believed that "unless men's consciences are greatly stupefied" such golden rule approvals and disapprovals are "naturally and necessarily suggested" and "habitually, spontaneously, instantaneously, and as it were insensibly arise in the mind"— no need to read contemporary moral philosophy. Any person "easily and quietly sees" whether he, being in another's place, should "approve or condemn, be angry or pleased as he is."

So far we have ascribed to conscience only the formal capacity for swapping places to distinguish right from wrong. This formal test of self-consistency, by itself, would be empty. Indeed, it could mandate evil-doing, if natural conscience has no other foundation of its rectitude. We still need to ask, therefore, concerning the material content an agent should prescribe when he puts himself in the place of another. Our sense of secondary beauty informs conscience concerning the excellency of justice or desert; these material judgments, indeed, "must precede" formal self-consistency. "There is another thing that must precede," Edwards

-- 44 --

writes, "and be the foundation" of the "uneasiness" of conscience which he explored."1

If my reading is correct, Edwards held that natural morality was a rather splendid thing— though there is no true virtue in it. He ascribed to natural conscience the capacity to "swap places" even with God and to determine what is justly his due. If persons have any idea of divine things, they have a conscience which will excite within them "resentments which would be occasioned in themselves by an injurious treatment in any wise parallel" to their own failure to give God his due.2

Because of the concurrence of "self-union" (and all that means) with a taste for secondary beauty and excellence in moral matters (and all that means in material moral judgments that are fit, amiable and worthy) the power of conscience seems unlimited. So Edwards affirms:

Approbation and disapprobation of conscience, in the sense now explained, will extend to all virtue and vice; to everything whatsoever that is morally good or evil, in a mind which does not confine its view to a private sphere, but will take things in general into its consideration, and is free from speculative error... Thus natural conscience, if the understanding be properly enlightened, and errors and blinding stupefying prejudices are removed, concurs with the law of God, and is of equal extent with it, and joins its voice with it in every article.

And thus, in particular, we may see in what respect this natural conscience that has been described, extends to true virtue, consisting in union of heart to Being in general, and supreme love to God. For,

-- 45 --

although it sees not, or rather does not taste its primary and essential beauty, i.e. it tastes no sweetness in benevolence to Being in general, simply considered, or loves it not for Being in general's sake (for nothing but general benevolence itself can do that), yet this natural conscience, common to mankind, may approve of it from that uniformity, equality and justice which there is in it... Men by natural conscience may see the justice (or natural agreement) there is in yielding all to God, as we receive all from God; and the justice there is in being his that has made us, and being willingly so... and also the justice there is in our supreme love to God, from his goodness— the natural agreement there is between our having supreme respect to him who exercises infinite goodness to us, and from whom we receive all well-being.3

Nevertheless, to know the justice of duties to God and Christ, and to perform some or any of them for conscience's sake, is evidently not the same thing as to know and do his will from love and propensity of heart to union with him.

The foregoing passages carry the mind back to End of Creation, the "former treatise," where the same moral reasoning was used. To show that "reason teaches," no less than Scripture, that the end God had in creating any world at all was for his glory, praise, and name's sake, Edwards wrote:

When we are considering with ourselves, what would be most fit and proper for God to have a chief respect to, in his proceedings in general, with regard to the universality of things, it may help us to judge of the matter with the greater ease and satisfaction to consider what we can suppose would be judged and determined by some third being of perfect wisdom and rectitude, neither the Creator nor one of the creatures, that should be perfectly indifferent and disinterested. Or if we make the supposition that wisdom itself, or infinitely wise justice and rectitude were a distinct disinterested person... now such a judge... would weigh things in an even balance; taking care that greater, or more existence should have a greater share than less, that a greater part of the whole should be more looked at and respected than the lesser in proportion (other things being equal) to the measure of existence, that the more excellent should be more regarded than the less excellent: so that the degree of regard should always be in a proportion compounded of the proportion of existence

-- 46 --

and proportion of excellence, or according to the degree of greatness and goodness considered conjunctly... To determine, then, what proportion of regard is to be allotted to the Creator, and all his creatures taken together, both must be as it were put in the balance; the Supreme Being, with all in him that is great, considerable, and excellent, is to be estimated and compared with all that is to be found in the whole creation: and according as the former is found to outweigh, in such proportion is he to have a greater share of regard. And in this case, as the whole system of created beings in comparison of the Creator would be found as the light dust of the balance (which is taken no notice of by him that weighs) and as nothing and vanity; so the arbiter must determine accordingly with respect to the degree in which God should be regarded by all intelligent existence, and the degree in which he should be regarded in all that is done through the whole universal system... And as the Creator is infinite, and has all possible existence, perfection and excellence, so he must have all possible regard. As he is every way the first and supreme, and as his excellency is in all respects the supreme beauty and glory, the original good, and fountain of all good; so he must have in all respects the supreme regard...

Thus we must conclude such an arbiter as I have supposed would determine in this business, being about to decide how matters should proceed most fitly, properly, and according to the nature of things. He would therefore determine that the whole universe, including all creatures animate and inanimate, in all its actings, proceedings, revolutions, and entire series of events, should proceed from a regard and with a view to God, as the supreme and last end of all...4

Thus I have gone upon the supposition of a third person, neither Creator nor creature, but a disinterested person stepping in to judge of the concerns of both, and state what is most fit and proper between them. The thing supposed is impossible; but the case is nevertheless just the same as to what is most fit and suitable in itself. For it is most certainly proper for God to act according to the greatest fitness, in his proceedings; and he knows what the greatest fitness is, as much as if perfect rectitude were a distinct person to direct him. As therefore there is no third being beside God and the created system, nor can be, so there is no need of any, seeing God himself is possessed of that

-- 47 --

perfect discernment and rectitude which have been supposed. It belongs to him as supreme arbiter, and to his infinite wisdom and rectitude, to state all rules and measures of proceedings... For being interested unfits a person to be an arbiter or judge, no otherwise than as interest tends to blind and mislead his judgment, or incline him to act contrary to it. But that God should be in danger of either is contrary to the supposition of his being possessed of discerning [discernment] and justice absolutely perfect... [I]t therefore belongs to God whose are all things, who is perfectly fit for this office, and who alone is so, to state all things according to the most perfect fitness and rectitude, as much as if perfect rectitude were a distinct person. We may therefore be sure it is and will be done.

I should think that these things might incline us to suppose that God has not forgot himself, in the ends which he proposed in the creation of the world; but that he has so stated these ends (however he is self-sufficient, immutable and independent) as therein plainly to show a supreme regard to himself. Whether this can be, or whether God has done thus, must be considered afterwards, as also what may be objected against this view of things.5

Edwards' stunning use of an independent arbiter to show God himself to be the supremely worthy and fit end in creation may be compared with the "ideal observer" theory of ethics proposed in the twentieth century.6

This brings us to Edwards' standard argument: that there is no true virtue in conscientious morality. The weaker form of this argument is that these two moralities are distinct; even when together, they are independent variables. Not all approbations and condemnations of conscience, experience teaches, are in proportion to virtuous temper of mind.7

However, the stronger form of the argument is the more striking. The "greatest reason" Christians have for believing that true virtue is not the same as that which conscience dictates is that they believe there will be a day and a state of affairs in which the two are entirely separate. Then and there, no "thought experiment" or search for supposable examples of robber bands will be required to tell the difference.

Christians have the greatest reason to believe from the Scriptures that in the future day of the revelation of the righteous judgment of God, when sinners shall be called to answer before their Judge, and all their wickedness, in all its aggravations, brought forth, and clearly manifested in the perfect light of that day; and God will reprove them, and set their sins in order before them; their consciences will be greatly awakened and convinced, their mouths will be stopped, all stupidity of conscience will be at an end, and conscience will have its full exercise... And this, according to the notion I am opposing, would be the same thing as then being brought to the fullest repentance; their hearts being perfectly changed to hate sin and love holiness; and virtue or holiness of heart in them will be brought to the most full and perfect exercise. But how much otherwise have we reason to suppose it will then be?... 'Tis proper for a judge when he condemns a criminal to endeavor so to set his guilt before him as to convince his conscience of the justice of the sentence. This the Almighty will do effectually, and do to perfection, so as most thoroughly to awaken and convince the conscience...

But supposing natural conscience to be what has been described, all these difficulties and absurdities are wholly avoided. Sinners, when they see the greatness of the Being, whom they have lived in

-- 49 --

contempt of, and in rebellion and opposition to; and have clearly set before them their obligations to him as their Creator, Preserver, Benefactor, etc. together with the degree in which they have acted as enemies to him, may have a clear sense of the desert of their sin, consisting in the natural agreement there is between such contempt and opposition of such a Being, and his despising and opposing them; between their being and acting as so great enemies to such a God, and their suffering the dreadful consequences of his being and acting as their great enemy: and their being conscious within themselves of the degree of anger which would naturally arise in their own hearts in such a case, if they were in the place and state of their Judge. In order to these things there is no need of a virtuous benevolent temper, relishing and delighting in benevolence, and loathing the contrary. The conscience may see the natural agreement between opposing and being opposed, between hating and being hated, without abhorring malevolence from a benevolent temper of mind, or without loving God from a view of the beauty of his holiness. These things have no necessary dependence one on the other.8

There's nothing like the Day of Judgment for concentrating the mind!

Edwards' appeal to those to be condemned on the Day of Judgment was, for him, an appeal to real states and intelligences to clarify the nature of natural conscience in any intelligent willing creature when conscience alone is perfectly in exercise. In this world of trial, the conscience of saints is often dim. God's common grace often assists conscience to do its office more fully in the unregenerate. In this world of trial, saints and sinners alike may ask, Who? Me? Why me? (Matthew 25:31–46). But when perfect judgment is come, their deeds will be set in order before them (Psalms 50:21). The perfection of judgment requires both that all mankind be judged together and that the occasion be public for justice to be fully manifest. And finally, those sentenced must have no doubt about the justice of the sentence. If they still ask, Who? Me? Why me? the Judge has not perfectly judged them.9

It is a serious error to read the case of the Judgment Day as denying the worth of conscientious morality; or to say that in this time of trial conscience produces no fruits in justice done, only approbations; or to say that grace alone enables men to do what justice requires, i.e., conscience itself has no motivational power. We need to remember that in Edwards' example from Christian belief the present moral world has come to an end. God's moral constitution of it has come to an end. Presumably there is no more secondary beauty imaging the divine beauty, no more God's laws of nature sustaining the derivation of love to some and many others from love to self, no pity or familial kind affections (to which we next turn), and, I judge, no more of the fruits of conscience's golden rule morality in actual justice done and to be done, nor need for that. The point was simply to show decisively that in this life— as on that Day when men will have (in words from Miscell. no. 623)1 "an illumination of conscience far beyond any have in this world"— our conscience (which is "nothing else but a sense of the equality and answerableness" of God's judgment upon our sins) never disposes us to see "the hatefulness or deformity," the true ugliness from a sense of the heart, of sin in general, or of our sins in particular so as to dispose human hearts to do justice— or anything else— from pure benevolence to God.2 If this were so, the Day of Judgment would be a day of universal conversion to love to God!

INSTINCTUAL KIND AFFECTIONS. Since Edwards called our sense of secondary beauty in moral matters an "instinct" of nature, we need to pay careful attention to his definition of this fourth and final element in his account of common morality. "Instincts" are "inclinations natural to men... determining their minds to certain affections and actions towards particular objects." These instincts differ from our "instinctual" sense of beauty in their degree of specificity. But although instinctual kind affections are oriented toward particular persons or particular occasions, they (like the other principles) lead to admiration for or aversion to general qualities and characters. The last terse paragraph of this chapter states that "natural instinct, disposing men to pity others in misery," is also "a source of a kind of abhorrence in men of some vices, as cruelty and oppression; and so, of a sort of approbation of the contrary virtues, humanity, mercy, etc." Edwards' criticism of the virtues enkindled

-- 51 --

by natural pity is limited to "so far as they arise from this cause only."3 And the same word "kind" in this chapter and in 1 Corinthians 13:4 adumbrates a convergence of some sort between virtues infused with or by charity and virtues based on natural principles— to be discussed later. Edwards chooses to discuss two sorts of instinctual kind affections that move us to particular others, or to others under specific circumstances.

(1) Familial "kind affections" join together husband and wife, parents and children. Edwards expressly refuses to dispute with anyone who refers what we today call "special moral relations" (and he called "relative duties") to extended self-love. "In my opinion both [these accounts] are true," he writes, "viz. that natural affection is owing to natural instinct, and also that it arises from self-love." Again we see that Edwards' account of common morality is multifactoral. Between husband and wife "there is doubtless a disposition both to mutual benevolence and mutual complacence [esteem] that are not naturally or necessarily connected with any sensitive desires."4 Sexual attraction serves the preservation of mankind; so it seemed obvious to Edwards that in addition kind affections provide us comfort on earth in congenial community. My guess is that, of the familial loves mentioned, there is between husband and wife more complacence (pleased by the good) than benevolence; between parents and children, more benevolence (good-giving) than complacence; between children and parents, more complacence (or esteem) than benevolence. These are the loves familiar in moral discourse; and, again, the same-named loves (we shall see) also arise from absolute or pure benevolence to God. Relative duties and dispositions are also specifications of charity.

(2) There is a natural inclination to pity that is universally excited in all men on occasions proper to it. Pity is "uneasiness on the sight of others' extreme distress." When Edwards asks why the Creator implanted in us the kindly affection of pity, the reply is: "for the same purposes as most other instincts, viz. chiefly for the preservation of mankind, though not exclusive of their well-being."5 My guess is that he would have said that familial kind affections are implanted chiefly for well-being and comfort.

Edwards believed that experience shows a correlation of pity with the purpose he ascribes to it in the designs of a wise and merciful God. He has

-- 52 --

so made men that in cases of great calamity they rush to the aid of strangers, for it is then that men stand most in need of the help of others besides their near friends with whom they have closer ties of kind affection. Otherwise calamities would tend to men's destruction from the earth. From this we may see something of the reason God has so strangely endowed men with a natural sense of pity in the face of extreme calamity to persons indifferent, but not with an equal sense of rejoicing at the sight of others' great prosperity. This is "agreeable to the state that God designed mankind for here, where he intends their preservation, and comfortable subsistence."6

For preservation, pity and sexual instinct seem sufficient; for comfortable subsistence, familial kind affections. When we add to these final elements of God's moral constitution the sum of men's instinct for the secondary beauty of answerable moral relations in family, neighborhood, and civic friendship, the immediate and instantaneous operation of ease or dis-ease of conscience, and the fact that self-love is never exercised without God's ordering of it, there is no reason to ascribe to Edwards a bleak view of the life or the morality of mankind during this time of trial.

Nevertheless, his negative thesis still stands. There is nothing of true virtue in instinctual kind affections. The two are utterly diverse. In particular, familial affections arise from a quite different source and have no tendency to produce general benevolence. Affections and benevolence do not vary directly with each other.

With regard to pity Edwards uses the stronger form of his standard argument. Pity is shown to be entirely separable from true virtue, not merely distinguishable from it. Pity not only does not vary directly with goodwill; it can even consist with illwill. Therefore true benevolence must be a distinct thing, essentially different from pity— though Edwards has shown that pity produces approvals of "humanity, mercy, etc." and disapprovals of cruelty and oppression in general. For we feel pity toward persons in extreme anguish even though notice of their deaths would cause no great uneasiness. We are moved to pity brute creatures under extreme and long torments, but not— at least not to the same degree— by seeing them put out of all sensible pain or pleasure "at butchers' shambles in great cities."7 We pity persons in great pain whose positive pleasure we may be very indifferent to. We pity others under

-- 53 --

exquisite torment whose prosperity excites in us the "comparative spirit" of envy.8

The Two Sources of Morality

So there are two moralities: first, common morality from natural principles, and second, true virtue, pure holy goodwill, absolute benevolence to God. In the next section we shall see what this means in terms of various virtues in Christian moral life from the indwelling Spirit's infusing divine love in hearts and lives. Before concluding on the second dissertation, however, Edwards in Ch. VII offers four reasons why common morality, wholly diverse from true virtue, has "yet by many been mistaken for true virtue."

(1) The two resemble one another in nature. Ordinary morality has "something that belongs to the general nature of [true] virtue."9 There is "a sort of love" from natural principles, both benevolence to "persons and beings" and complacence in anything in them resembling virtuous benevolence to being or beings. There are also fruits of these principles in qualities and characters, i.e., in natural virtues.

(2) The two resemble one another in tendency and effects. Both tend to the common good. There may be beneficent effects without pure benevolence. As Edwards wrote concerning judgments of desert and justice from secondary beauty or fitness in moral relations: "So that the tendency of general benevolence to produce justice, also the tendency of justice to produce effects agreeable to general benevolence, both render justice pleasing to a virtuous mind."1

(3) "There is indeed a true negative moral goodness" from natural principles. An intelligent willing being "framed" without them or created by God with no moral constitution, would manifest much greater evil. Men would be more vicious, would characteristically resemble true virtue less; and they would "in innumerable respects" be less restrained "from acts of true wickedness" than they actually are.2

This language calls for a reminder that Edwards is not here speaking of restraining grace, but of a negative moral goodness belonging to his

-- 54 --

ethics of creation, his account of how God continually creates, rules, and preserves the moral world. There is no reference to either restraining or assisting "graces before grace" in Edwards' analysis of natural morality. (4) The last reason why those things that have not the essence of virtue are very often commonly mistaken for true virtue is most important for our present purposes. Virtues are properly called by the same names whether they spring from natural principles or from benevolence. "Another reason why these inferior affections, especially some of them, are accounted virtuous is that there are affections of the same denomination which are truly virtuous."3 Here, I judge, we must cease to speak of two moralities— natural and inspirited— and must address ourselves directly to two sources of morality that are, in important respects, one and the same morality. Immediately following the sentence above, Edwards gives, as one example, a fruit of charity properly called by the same virtue term that he used to designate one of the two instinctual kind affections.

Thus, for instance, there is a truly virtuous pity, or a compassion to others under affliction or misery from general benevolence. Pure benevolence would be sufficient to excite pity to another in calamity, if there were no particular instinct, or any other principle determining the mind thereto. It is easy to see how benevolence which seeks another's good should cause us to desire his deliverance from evil. And this is a source of pity far more extensive than the other. It excites compassion in cases that are overlooked by natural instinct.

"Virtuous pity" and the various virtuous dispositions in heart and life set forth in the sermons on 1 Corinthians 13 are specifications of charity alone. Overall, therefore, Edwards gives an account of virtues that are both theological and moral, and which are actualized on the same level as the natural virtues. Of these "theological moral" virtues he here says only that many Christian virtue terms are also fittingly used in common moral discourse to designate the fruits of natural principles.

All along in his several accounts of common morality, Edwards prepared for this by qualifying interjections ("Not but there can be..."), suggesting, for example, that there is gratitude from principles other than secondary beauty or extended self-love. In the technical language of True Virtue: "Respect to Being, in this proportion [compounded of respect to degree of greatness of being and respect to degree of its benevolence to being], is the first and most general kind of justice; which will produce

all the subordinate kinds

."4 I take that sentence to mean the same as the one-line note to himself in the "Controversies"— draft, "The Nature of True Virtue" (MS p. 188), which reads: "METHOD. Show how all virtues are derived from pure benevolence."5 Only the final brief paragraphs of Ch. VII of True Virtue cash in that promissory note. But surely the 1738 sermon series, which Tryon Edwards appropriately titled Charity and Its Fruits; Or Christian Love as Manifested in Heart and Life, dealt with that extensively. So there is a certain logic in the arrangement by which we come to the articulation of the virtues of true virtue last in this introduction.

Before, however, enlarging upon the positive nature of true virtue (as Edwards did in Charity and in the twelve positive signs in Affections), I suggest an abbreviated comparison between the analysis of morality compressed into the last three paragraphs of Ch. VII of True Virtue and that to which Thomas Aquinas devoted many pages.

"Virtuous pity" and the other fruits of charity in the sermons on 1 Corinthians 13 call to mind Aquinas on the infused moral virtues. These were moral virtues infused with the theological virtues (faith, hope, and charity), parallel with his natural moral virtues, and to which he also gives the "same denomination," e.g., justice, courage, etc.6 Edwards would agree with Aquinas that "charity is the mother and root of all the virtues."7 If it is objected that, unlike Aquinas, he would not have continued that sentence with the words, "inasmuch as it is the form of them all," that depends on the meaning of form. First cause and the end or final cause (Alpha and Omega) were the important ones for Edwards; formal cause was swallowed up in the laws of nature and moral constitutions. Still, he used Augustinian analysis and the language of virtues "arising from" and as "forms of" love.8 Aquinas' explanation of form in this context is the same: form is plastic to the surpassing end of charity. Charity is the mother of the other virtues, since she conceives within herself "the acts of the other virtues, by the desire of the last end." It is in this sense that "charity is said to be the end of other virtues, because it directs all other virtues to its own end." Thus "it is charity which directs the acts of all other virtues to the last end, and which consequently, also gives the form to

-- 56 --

all other acts of virtue.9 I know that elsewhere the philosophy of formmatter, and of virtues as excellences of divers faculties or appetites, remains intact. But in the contexts I have cited, the moral virtues seem to be specifications of charity alone. In these contexts Aquinas used Augustine to baptize Aristotle.

In addition, both Aquinas and Edwards teach that divine love in the heart penetrates and elevates natural morality. Charity perfecting the natural virtues in Aquinas (after infused moral virtues come the gifts and fruits of the Spirit, and the beatitudes) can be compared with Edwards' true virtue mixing its influence with natural principles, guiding and regulating their operation, which he treats in the final two pithy paragraphs summarily ending the seventh chapter. The more sweeping expression of Edwards' basic claim is that all virtues are specifications of charity, that for example, a virtuous pity or compassion would be a quality of Christian character even if men were without the same-named instinctual kind affection. His final, somewhat more limited assertion of that claim is that charity "excites compassion in cases that are overlooked by natural instinct." Having thus shown the preponderance of true virtue as a source of "theological moral" virtues when alongside homonymous natural moral virtues, he then proceeds to list "mixed fruits" of charity and natural principles. This should be read as yet another claim, corresponding to Aquinas' "grace perfecting nature":

And even in those cases to which instinct extends, it mixes its influence with the natural principle, and guides and regulates its operations. And when this is the case, the pity which is exercised may be called a virtuous compassion. So [i.e., in like manner] there is a virtuous gratitude, or a gratitude that arises not only from self-love, but from a superior principle of disinterested general benevolence. As 'tis manifest that when we receive kindness from such as we love already, we are more disposed to gratitude, and disposed to greater degrees of it, than when the mind is destitute of any such friendly prepossession— therefore, when the superior principle of virtuous love has a governing hand, and regulates the affair, it may be called a virtuous gratitude. So [in like manner] there is a virtuous love of justice, arising from pure benevolence to Being in general, as that naturally and necessarily inclines the heart, that every particular being should have a share of benevolence as is proportioned to its dignity, consisting in the degree of its being and the degree of its

-- 57 --

virtue... And so [in like manner] it is easy to see how there may be a virtuous sense of desert different from what is natural and common. And so [in like manner] a virtuous conscientiousness, or a sanctified conscience. And as when natural affections have their operations mixed with the influence of virtuous benevolence, and are directed and determined hereby, they may be called virtuous, so there may be a virtuous love of parents to children, and between other near relatives, a virtuous love of our town, or country, or nation. Yea, and a virtuous love between the sexes, as there may be the influence of virtue mingled with instinct, and virtue may govern with regard to the particular manner of its operation, and may guide it to such ends as are agreeable to the great ends and purposes of true virtue.

Genuine virtue prevents that increase of the habits of pride and sensuality, which tend to overbear and greatly diminish the exercises of the forementioned useful and necessary principles of nature. And a principle of general benevolence softens and sweetens the mind, and makes it more susceptible of the proper influence and exercise of the gentler natural instincts, and directs every one into its proper channel, and determines the exercise to the proper manner and measure, and guides all to the best purposes.1

The language of the final paragraph suggests the mingling of "Charity is... kind" (1 Corinthians 13:4) with "instinctual kind affections" in general, of which Edwards names and discusses only two.

What is this if not an account of the perfection of natural morality? To perfect means to bring to complete actualization; and by this Aquinas does not mean simply the complete actualization of virtues that are only specified by faculties of the soul. Instead, moral virtue in its perfect state "makes its possessor good."2 Radically to be turned to the supreme end of charity makes the man, the moral agent, good, which seems more than simply to bring the virtues or excellence of his faculties to actuality. Because of the central role conscience plays in True Virtue, one might conjecture a parallel with prudence in Aquinas, in this respect only: that through conscience or through prudence charity perfects every virtue, and perfects the possessor of them. If by a "sanctified conscience" Edwards does not mean charity perfecting prudence by conceiving within herself its acts by desire of the last end, and so conceiving the acts of other virtues as well, thus making their possessor good, I suggest that significant difference may be located in their concepts of conscience or

-- 58 --

prudence. The difference is in the moral philosophies of these two men, and not in their theologies of grace or divine love.

Finally, Aquinas' answer to the question "Whether a Man can Will or Do Good without Grace" does not support the notion that the natural or acquired virtues alone have an integrity that enables men to achieve their natural good, while grace only steps in to enable them to reach their supernatural end (the theological virtues, distinct from the natural). Aquinas believed that was true of human nature only in its prelapsarian state: "In regard to the sufficiency of his operative power, man in the state of pure nature could will and do, by his own natural power, the good proportionate to his nature, such as the good of acquired virtue, though not surpassing good such as the good of infused virtue." So even before the Fall, "he cannot by his own power fulfill the whole good that pertains to his nature," i.e., his eternal good. After the Fall, the natural power to will and to do the good proportionate to his nature was also lost. Explaining that "human nature is not so entirely corrupted by sin... as to be deprived of natural good altogether," Aquinas gives a rather astonishing rejection of any "hierarchical synthesis" of natural morality and grace. His explanation is that "even in a state of corrupt nature a man can do some particular good by the power of his own nature, such as build houses, plant vineyards, and things of this kind"!3 The good pertaining to man's nature which he can still will and do is practical reason in its exercise of making, not in its exercise of doing (ethics or politics). The "healing" fallen humanity needs lies in the very desire for the good, and in this man needs divine help, by which he is first moved to act well. Should we conclude that for Aquinas the grace of charity is needed to direct the acts of every natural, acquired virtue in fallen man to its own end? That after the Fall, all the virtues must be specifications of charity, conceiving in herself the acts of every virtue that can be in fallen creatures?

We seem to have arrived at the conclusion that Edwards' account of common morality is at least as favorable to the moral competence of the unregenerate, perhaps even more favorable, than that of Thomas Aquinas— if anyone supposes that praising fallen humankind is a test of the truth of Christian teaching, or even of what that teaching in fact is. It does seem that on the infused moral virtues, the gifts and fruits of the Spirit, and the beatitudes, Aquinas used Augustine to baptize Aristotle by total immersion three times (in the name of the Father, and of the Son, and of the Holy Spirit). Concerning the grace of charity perfecting

-- 59 --

rational, common, or natural morality, the two thinkers are most in agreement; most in disagreement on the need for charity to presuppose common morality (supposing that question asked of Edwards). At the same time, by Edwards' account, virtues from natural principles were never merely splendid vices (supposing Augustine to have thought so). Common morality has integrity without charity. There is a wholeness to moral life without charity, and a wholeness to moral life from charity. The former does not point to the latter, nor does the latter presuppose the former.

Other contexts may not support the interpretation and parallels I have suggested, and I do not know whether or when the Saint nodded. I am content with a weaker claim: from these comparisons it is reasonable to claim that these thinkers' music is the same, although the lyrics may have changed. This is to say, the main features— the general picture— of the Christian moral life is the same. It is the philosophy or metaphysics— and the science— of each that makes for any notable differences. The same can be said of any of the great doctors of grace in Christian history and tradition.

Charity and Its Virtues

These fifteen sermons unfold the meaning and movement of St. Paul's Hymn to Love, 1 Corinthians 13. Introductory to the whole are the first three sermons, whose Doctrines4 are: (1) "All that virtue which is saving, and distinguishing true Christians from others, is summed up in Christian or divine love." (2) "The ordinary influence of God's Spirit, working saving grace... is a more excellent blessing than any of the extraordinary gifts of the Spirit," and (3) "All which men can do, and all which they can suffer, will not make up for the want of sincerity in the heart."

The love of which Edwards speaks is one in principle [principium, spring or source) flowing out toward diverse objects. One and the same Spirit infuses love, influences the heart to love God and fellow man. Moreover, these "are not two works of the Spirit of God, one to infuse a spirit of love to God, and another a spirit of love to men. But in doing one he doth the other."5

-- 60 --

Nevertheless, the accent can fall on one or the other, love toward men or love to God. So following the three introductory sermons, Edwards' next seven sermons primarily concern the fruits of charity in disposition and action toward men. I call this division the "first table of love," reversing the order of the "tables" in the Decalogue. Here the Doctrines are: (4) "A Christian spirit disposes persons meekly to bear ill that is received from others, and cheerfully and freely to do good to others," (5) "A truly Christian spirit is opposite to an envious spirit," (6) "A Christian spirit is a humble spirit," (7) "A Christian spirit is opposite to a selfish spirit,"6 (8) "Charity contrary to an angry spirit," and (9) "That a Christian spirit is contrary to a censorious spirit; or in other words, it is contrary to a disposition uncharitably to judge others." These are Christian virtues particularly related to our neighbors. The final sermon in this section— on ver. 1 Corinthians 13:6— is one in which Edwards reads the Apostle as cutting short his specifications and summarizing all that he has said, or that can ever be said about these first fruits of charity in the words "Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth." From these words, Edwards derives the Doctrine (10) "All true Christian grace tends to holy practice."

In the second division or table of love, Edwards stresses love to God. Sermons eleven through fourteen, on ver. 1 Corinthians 13:7 and 1 Corinthians 13:8, expound the Doctrines: (11) "They who are truly gracious have a spirit for Christ's sake to undergo all sufferings to which they may be exposed in the way of their duty," (12) "There is a concatenation of the graces of Christianity," (13) "True Christian grace is that which nothing that opposes it can ever overthrow," and (14) "That great fruit of the Spirit in which the Holy Ghost shall not only for a season but everlastingly be communicated to the church of Christ is divine love." Readers will note more frequent use of "grace" and "graces" in this division of the sermons, as they will have noted that love to God is often given as a reason for the disposition that is a fruit of divine love going out primarily toward men.

The fifteenth and final sermon— ver. 1 Corinthians 13:8–10, on the theme (15) "Heaven Is a World of Love"— is Edwards' remarkable glorification of

-- 61 --

divine love in every respect: perfect love for one another in indefectible love to God. This is Edwards' triumphant conclusion and eschatological verification of the wealth of particulars he found in the chapter as a whole. The rhetorical power of this last sermon is rivaled only by his other "virtuoso performance,"7 "Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God."

It is impossible, and would be a travesty for me to attempt, to summarize the rich texture of Edwards' phenomenology of the Christian moral life in the sermon series as a whole. Nevertheless, a sketch can be given of the more important interconnections in Edwards' reading of St. Paul's chapter as an account of the Christian moral life.

The third sermon, to which I give the short title8 "Nothing Can Make

-- 62 --

Up for Want of Sincerity in the Heart," both concludes the general introductory section and is transitional to the particular virtuous dispositions and actions that are the fruit of love to men, which Edwards depicts in analytical detail in the first table of love (sermons four through ten). Edwards' use of the plurals "performances" and "sufferings" indicates that he is still speaking in somewhat general terms of moral matters. By its extremity, giving all one's goods to feed the poor encompasses all performances. And giving one's body to be burned, by its extremity, encompasses all sufferings. If these extremes profit nothing and have no ultimate significance without sincerity in the heart, then nothing else of a "moral nature" can. So Edwards goes on to analyze in the following sermons particular moral qualities— seemingly less extreme— that are fruits of charity toward men.

To give all one's goods to feed the poor seems to be an action, and martyrdom looks like suffering an action by others. But both are a sort of doing, on Edwards' reading. Both are acts of giving. "When a man has given away all his goods," Edwards writes, "he has nothing remaining which he can give but himself." Yet giving himself, his own body, avails nothing "if the heart is withheld."9 So "sincerity" in the heart means to be a self-giver.

We should take Edwards at his word when he says "great" performances and sufferings, for he uses it as a term of approval. These things are admirable simpliciter. "Many of the heathen... have been eminent for justice, and others for their great deeds which they have done for the public good"1 So also of "great sufferings": "history gives an account of some who have yielded themselves to voluntary death out of mere stoutness and sturdiness of spirit, who have braved it out and died rather than yield to the demands of others when they might have saved their lives."2 These words do not express a grudging admiration. True, Edwards speaks in this context of great performances or great sufferings done from mixed or false motives. But he deems these things "great" simpliciter. Moreover, he believes them often done from "natural principles" or motives that as such are admirable. Indeed, "it is hard to say how far natural principles may carry men in particular duties and performances."3

-- 63 --

Edwards draws no hard-and-fast line between the fruits of natural principles and the fruits of divine love in the heart. Nor is there any reason to believe that he changed his mind about this subject between the sermon series and True Virtue, which analyzes, as we have seen, four sources of a common morality that Edwards knew to admire.

Edwards' standard of excellence, however, was different from the praiseworthiness of natural virtue or common morality. He assessed moral matters by their "worth in the sight of God." Recall that "Christian love is one and the same in principle" whether exercised toward God or toward fellow creatures. The test, then, is not the sincerity of love of neighbor in separation from love to God. There is no such love. Nor is it a heteronomous or pious standard of love to God in separation from love to fellow human beings. There is no such love. To ask whether there is any sincere regard to God in the matter is identical with asking whether there is any sincere love to fellow men in the hearts of those who do great deeds or suffer bravely in some cause. Charity alone in the hearts of moral agents is "amiable" to God. "Whatever is done or suffered, yet if the heart is withheld there is nothing really given to God."4

God delights in the giving heart, not in the gift he does not need. He delights in the cymbalist praising him, not in tinkling cymbals used in his praise. This, I suggest, is a definition of gift-giving to God; a gift is no gift without a willing giver, a self-giver. It is also a definition of gift-giving to neighbor. Without sincerity in the heart the essential gift is withheld. Charity, then, is Edwards' basic moral notion in terms of which every disposition or action is assessed, whatever be their greatness or goodness simpliciter, whatever principle is their source or spring, and whatever benefits result.

It may be helpful to interrupt my exposition of Edwards' series at this point in order to take up two different groupings of sermons, considering together certain related sermons out of the order of the verses in 1 Corinthians 13. First, I shall lift up to view the "progression" in Edwards' exposition of qualities of Christian life in sermons four, eleven, thirteen, and fourteen, whose texts are deceptively similar statements in ver. 1 Corinthians 13:4, 1 Corinthians 13:7, and 1 Corinthians 13:8. These qualities, fruits, or graces, I suggest, viewed in their significant sequence, manifest an increasing strength and an increasing unity of the whole company of virtues, which reflects the story of redemption. Then, second, we will view together sermons two and (again) fourteen, whose short titles are "Love More Excellent Than Extraordinary Gifts of the

-- 64 --

Spirit" and "Divine Love Alone Lasts Eternally." In these two sets of sermons, I suggest, we more than glimpse the objective story line of the work of redemption that frames a "moral progression" of the church— individually or collectively, as Edwards puts it. After exhibiting this theological-moral story line throughout Edwards' exposition of the chapter as a whole, we return to his account of the remainder of the specific fruits of charity in the first and second tables of love, which in their variety are the sum of divine love in the heart and the sum of this, Edwards' most important treatise on Christian ethics.

A progression in moral matters running through the sermon series is the ingenious interpretation Edwards gives of four phrases in 1 Corinthians 13:4, 1 Corinthians 13:7, 1 Corinthians 13:8— "Charity suffereth long," "Beareth all things," "Endureth all things," and "Charity never faileth." These texts seem to say much the same thing, or at least to have largely overlapping meanings. Edwards devoted a sermon to each: "Long-suffering and Kindness" (sermon four), "Undergoing Sufferings a Duty to Christ" (sermon eleven), "Grace Never Overthrown" (sermon thirteen), and "Divine Love Alone Lasts Eternally" (sermon fourteen).

The first of these sermons, based on the text "Charity suffereth long and is kind," begins what I have called the first table— love to neighbor. The next two fall within my second table— love to God in this life. The fourth sermon, on "charity never faileth," is on the theme— if I may so phrase it— charity and its fruits gone to heaven. To follow this thread through the series enables us to grasp the movement Edwards saw in the chapter as a whole, from accent on love to men to accent on love to God, from grace to glory, from the indwelling Spirit's implanting divine love in the heart back to God as the heart's supreme object and enduring delight.

Suffering long in the face of ill received from others and being kind in doing good to others are the first pair of particular fruits of charity directed toward our fellowmen. Indeed, these two qualities or dispositions of moral agents interpenetrate each other. Edwards holds the two virtues— long-suffering and kindness— together in a single duplex sermon. The disposition "meekly to bear ill, what is received from others" and the disposition "cheerfully and freely to do good to others" are not properly understood as separate qualities. There is kindness to others already in meekly suffering ills done by them, and kindness in doing good to others must encompass long-suffering in this life, and is already freely and cheerfully willing to do so. The force and meaning of this extraordinary sermon was seriously impaired when Tryon Edwards divided

it into two "Lectures"— a fault repeated in all heretofore available editions which are only reprints of Tryon's publication.

But does not "Beareth all things" say the same thing as "Charity suffereth long"? Edwards thinks not. Just as the sermon on the twin themes of long-suffering and kindness is the first in the table of fruits of love to others, so the sermon on "Beareth all things" is the first in the table of fruits of love to God. In this phrase the Apostle proclaims the tendency of charity to dispose men and make them ready to undergo sufferings for Christ's sake; and so the short title given this sermon is "Undergoing Sufferings a Duty to Christ." Many have supposed the words "Beareth all things" to refer to suffering injuries from men. Edwards' interpretation is that they mean bearing all things "for the cause of Christ and religion."

He offers four reasons for rejecting the ordinary reading.5 (1) We ought not to suppose that the Apostle "in reckoning up the various fruits of charity, would use a tautology, and now mention the same thing in this verse." (2) The Apostle "seems evidently to have done with fruits of charity of that nature," since he summed them all up in the previous, transitional verse on which Edwards preached his tenth sermon, "Grace Tends to Holy Practice." (3) Many times elsewhere the Apostle speaks of the great fruit of charity in suffering for the cause of Christ. And finally, (4) the words that follow in ver. 1 Corinthians 13:7— "believeth all things," "hopeth all things"— "all show that the Apostle has done with those fruits of charity, which immediately respect our neighbor."

Again, does not "Endureth all things" say the same thing as "Beareth all things"? Edwards thinks not. All these "synonymous expressions" are "vulgarly understood... as though the Apostle in all of them only said the same thing over and over in different words."6 We can see upon close consideration that each "signifies a distinct fruit of charity." Meekly suffering injuries from men is one fruit. Bearing all things for the sake of Christ that are in the way of duty is another fruit. "Endureth all things" signifies something different still.

Edwards bases his reading of "Endureth all things" on the original Greek panta hypomenei, which he says means "remains under all things," i.e., endures under all opposition that can ever be brought against it. To get this reading out of the Greek πάντα ὑπομένει Edwards separates out the prefix ὑπο from μένει. Long before the New Testament was written, the prefix had merged with the substantive, as in our word "withstand."

-- 66 --

To "endure under" is the meaning, just as we today might make a point by saying that to "withstand" has great strength because it means to "stand with." Edwards seems to be quite aware of what he is doing, since he does not claim his to be a literal reading. Instead, he says that a more "liberal" translation could be "remains under all things."7

More important to note is the fact that the movement of thought through these verses and sermons parallels Edwards' view of the history of the work of redemption. Concerning the first three texts, Edwards makes the same argument. These synonymous expressions are "vulgarly understood... as though the Apostle in all of them only said the same thing... in different words." But we ought not suppose the Apostle was a tautologist. On the story line of redemption, to endure or remain under all possible opposition is the final fruit of charity that Christians need in this life. This virtue strengthens the church for the final tribulation before the reign of Christ on earth.

The text of the final sermon in this group of four is "Charity never faileth." "Charity never faileth" seemed so obviously different from "Charity endureth all things" that Edwards did not trouble to defend the Apostle against uttering a tautology in this instance. Bearing up under all possible opposition is no longer needed as a fruit of charity. Neither is undergoing suffering for the sake of Christ in the way of duty, nor is kindly long-suffering in the face of ill done to us by others. "That great fruit of the Spirit in which the Holy Ghost shall not only for a season but everlastingly be communicated to the church of Christ is divine love" is Edwards' Doctrine, penultimate only to his great peroration, "Heaven Is a World of Love." Thus the saving gifts and graces move through these four sermons toward God's end in redemption, which is his end in creation. This progression is sufficient to show that the story line of the history of redemption governs Edwards' account of the Christian moral life. As Edwards reads the chapter, Christ is building up his kingdom in the soul, and among men.

Is there a progression in moral matters corresponding to this Christological-eschatological progression? Of course there is. But we need first to consider additional details concerning that objective story line by taking note of some significant differences between the second and fourteenth sermons in exposition of the passing away of the spiritual gifts.

Edwards' stress in the second sermon is indicated by its Doctrine: "The ordinary influence of God's Spirit, working saving grace in the

-- 67 --

heart, is a more excellent blessing than any of the extraordinary gifts of the Spirit." To magnify the far-surpassing blessing of divine love in the heart, Edwards heaps praise on the excellency of those "wonderful" endowments given in times past.8 These were the gifts spoken of in chapter twelve: the gift of tongues ("languages which they had never before learned"), the gift of prophecy, the gift of knowledge ("immediate revelation or inspiration," but including also "speculative knowledge"), and the faith of miracles.9 This sermon introduces Edwards' distinction between "common" and "uncommon," or saving, gifts, crisscrossing the distinction in Scripture between ordinary and extraordinary gifts. Common to the godly and ungodly alike are some ordinary gifts as well as the extraordinary gifts just named. But there is one ordinary, uncommon gift, working grace and infusing divine love in the heart.

This saving gift of the Spirit— and what makes it uncommon— blesses men with "a quality inherent in the nature of him who is the subject of it," "makes a man's heart and nature excellent."1 In this the Spirit— who is the Love of the Father and the Son— does "communicate himself in his own proper nature to the man," "impart itself in its holy nature."2 The Spirit becomes "an indwelling vital principle in the soul." "[T]he subject becomes a spiritual being, denominated so from the Spirit of God which dwells in him and of whose nature he is partaker," "a partaker of the Spirit so as to become spiritual in himself and in his own nature." Indeed, this "more excellent blessing" is what Edwards means by "the spiritual image of God," the "amiable image and likeness of Christ."3 Here is the first reference to the notion of "participation" in the "divine nature" (2 Peter 1:4) that became crucial for Edwards' demonstration that God's end in creation was "but one."

By contrast, the common influences of the Spirit are "adventitious"; they "produce effects in the man," or by him. They are like "garments" or— since Edwards speaks here only of the "common extraordinary" gifts, reserving the "common ordinary" ones for his later sermon— like "precious jewels, which a man carries about him." Miraculous gifts do not make "the very soul itself... a precious jewel."4

-- 68 --

Comparison of privileges is the main point of the second sermon. Subordinate to this, Edwards discusses at some length— in language that anticipates Distinguishing Marks and Affections— what are "no certain sign of grace, nor anything saving,"5 and from this he concludes: "All the fruit of the Spirit, upon which we are to lay weight as evidential of grace, is summed up in charity or Christian love, because this is the sum of all grace"; or is "comprehended in that charity which is the fruit of the ordinary influences of the Spirit."6 Edwards' theme is discerning the Spirit.

One reason saving grace in the heart is more excellent is that divine love (summed up or comprehended in grace) was "the end of all extraordinary gifts," their end in the history of redemption.7 In Edwards' rendering of the movement of the chapter, it is notable that the purpose of every common gift (ordinary and extraordinary alike) is fully developed only in sermon fourteen when the end of them all— indefectible perfection— comes more fully into view. Moreover, the class of "common ordinary" gifts is specified and given meaning only in the later sermon, when they, too, are then and then only viewed as having passed away from the church, collectively and individually.

The theme of sermon fourteen is divine love everlastingly communicated to the church of Christ; "that great fruit of the Spirit" gone to heaven and there "confirmed" forever. Several things are a consequence of this, looking back upon the movement of redemption on earth. For one thing, the division of the chapter at the sixth verse between charity going out primarily to our neighbors and charity going out primarily to

-- 69 --

God and Christ is ignored, or rather becomes an indifferent distinction.8 Another small but perhaps significant feature of sermon fourteen is the fact that there is a brief sketch of "certain seasons" in the work of redemption when extraordinary gifts were granted before the period from the coming of Christ to the closing of the canon. Without elaborating further on the redemption God has been working in every age since the Fall of man, this recital of past temporary displays of miraculous gifts starts with "the church before the beginning of the canon of Scripture; that is, before the writing of the book of Job9 and the five books of Moses."1

Far more important to notice is that the perimeters of the time in which the church now lives are more sharply marked in sermon fourteen— with the near approach of the end time within which Christ will reign on earth, and the end of that end time. In the second sermon, which was mainly a comparative evaluation of excellences, Edwards had simply insisted that the extraordinary gifts have ceased since the closing of the canon of Scripture and are not to be expected in "the glorious future times of the church." The "calling of the Jews2 and

the destruction of Antichrist"3 are the only lines drawn on those approaching glorious times. For characterization of those coming times we have to turn to the later sermon. The moral features of life within those times or eons are worth noting.

Of course, Edwards continues to preach that gifts of miraculous inspiration ceased long ago and are no more to be expected. But now, following the movement of the chapter further along the story line of redemption into the end (he believed) of creation and providence as well, far more is said about that end, telos, or purpose. Edwards used means-end language to group extraordinary means with some external ordinary means granted the church on earth. "When those saints who once had the enjoyment and use of those means of grace came to heaven, the miraculous gifts as well as other means ceased. There is no occasion for any means of grace in heaven; either ordinary, such as stated ordinances of God's house, [as] sacraments and prayer, or extraordinary, as prophecy, and gifts of tongues, and gift of knowledge." The end toward which the whole creation moves with the story of redemption is "a perfect man, unto the measure of the stature of the fullness of Christ."4 As in the second sermon we located an early use by Edwards of the notion of "participation," so here we have pleroma, the fullness, which together with partaking of the divine nature clinch the argument of End of Creation.

Edwards deploys the full force of a means-end argument: this is the "one reason" he gives for the doctrine of this sermon. "Miraculous gifts being means to a further end are good no further than as they tend to that end, and so cease when that end is obtained. But divine love is that great good which is the end itself, and therefore remains when the means cease." Immediate revelations "were never given for any other end but only to promote the setting up and building up of Christ's kingdom in men's hearts."5 Edwards' language here is almost exactly that of the sermon series he preached the following year on the story of the work of

-- 71 --

redemption.6 Clearly, also, the kingdom of Christ building up in the souls of men should never be separated from the kingdom of Christ building up in the world among men— certainly not in a sermon next to one in which heaven is a world, essentially not a gathering place for individuals.

Most important for comprehending every facet of Edwards' understanding of the Christian moral life is the class of "common ordinary" gifts, not developed at all in the second sermon. These are "common convictions of sin, common illuminations, common religious affections."7 (He does not say "common gracious affections.") These gifts are common to the good and the bad, the godly and the ungodly alike; they are experienced by the Spirit's "adventitious" influence upon both those who are truly virtuous and those who are truly without virtue. Those, too, are means, and they have the same destination as wonder-working did. "They have no goodness in themselves, and are no further good than as they tend to promote grace and holiness radically and summarily consisting in divine love."8 But these common ordinary gifts have not yet passed away; they still promote Christ's kingdom in converted or unconverted human hearts, and will do so in the world and in the church to the end of time. In the church this means until the deaths of "particular members" but "collectively as a body" until the Day of Judgment.

A final point might easily be missed by the reader. In the tenth sermon Edwards distinguished "that faith which is saving from that which is only common." There are then a sort of "faith" and also a sort of "hope" that are as common as religious experience generally. In the present sermon, he lists as fruits of the Spirit "that conviction, that faith, and illumination... common both to godly and ungodly." He speaks of "common temporary faith"; of when "tongues and faith fail." When wicked men die, "their common temporary faith which they have had, which has had no divine love as an ingredient in it and to be the soul of it

shall cease"; but when saints "come to die," they "shall leave all that was common in their faith and in their hope and all that did not appertain to divine charity."9 When that which is perfect is come, that which is in part faith and in part hope will pass away, and only that in every virtue which appertains to divine charity, as an essential ingredient in it and the soul of it, shall last eternally.1

This completes the two groups of sermons I have chosen to lift out of sequence. First, the increasing perdurance or strength of the saints, individually and collectively, shown by Edwards' interpretation of the four synonymous expressions in ver. 1 Corinthians 13:4, 1 Corinthians 13:7, and 1 Corinthians 13:8. Second, the additional Christological-eschatological enforcement of our understanding of the story line of redemption by his two sermons on the extraordinary gifts, i.e., the theological-moral movement between sermon two and sermon fourteen. If such is the frame story of the charity sermons, then we would expect something of the same movement to be evident in Edwards' consideration of particular virtues, which I now resume. It is not too farfetched to surmise that Edwards assumed a movement in the Old Testament of the Decalogue downward, with a first table of commands primarily indicating duties to God, and a second, primarily duties to our fellow creatures— as he read St. Paul's chapter to be a continuation of that movement of a people, individually and collectively, back to God. This is his root metaphor, Alpha and Omega, expressed in a famous passage in End of Creation as God's fullness received and returned, both "emanation" and "remanation."2 Turning now to Edwards' account of the virtues as specifications of charity or divine love in heart and life, we shall look at two things simultaneously: (1) the remarkable tapestry of virtues Edwards weaves and (2) some important moral progressions corresponding to the Christological-eschatological movement in the chapter.

One identifiable moral progression began with long-suffering and kindness— the first fruits of charity that have our fellow creatures as object directly and immediately. There is progression in perdurance, beginning with long-suffering and continuing in the sequence to "Charity never faileth." This is moral, not ontological only. "Greatness of soul" and Stoic virtus (which Christian long-suffering resembles) accent strength, not only a moral quality like sincerity or gentleness or kindness

-- 73 --

that, standing alone, could be fragile. Christian virtus also goes on from strength to strength, from its beginning in divine love implanted in us and among us (the kingdom Christ is building up). That strength ultimately empowers the whole array of virtues.

Meekness was joined with long-suffering in the Doctrine of the fourth sermon. Edwards quickly wove into his tapestry the qualities of peaceableness, calmness, quietness and repose of mind, self-possession, and "smooth outward behavior." Long-suffering with these qualifiers means essentially "always continuing" to bear injuries, just as for Kierkegaard "charity hopeth all things" means essentially a love that is "always hopeful."3 Edwards' characterization of this virtue often sounds positively Stoic in its imperturbability. Indeed, he calls this virtue "a godlike greatness of soul"; "a more noble and excellent valor and greatness of mind than great conquerors who subdued walled cities, [possessed]." By this virtue we are fitted "to live in such a world as this." The wise Christian is "like the moon walking in her brightness, while the dogs are barking at her she is not moved, but goes on in her own course placidly shining through the heavens still." But Christian long-suffering, unlike Stoic imperturbability, is not grounded in curbing the passions but in love to God, in gratitude, and imitation of Christ. This is what "sets persons very much above the injuries of men."4 Therefore, suffering ill from others is already interpenetrated and suffused with kindness, a spirit of being willing freely and cheerfully to do good to them. Contrariwise, to be kind, to be free and cheerful in willingness to continue doing good to others, to the good and the bad, "both to friends and enemies," "to the thankful and the unthankful,"5 in short, to all creatures who are in the natural image of God, has of necessity an ingredient of long-suffering in it.

Under "kindness" Edwards introduces the familiar loves of benevolence and complacence. These are but "various denominations" of Christian love. As charity goes out to others, he wrote, "as it respects the good enjoyed or to be enjoyed by the beloved, it is called love of benevolence; and as it respects good to be enjoyed in the beloved, it is called love of complacence." Of the two familiar loves, Edwards takes the love of benevolence to be the more fundamental, "most of an imitation of the eternal love and grace of God, and the dying love of Christ."6 Evidently, however,

-- 74 --

benevolence as ordinarily understood is only one "denomination," one of several "exercises" of charity. Here we have the seed of Edwards' devised term, "pure" or "absolute" benevolence, meaning love to God (and all that implies), which he uses in the first chapter of True Virtue.

Even ordinary love of benevolence is more than a "wishing good," for it disposes its subject to do something effectual. Thus Edwards' agent-morality holds together benevolence and beneficence, heart and life, an ethics of virtue and an ethics of action, which are often rendered too distinct today. Yet this beneficence evidently has charity in it, of which it is an exercise. For Edwards has been speaking of the cheerful giver of the first sermon, one who does not withhold the heart from benefits given away.

A second pair of distinguishing Christian virtues is drawn from "Charity envieth not" and "Charity vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up. Doth not behave itself unseemly." We should begin now to pay attention to the tapestry, or the architecture, of Edwards' systematic discussion of these virtues. "Suffering long" had respect to the ill received from others, and "kindness" treated good to be done to others. In the next pair, "envying not" has to do with the good or prosperity others possess, while "vaunting not" involves the good or prosperity we ourselves possess. These two virtues deal with good possessed, and are not as closely or as internally related virtues as the first pair; therefore Edwards devotes two different sermons to the opposite of envy and to humility.

The notion of comparison, we shall see, is used by Edwards in different ways to analyze each of these fruits. Envy is defined as "a spirit of opposition to others' comparative happiness."7 This is evident when others' prosperity is above ours. But we may be envious of those equal or near us, lest they outdistance us in good or goods possessed; and of those below us who (a comparative spirit is quick to suspect) may be fast catching up. Thus an envious spirit seeks to be "singular." It finds occasion for bitterness by comparing one's own allotment with that of anyone else.

Edwards then promptly reserves the word "envy" for those equal or above us, and suggests that envy of those near or below us in good or goods is better called "grudging." In its extremity, envy or grudging shifts from others' prosperity to dislike and even to a settled hatred of their persons. Hatred of person is another sort of vice altogether. It (not selfishness) is the very opposite of charity itself. Having introduced grudging as a refinement of terms in moral discourse about envy, Edwards

-- 75 --

invokes with mounting rhetorical power the fact that God in Christ did not grudge us anything there was in his possession or power, including bringing lowly sinners to glory with him.8 This is important to note here because Edwards is going to deny that Christ in the divine nature had "humility"; his rather was the excellence of "grudging not." So also the superior saints in heaven begrudge not the lower, whose perfect solidarity with those above them is "envying not."9 An ungrudging

-- 76 --

spirit— introduced here in the sermon on envy— gains increasing sway, in sermons that follow, over the moral life that Christ is building up in and among his creatures.

A Christian spirit sees envy to be odious; it "mortifies such a principle or disposition in the heart"; it brings contentment and quietness of mind in relation to others' prosperity; it teaches us rather to rejoice therein.1 Here the structure of Edwards' treatise on the Christian moral life again becomes visible. We are disposed to this virtue and to mortify the vice in two ways.2 We are disposed to the virtue "envying not" first by charity immediately and "directly of its own tendency." We are disposed to it second by charity working indirectly and "mediately, as it works humility." Thus humility— which, we shall see, is a hinge or cardinal virtue in Edwards' system— is a mediating fruit of charity that promotes the virtue "envying not," even as also charity directly engenders that virtue. Put the other way round: Charity directly mortifies the vice of envy. This it does also "more mediately" by mortifying pride in the good or goods we possess.

Moreover, these pairs— pride/humility and envy/envying not— are mutually fructifying vices/virtues. As Edwards says in the next sermon, being puffed up with pride most provokes one to envy and is used to excuse envy. Conversely, an envious spirit fuels pride and provides an excuse for it. Rejoicing in the good others possess (contra envy) and humility in regard to the good we possess (contra pride) are evidently complementary and mutually supportive Christian dispositions.

For the text of his sixth sermon on humility, Edwards joins together the final words of ver. 1 Corinthians 13:4, "vaunteth not itself, is not puffed up," with the first phrase of ver. 1 Corinthians 13:5, "Doth not behave itself unseemly." These statements refer to a greater or lesser degree of pride, especially as being puffed up manifests itself in "vaunting" and other behaviors. My comment will stress humility as the struggle of a Christian spirit against pride, understanding these cardinal opposites to be Godward in orientation. Only a reading of the sermon itself will show how Edwards locates

-- 77 --

humility among the dispositions of love primarily going out to our neighbors.

Just as the focus of "envying not" was upon the comparative good possessed by others, so humility is a "sense of our own comparative meanness" focused upon the good we possess. "Meanness" is not a demeaning term— except to moral agents who aspire to be "all" or "singular" in their autonomy. Edwards expressly excludes men's "low opinion of themselves as compared with other men from melancholy, or from the meanness of their circumstances, or from their natural constitution." Theirs is not true humility; indeed, one can ascribe to Edwards the belief that such men should think of themselves— in the natural image of God, or the spiritual— more highly than they do. Notice also that, in relation to our fellow creatures, Edwards' meaning is that "he who has a right sense of himself with respect to God will open his eyes to see himself aright in all respects." If a person sees how he stands with "respect to the first Being... it will tend greatly to help him to a just apprehension of the place in which he stands among his creatures."3 It is fair to call this rectitude in self-esteem.

A contemporary reader is apt to be misled early in this sermon. Edwards first speaks of humility as "an excellency proper to all created intelligent beings," men or angels. It "primarily" consists of our sense of "meanness as compared with God or a sense of the infinite distance between God and us,"4 entailing rectitude of self-esteem among men. This we may call "creature feeling," and "fellow-creature feeling." This sense of humility is proper to all creatures, great and small, to excellent and glorious beings. The saints and angels in heaven excel in it; and this same sense of proper "comparative littleness" was before the Fall.

But Edwards' next point is: "Humility in fallen man implies a sense of a twofold meanness before God, natural and moral."5 In this world a humble person has not only a sense of his distance from God's greatness but also a sense of sin, filthiness, and pollution before God's infinite holiness. Here Edwards cites, among other scriptures, the passages from Isaiah and Job that Rudolf Otto later used as examples of creature feeling suffused with a sense of self-abhorrence, of uncleanness and

-- 78 --

repentance in dust and ashes.6 Both littleness and unworthiness are constitutive of humility as a Christian virtue in this life.

Still, a sense of both natural and moral meanness is a Christian virtue. The ungodly have no such sense. When Edwards says that man's "natural meanness is become much greater since the Fall," since the "moral ruin" that "greatly impaired his natural faculties, though it has not extinguished them,"7 he does not mean that the ungodly need to acknowledge a greater natural meanness. Nor does he mean that only the ungodly— the fallen— are subjects of the second element in humility, a sense of their unworthiness. The contrary is the case. He means rather that insofar as a person is come to a knowledge of the first Being, only "so far is he let into the knowledge of other things." These words immediately precede the introduction of moral meanness as an ingredient of humility in fallen man.8 Humility is a sense of our "natural meanness" before God's greatness along with a sense of our "moral meanness" before God's excellency and the beauty of holiness. The saints in heaven have both components. Indeed, the sense of moral meanness increases among the inhabitants of heaven as their sense of God's excellency increases. It is a mistake to suppose that moral meanness primarily respects the inherent vileness in only some or many men. It is also important for Edwards that our sense of moral unworthiness has respect first to God's excellency. "It is necessary that we should not only see," he wrote, "God's greatness, but also his excellence and holiness."9 So in the end Edwards teaches that there can be no true humility— no true self-understanding— without a creature's taking his true measure by God's loveliness, excellency, and holiness. The beauty (pulchrum) of God's goodness (bonum) is the transcendental measure, not any trivial distinctions among fellow worms of the dust. Moreover, a sense of the divine loveliness works humility; a sense of vileness, never. This sequence, or at least the logical priority, is important for Edwards. From love to God

-- 79 --

in the heart comes humility, and among men every "answerable" behavior.

Concerning other virtues so far considered, the claim was that they are fruits of charity, or are its "tendency" or "effects." Concerning humility, Edwards makes another and quite different claim, namely, that charity and the exercises of love drawn forth by the gospel imply humility. He does not mean, of course, logical implication. It is of the utmost importance for us to ponder this word's meaning, even if it cannot be made sun-clear. For here it is that Edwards gives the reason for his astonishing assertion that if everything could be stated about the many works or fruits of humility, that statement "would in some way or other include the whole of our duty, both towards God and towards men."1 Christian humility seems, therefore, to be that in which the Christian life summarily consists, as was said of charity in sermon one. What then, we must ask, is the relation of charity and humility? Can we say more than that humility is in some way or other more than a tendency or an effect of charity? The notion "implication" is Edwards' word at this point for the difference between humility and the other virtues.

Edwards explains his strong claim that charity and gospel works "imply" humility simply by saying that charity "implies it as an essential qualification"; not only is it among love's effects. "True divine love is an humble love," he writes, it is "not true divine love" if it is "not an humble love."2 He writes humility into the "essential" meaning of divine love. Charity enfolds humility in a way that goes to the essence of each, even more than the interpenetration of long-suffering and kindness. One has not said all that needs to be said about the nature and meaning of Christian love until one has brought humility as a component into the heart of the matter. Humility as an essential qualification of charity is one and the same work of the Spirit, just as love to God and to men are not two gifts of the Spirit but one. In this sense, a humble love is the sum of all virtues. The reason humility lies deep in the heart of charity is that both are immediate responses to the loveliness of God, to his moral excellency.

Of course, charity still tends to produce humility. In the case of humility, however, implies and tends or causes are not clearly distinguished in Edwards' exposition. "If the knowledge of God as lovely causes humility," he writes, "then a respect to God as lovely implies humility." Seeing therefore that "a sense of God's loveliness... works humility, hence

-- 80 --

we may learn that divine love implies humility."3 Thus Edwards seems to infer from the tendency of God's loveliness to cause humility that it can be said of the nature of the virtue that it is implied by divine love. We could go so far as to say that divine love and humility co-imply each other in that the same Spirit works both in the heart, and one cannot be what it is without the other. A love that is not humble is, by that negation, no true love, not essentially a virtuous love.

The drift of Edwards' subsequent interpretation of 1 Corinthians 13 will be to enlarge upon this notion of "implication" and to amplify its role, both in sermon twelve on the "concatenation" of all Christian graces, and in the sermon on heaven. "Implication" also proved crucial, along with "fullness" and "participation," in the argument that God's end in creation was "but one."4 Here then— in a virtue's "essential qualification" of another virtue— is another moral progression corresponding to the story line of redemption.

Sermons on the next three texts— "Seeketh not her own," "Is not easily provoked," and "Thinketh no evil"— have in common a special concern with moral relations among persons. Left behind are the virtues of moral agents undergoing injuries or doing good and virtues respecting the goods or happiness possessed by ourselves or by others. Left behind are the more personal virtues. The focus of attention is now upon fruits of charity that are qualities inhering in men's attitudes toward one another. Edwards moves now to social virtues, to virtues of interpersonal relations. The move may be slight, but it is significant. While envy, for example, could wax so strong as to take the form of bitterness and hatred toward the person whose good or prosperity one envied, the vices brought

-- 81 --

now into consideration begin with the person, or with the agent's relation to persons. Before, even in pride and humility, the comparison focused on good belonging to oneself or another. Now, the vices of selfishness, anger, and censoriousness are directed upon persons themselves.

We can pass by the seventh sermon (on selfishness) since love of one's own happiness and other-love has already been considered as a theme running through all three ethical writings. We come then to the eighth and ninth sermons on an angry spirit and on a censorious spirit. It is, of course, a fruit of charity to mortify each of this pair of vices directly and immediately. But there is more to be said about the fabric of the virtues of love Edwards is weaving. At the beginning of the sermon on an angry spirit Edwards groups this with censoriousness and looks upon the pair from the viewpoint of his two previous sermons. These vices of anger and censoriousness, he says, are "commonly the fruit of pride and covetousness." "Covetousness" is now his word for a selfish spirit that blocks out all virtuous other-love. The word is appropriate, for Edwards means that a principle of sin in the heart disposes us to covetousness seeking our happiness in only our own. Selfishness (covetousness) joins pride as "fountains" of an angry spirit and a censorious spirit. Indeed, "those two lusts of pride and covetousness... are, as it were, two cardinal vices or fountains of sin and wickedness in the heart."5 As vices they have further fruits. So humility and uncovetous other-love are cardinal virtues that mediate charity's effects throughout the moral life by mortifying anger and censoriousness, which charity also works to mortify directly and immediately.

Edwards' sermon series is extraordinary as a phenomenological account of moral matters. The sermon on anger, in addition, indicates its remarkable moral psychology. I pick out only one point from among the many ways in which Edwards sorts out suitable anger from unsuitable anger. The anger that is contrary to a Christian spirit is anger without any end in view. Men who permit such anger to arise continue in it "without any consideration. Reason has no hand in the business. Their passions go before their reason." Such anger is "not the anger of men but of beasts. It is a kind of beastly fury, rather than any affection of a rational creature."6 This sort of anger can be compared to ire, the name passion of the group Aquinas called irascible. Ire alone among them is

-- 82 --

paired with no opposite passion toward good; even in respect to evil it spins on the spot and has no outcome.7

Here it is plain that Edwards means to say that the distinguishing fruits of love mix with natural morality— at least negatively. "If love be in exercise," he writes, "it will tend to keep men's irascible passions down in subjection, so that reason and love may have the regulation of them, and will prevent their being in inordinate degree, or of long continuance."8 Evidently Edwards believed that charity infused in the heart reaches and influences the passions in such fashion that men can attain the ends reason proposes.

Take ire, or continuing in anger to no end, and join it with envy, which is a comparative spirit directed not only to those who are superior to us in good or possessions but also to those near us or below us who may yet be our rivals— envy leading even to bitter hatred toward their persons. Or join irrational anger with censoriousness in the next sermon. This was a hellish way to live, according to Edwards. My words are carefully chosen in saying this. For there is a coda in Edwards' peroration on "Heaven Is a World of Love," in which Edwards depicts hell as no "world"— no society— at all.9 As heaven is a world in which creatures are united in love to one another in their love to God, so hell is a place where men are given up to their hatred of themselves and of one another in common but entirely divisive hatred of God. There is no uniting love there. They hate themselves and they hate one another. They torment themselves and they torment one another. Envy raised to the height of bitterness toward every other soul, censoriousness of everyone else and anger so undue as to have no telos in view, and so no finis, is their permanent plight.

The Doctrine of the ninth sermon defines a censorious spirit as "a disposition uncharitably to judge others." It consists in a disposition to think and judge evil of others with respect to three things: their state (saved or unsaved), their qualities, and their actions. My comment on this slender sermon can be brief, beginning with a summary of the tapestry of virtues Edwards has been weaving. Pride tends to selfishness; pride and selfishness (or lack of other-love) together tend to an angry spirit. So with judging uncharitably: "forwardness to judge and censure others shows a proud disposition"; pride tends to ill will to persons; "where hatred and ill spirit against others most prevail, there a censorious spirit

-- 83 --

does most prevail."1 Divine love in the heart mortifies censoriousness through this chain, and also directly. Thus, charity mortifies a censorious spirit mediately and immediately. Humility— the opposite of pride— does so also.

The position taken concerning judging others' "states" is a moderate one. "All positiveness in an affair of this nature seems to be without warrant from the Word of God. God seems to have reserved the positive determination of men's state in his own mind, as the only searcher of the heart, and trier of the reins of the children of men."2 The same is not true of actions: "Some men's actions give such clear evidence of the badness of their intentions that it is no judging the secrets of the heart to judge that their designs and ends are naught. Therefore all judging ill," Edwards concludes, "of others' state, or qualities, or actions, is not an uncharitable censoriousness."3 These statements show in the case of notably wicked actions a willingness to move from condemnation to action to censoring dispositions— and states (?). Issues such as these were to be involved in the church-covenant controversy that occasioned Edwards' dismissal from the church in Northampton.

Students of Edwards will want to note the tension in this sermon between knowing persons' qualities and states by their fruits and reserving to God the judgment infallibly concerning those states of soul. There is the same tension between this sermon and the next on holy practices, and by way of emphasis throughout Edwards' writings. Among the marks or signs of gracious affections, in general, the "certainter" the sign the less public it is. Nevertheless, greatest stress on openness to human judgment is to be found in the Twelfth Sign. Having shown that Christian practice is "a great and distinguishing sign," Edwards writes, "I may go further, and assert, that it is the chief of all the signs of grace, both as evidence of the sincerity of professors unto others, and also to their own consciences."4 Edwards returned to this worrisome problem ten years later than the present sermon, preaching in 1748 again on 1 Corinthians 13:8–13.5

-- 84 --

By the text of the tenth sermon, "Rejoiceth not in iniquity, but rejoiceth in the truth," Edwards understands the Apostle to say in effect, "I have mentioned many excellent things to which charity has a tendency, and shown how it is contrary to many ill things; but I need not go on to multiply particulars, for in short charity is contrary to everything in life and practice which is bad, and tends to everything which is good."6 Edwards defends his interpretation by citing the expression in 3 John 3: "thou walkest in the truth." Later on he reinforces this understanding by citation of other scriptures, including several from St. Paul. One example is the reference in Romans 2:8 to those who "do not obey the truth but obey unrighteousness." Later, he also supports his interpretation of this text by drawing attention to the word "know" in the Bible as meaning practical as opposed to speculative knowledge. "He who knows God sees him to be worthy to be obeyed."7 So rejoicing in the truth and knowing God are not dormant but vital principles in the heart tending to every excellent behavior.

Edwards' proofs in this sermon are from the active nature of grace and of "particular graces." While the immediate "foundation" of grace is in the understanding, its immediate "seat" is in the will; and "all the executive powers of... man, whether of body or mind, are subject to the faculty of the will."8 Later Edwards explains that, by locating the immediate "foundation" of grace in the understanding, he means in a conviction of the understanding; and conviction tends to practice. By conviction

-- 85 --

he means the understanding's judgment that some "great things" are real and true. Conviction, then, cannot fail to move men and much alter their behavior.9 So men's movement to love and to holy practices by the "seat" of grace in the will is, as it were, already under way in the certain conviction of the truth of the gospel in men's understanding of it, i.e., their "knowledge" of it.

The same conclusion follows from a correct understanding of "principle." "If grace be a principle," Edwards asks, "of what is it a principle but of action? Principles and acts are correlates... Thus the meaning of a principle of life is a principle of life which acts... So by a principle of sin is meant a principle whence flow acts of sin... And a principle of love is a principle whence flow acts of love. So when we say a principle of grace we mean a principle whence flow gracious actions... So it is absurd to talk of a principle that it does not tend to practice."1

Edwards' stress on practice is not surprising. More notable is the fact that he unhesitatingly spoke of "infused grace";2 and throughout the charity sermons, and elsewhere in his writings, exhibited his belief that infused grace and love become qualities of the soul issuing in other moral qualities or dispositions and in holy practices. He speaks of infused love

-- 86 --

and infused grace and infused virtues actually altering the quality of human hearts and life.3

This tenth sermon is transitional to the work of the Spirit imparting love that goes out primarily to God. So it seems appropriate that Edwards again discusses humility as a Christian virtue. While humility is only one of twelve particular graces that Edwards invokes to show that grace leads to holy practices, he again accords it a special place, as he did when this disposition was first viewed primarily as a source of virtuous behavior among men. He notes that the particular grace of humility is "abundantly recommended and insisted on in Scripture, and seems often to be spoken of as most distinguishing of true Christian virtue from that which is counterfeit."4 Here— and in what was said about humble love in sermon six— humility rivals charity in the heart as that which disposes to every virtue and right action. Humility is an "essential qualification" of charity, even as pride is an essential quality of the heart that lacks love. But pride is not love's opposite; that title is reserved for hatred alone. In both contexts, Edwards' thought can also be rendered by saying that humility is a fruit or effect of love that begets or causes other fruits or virtues, even as pride is a vice that begets or causes other vices as well.

Holy practice, however, is his present theme. If a proud and scornful spirit has been mortified, and "humility implanted in his heart in the room of it, surely there will be an alteration in the carriage and practice; for humility of heart is a principle which has as much tendency to practice as pride of heart has."5 Notice that in these words Edwards calls pride and humility principles of action. The same was said of divine love in the heart and, from this sermon on, will be said of grace in the heart. The same is not said so plainly of other particular graces.

Not too much should be made of the fact that of the twelve particular graces the word principle is used only of humility. For all are principles that must be principles of action. Edwards calls his discussion of these graces "an induction of practice." These graces are: a true and saving faith in Jesus Christ, the conviction of the understanding and judgment that is in saving faith, the act of will that is within justifying faith, true trust in God, true love to God, true and saving repentance, true humility, true fear of God, a spirit of thankfulness and praise of God, heavenly-mindedness, a spirit of Christian love to men, and a true and gracious

-- 87 --

hope. Notice that love to God and love to men are particular graces; the first sermon's "goings out" of divine love in the heart toward God and toward men are here listed as different loves. Also, the wording of this list entails— and Edwards says as much— that there are "false faith," "false trust," "false hope." That faith which is saving is to be distinguished from faith that is "common,"6 a true and gracious hope from its counterfeit. And— not mentioned in this sermon— legal humiliation is not the same as saving humility; there are a false fear of God, a sorrow for sin that is not saving repentance, a drivenness from the world that is not heavenly-mindedness.

More important to note is that we are moving along the story line of redemption toward the sermon on the concatenation of all Christian graces. Here holy practices are the tendency and effect of these graces or virtuous dispositions, as these presumably are the effects of the Spirit's working divine love in the heart. In addition to that, in the later, twelfth sermon the particular graces imply and are essential qualifications of one another. This has been said so far of only humility and charity. Simply reading Edwards' induction of practice, however, is enough to convey that these graces are qualities of heart that qualify one another; and that he has all but stated the concatenation or unity of the virtues as the possessor of them becomes "a perfect man" (Ephesians 4:13). And notice this is in a sermon that mainly summarizes— instead of continuing to itemize— virtues primarily directed to neighbors, and no more than points to greater absorption of moral life into love to God.

In proving that the particular grace of true love to God leads to practice, Edwards also appeals to natural loves as principles of action. "Love to creature objects, we see, does powerfully influence men in their actions and practice. Yea, what is it, which chiefly keeps the world of mankind in action from day to day, and from year to year, but love of some kind or other." Among the loves that make the world go round (love of money, honor), Edwards cites the love of friendship to show that "reason teaches that a man's actions are the most proper trial and evidence of his love." Thus, "reason... teaches all mankind that the most proper evidence of his being a real and hearty friend, as he professes, is his appearing a friend in his deeds." Edwards goes on to say— and this is worth more than a moment's thought— that a person's natural love of friendship will go so far as "to suffer in his private interest to do him a kindness."7 With the introduction of these latter words, the reader should

-- 88 --

be reminded of the sermon on "Long-Suffering and Kindness" and of the chapter in True Virtue on the instinctual kind affections. The convergence of divine love and the natural affection of friendship in long-suffering and kindness must mean that the same virtues may arise from either source or from both mixed together. This is the analysis of Christian moral life in the posthumously published dissertation, but generally not of Charity, where Edwards concentrated on showing the virtues to be specifications of charity. But here we have both kindness and long-suffering as natural and as infused virtues; and of the two, kindness surely lasts longer than this life of trial and tribulation. What else could be Edwards' name for the "answerable behavior" of the saints in heaven? So the love of friendship among "natural" persons is to be perfected and assumed into truly virtuous love.

Edwards' use of examples from ordinary morality in the charity sermons serves mainly to prove some point to be reasonable to believe, or for argument in the form of how much more so Christians ought to be or do. He uses natural loves as examples of the reasonableness of Christian practice from true love, sometimes as a positive measure of the sacrificial devotion to which neighbor-love leads. An exception to this is a final point worth noting in sermon ten, which now concerns a cluster of graces that surely are qualities of humility set over against natural or "pagan" morality. This may not be enough to show that Edwards' two treatments (so far) of humility replicates something of the larger movement through the chapter of moral progress in the history of the kingdom of Christ building up in the soul; but that, too, is worth pondering.

A possible movement in the nature of humility from the first table of love in transition to the second table— from humility among men to humility primarily before God— can be glimpsed by recalling Edwards' earlier praise of pagans who were eminent in virtue. No sharp contrast was drawn between their virtuous love of earthly causes and Christian virtues. Now, however, in the transitional tenth sermon, the eminence of pagan nobility is seen in a different light. We now see Christian humility stressed as a virtue in contrast to eminent natural virtues. Thus humility is elevated above the context in which Edwards formerly appealed to admirable natural virtue as an analogue to true love's fruits. Christian practices now are sharply distinguished from heathen, i.e., natural, morality. A chief component of this contrast is a "humble, meek, and self-denying, self-renouncing, and even heavenly walk and behavior." Measured by this, natural virtues come under a different assessment.

-- 89 --

Many heathens were very eminent for many moral virtues, and wrote excellently of them; as of justice, and of generosity, and of fortitude and others. But they were far from a Christian poverty of spirit, and lowliness of mind; they sought their own glory, and gloried exceedingly in their virtue, and said nothing about such a walk as the gospel commands, a walking in self-emptiness, poverty of spirit, self-diffidence, self-renunciation. And they said little of meekness, and did not own love of enemies, and forgiveness to be any virtue. Therefore, these peculiarly Christian virtues are called Christian virtues by way of eminence.

Ask yourselves, Edwards exhorted his congregation, "if you are essentially distinguished and different in your spirit from the mere moralist, or the heathen sage or philosopher," whether you have "a spirit of special esteem for and delight in these virtues that do especially belong to the gospel."8 There is a distinctive Christian morality, and there is a natural morality that Edwards did not despise by calling it heathen. So while grace in the heart disposes to every virtue and holy practice, there is a distinction in eminence to be drawn, for example, between justice, and humility or forgiveness. Some thinkers have developed and extended this distinction under the names of the theological and the natural virtues. By such a classification, humility was for Edwards a theological virtue, along with faith, hope, and charity. He was prevented from this hierarchical and more static view of the elevation and perfection of moral life by his view of moral progress within the narrative of the kingdom building up in the soul and among men.

Two triumphal sermons remain to be considered. "Believeth all things, hopeth all things" is the text of Edwards' twelfth sermon. A frequent misunderstanding (according to Edwards) is that by these words the Apostle meant that "charity disposes men to believe the best and hope the best of our neighbors in all cases." But he had done with that in the words "Thinketh no evil" (sermon nine). Just as the preceding words in the present verse, "Beareth all things," mean the grace to suffer all things in the way of duty to Christ (and not suffering long the ills done to us by our neighbors), so believing and hoping all things are fruits of charity "quite of another kind." And afterward the Apostle begins to speak of faith, hope, and charity— those "three great and principal graces of the gospel, which have God and Christ as their main and immediate object"— not our neighbors.9

-- 90 --

This introduction hardly prepares the reader for Edwards' stunning development of the Doctrine, "There is a concatenation of the graces of Christianity." He means more than that the Christian virtues hang one from another as links in a chain, or that they are interlinked in some other fashion. The reader should let Edwards' forceful rhetorical reiteration of the expression "concatenated together"1 make room for whatever meaning he puts into it. For no image of merely extrinsic connections can begin to convey the unity in plurality that this sermon teaches is the destiny of Christian moral life. And we need also to remember that in this sermon Edwards is still exhibiting Christian moral life actualized at a point in time within the story of redemption when, in his view, the church is still under tribulation. The next sermon has yet to treat of charity enduring under all things, all opposition that may be brought against it in this life; after that comes his sermon on "Charity never faileth" in heaven when all opposition has ceased. Still, in this sermon on "Christian Graces Concatenated Together," Edwards presents an extraordinary account of the concentration of the virtues, even during the church's militancy against opposition, in a life being made wholly new, and made unifiedly wholly new.2

The six "reasons"3 for the doctrine are noteworthy in that as a group they exhibit Edwards' master image of "from Alpha to Omega." This metaphor for cause/end is also reproduced in one or more of them. But we are further along the story line of the work of redemption. The graces are more closely woven than when we began with the first fruits of charity flowing out directly and immediately to our neighbors. From the beginning there was a certain unity among various virtues because they were not distinguished as excellencies of various faculties of the soul, but rather as specifications of charity and of the answerable will and loves of unitary moral agents. This ground of unity of the virtues Edwards now expresses to the highest degree in terms of redemptive grace that concatenates them all together— both the Christian virtues that parallel natural virtues and those that do not. Divine love believing and hoping all things of God and Christ reaches back and concatenates together all the fruits of charity in a moral life made unifiedly wholly new. The unity of Christian life also refers to far more than the three theological virtues— faith, hope, and charity— to which the Apostle turns next.

-- 91 --

By "concatenated together" Edwards means three things. All graces and virtuous dispositions (1) "go together, so that where there is one, there are all; and when one is wanting, all are wanting." But there is "not only a conjunction" among them. In addition (2) there is a "mutual dependence," a causal relation among them; one "promotes" the other. (3) "One is, in some respects, implied in the very nature of the other." One is "essential to another, and belongs to the essence of it."4

Essential implication is the interior meaning of concatenated together. This was said before of charity and humility. A sort of implication was also suggested in the transitional sermon by naming a number of graces or dispositions that are associated with or aspects of humility, and by listing that cardinal virtue alongside eleven other, lively particular graces that issue in practice. Edwards now states clearly and systematically that all the different graces of Christianity are essential to one another as qualities. This is true not only of faith, hope, love, and humility in the various combinations Edwards celebrates. Repentance and conversion, thankfulness and reverence, Christian patience and thankfulness are brought into the interchanges of divine love. They all are motions of faith, hope, and love, since each has God and Christ directly and immediately as object. So also is heavenly-mindedness moved by God's loveliness and excellency, as distinct from a weanedness from the world born of life's miseries. And each of the usually named theological virtues has these other essential ingredients in it (e.g., thankfulness), even as all mutually promote one another.

Not only so. The same is true of the virtues that have our neighbors directly and immediately in view (which latter certainly overlap with virtues in common or natural morality). Thus "a true love to God tends to love to men, who have the image of God," and the reverse: "love to the image cherishes love to the original."5 Christian love to men is "a kind of mediate exercise of love to Christ." And "that justice and truth towards men which are truly Christian graces have love in them, and it is essential to them."6 Natural virtues are also subsumed here. Edwards' fullest systematic account of principles of common or natural morality, and their fruits, that are admirable is in True Virtue. But in the sermon series and here in his twelfth sermon we have the fullest account of the abbreviated suggestion in True Virtue, that graces mix with those natural principles7 to direct, elevate, and perfect them in a moral life made new

-- 92 --

unifiedly. Edwards sums this up in his use of self-examination, which is one long exhortation to his congregation that they prepare their hearts for the unity in the manifoldness of Christian virtues. Try one by the other quality or character, is his repeated plea, for if one grace is wanting, nothing has begun to be made new. Remember that all vices are mortified together, or none are. As with the first birth, if any part is missing, there is a monster of a man.8 In conclusion: be not "like a cake [not turned], which is half baked, half raw."9

Before coming to Edwards' final exultation concerning God and the saints glorified together in heaven, there are two additional matters from sermon fourteen that need noting here, and which manifest the dramatic logic in his placing intervening sermons (two steps, as it were, in progress in the durability of divine love to its perfection in indefectibility) between "concatenated together" and "glorified together."

The first could pass entirely unnoticed, yet is not without some significance. In the text explication of the preceding sermon, Edwards had just repeated once again the line of demarcation drawn across the series at the tenth sermon on holy practice, as the Apostle's ceasing abruptly from itemizing fruits of charity primarily respecting our neighbors. Then, preaching his penultimate sermon— on things that shall have passed away when love alone lasts eternally in the redeemed community— he reached back to make connection with ver. 1 Corinthians 13:1–3 and with his second sermon on the extraordinary gifts that have long since passed away.1 This move traverses ver. 1 Corinthians 13:4–7 and, subordinately, has the effect of

-- 93 --

grouping these verses (formerly split apart at ver. 1 Corinthians 13:6) together into one unitary account of Christian morality in this life. Looking back on the church's pilgrimage, individually and collectively, every virtuous disposition is looked on as belonging to one continuous movement toward the ultimate end for which God created the world, without division into primarily this or primarily that respect.2 So, I suggest, holy practice respects not only our fellow creatures in this life. The saints above (being Puritans) are engaged in all manner of behaviors answerable one to another; they are not engaged simply in common contemplation and vision of God. There is then no great inconsistency in the shift of holy practice from the midpoint of the sermon series to its location as the final Twelfth Sign in Affections.

The second matter is more substantive for Edwards' theological ethics. From the beginning of the sermon series, the movement has been from God manward and returning to him. This is Edwards' master image or root metaphor. Divine love is a supernatural principle implanted in the heart. The ordinary saving gifts of the Spirit are qualities "inherent in the subject, making men's hearts and natures excellent." The Spirit "imparts himself to the soul in his own holy nature," "an indwelling vital principle in the soul."3 So Edwards used the term infused of divine love and all other graces. Thus the movement manward goes to the bottom of our being, there to effect a "turning." And in the movement through the sermons of return to God, who graces broken and fragmented men and their communities, we have traced the increasing durability (the virtus) of the virtuous against all opposition.

What now happens when we come to love's everlastingness in heaven? It seems to me that now Edwards' language is that of atonement accomplished in finality, the covenant of redemption once enacted now complete for all eternity. The "purchase" has been paid. The great promise the Father made to the Son to make him Head of a redeemed people is not now looked on as future in any of its effects. So the great fruit of the Spirit is now looked on as everlastingly "communicated" to the church.

-- 94 --

The Spirit indwelling in men becomes a "proper right." To divine love in the heart the saints have "sure title." Theirs is a "sure covenant" by which the Spirit is "made over" to believers, not only communicated in his own proper nature to them. Concerning saving grace, this implies "the continuance of it forever." A most telling expression of the indefectibility of that great gift in Edwards' penultimate sermon is: "Christ is become theirs, and therefore his fullness is theirs, his Spirit is theirs, the Spirit of Christ is their purchased and promised possession."4

Throughout the series, among people not yet made wholly new, the conflict has been between "other-love derived to them" from the self's love to self and "other-love derived to us" from our first being united with them in true affection as a specifying principle (working conjunctively with persons' general love of their own happiness). Now that struggle is over. In the description of the society of heaven in Edwards' final sermon, there are echoes of finding or placing our happiness in others, as Christ placed his glory in ours. But a most telling expression of the everlastingness of the end to which uniting love has come is Edwards' ringing rhetorical changes on "theirs." The language of the final sermon is that we have propriety in one another, belong to each other, are each other's own. All the saints are our possession, their happiness is ours, ours is theirs, even as Christ is become ours and theirs forever.

The fourteenth sermon's argument, then, is this: If there is in this life any quality inhering in the souls of men that endures under all opposition (sermon thirteen), that surely would be a senseless quality if it had exercise only against this— worldly opposition. Edwards' co-inhering argument for the perseverance of the saints in this life (their preservation) need only read the proposition just stated in the reverse direction: If it is true that "charity never faileth," as the Apostle testifies, then surely (looking back on earth) that same charity must endure against all opposition that can be brought against it in this life no less than it has strength to meet the demands of Christ in the way of duty and to suffer long and with kindness the ills that other persons do to us.

Suppose the saints might not persevere. Suppose grace in the heart could be overthrown. Then God might still be imagined to be engaged in some other sort of redemption. Generation after generation of men might be born to be reborn. As the Spirit works divine love in the hearts of some persons in every generation, others fall away. For we are supposing that there is only a fragile grace and love in their hearts. God could still be imagined to bring a people of his to the end of time. This could be

-- 95 --

so if God is imagined to be constantly mending the "broken links as well as he can, and be rectifying his disjointed frame and [the] disordered movements"5 of this church in every generation. As some fall out, others are graced and brought into some sort of continuing story of redemption. But then there would be nothing indefectible indwelling among men. There would be no surety that divine love lasts eternally. God's end in creation, providence, and redemption would not be inherently sure, but only contingently so (because he is powerfully good at mending fences). "[I]t must be so that there must be some way of influence, and some kind of fruit of the Spirit, which is unfailing and everlasting."6 Why must this be so? Because otherwise, creation would be end-less, or without end, redemption would be without its end, without an end in character with the story of it, without any end that God has, in the course of it, disclosed to the minds of men to be his purpose. Such suppositions were for Edwards suppositions contrary to reality. He never supposed that nothing of what men now have indwelling in their hearts has a durability that participates in God's own eternal purpose.

Not only is the preservation of the saints assured, as Adam's righteousness was not. Also their "holiness or spiritual image of God" may be vastly more perfect, by virtue of God's further manifestations of his loveliness and benefits, than was Adam's in innocency.

Adam's perfection in holiness did not render utterly impossible that he should love God more, let God make further manifestations of himself [whenever] he would, or whatever further obligations he might receive from God. 'Tis probable that some Christians have had higher exercises of love to God than ever our first parents had, and yet were exceeding far from sinless perfection which they had. The occasion we have to love God is now infinitely greater than that which they had. 'Tis probable that some Christians have had higher spiritual joys than ever Adam had.7

"Heaven Is a World of Love" must be read aloud to be appreciated. The reader may wish to do just that at this point. If commentary must intervene, the following may be of some help.

I begin with several questions, all arising from the problem of how to understand Edwards' striking statement that the communication of divine

-- 96 --

love "shall be, as it were, the only gift or fruit of the Spirit, as being the most perfect and glorious, and which being brought to perfection renders others, which God was wont to communicate to his church on earth, needless."8 That suggest, that the perfection of the righteous is a monadic unity, love alone remaining in glory. The question this raises is how to conceive the unity of the virtues in perfection. Moreover, on its face, the statement sharply divides this world from the next, rendering needless many fruits of charity so far considered. Finally, what of faith and hope if only charity lasts eternally?

Monadic unity cannot be Edwards' meaning, for "one alone cannot be beautiful,"9 in God, in heaven, or on earth. "One alone, without any reference to any more, cannot be excellent; for in such a case there can be no manner of relation no way, and therefore, no such thing as consent."1 This suggests— and the final sermon in its entirety confirms— that love is the only gift or fruit of the Spirit in heaven in the sense that all fruits, leaving aside those needed under trial and opposition, are gathered up perfectly concatenated together. The unity of the virtues in the church brought to "mature manhood" individually and collectively at which we have now arrived ought, perhaps, to be spoken of as their perfect "harmony." The singing of God's praise is heaven's delight; and unless the hearts of all heaven's hosts are to be open to one another's direct view, how they were to hear one another over such great distances, through what "medium," and by what faculties, so as to join chorus together, was a problem Edwards more than once addressed in his notebooks.2 The secondary beauty of music was "some image" of the excellency

-- 97 --

of heavenly society. Best of all, we need to keep in mind that the divine trinity was Edwards' paradigm.

Heaven does not consist in undifferentiated divine love that has left behind the virtues conceptually depicted throughout Edwards' exegesis of 1 Corinthians 13. Nor is heaven only the abiding place of "faith, hope and charity, these three; but the greatest of these is charity." All virtuous dispositions are subsumed in charity, elevated and perfected with it as graces that participate essentially together. Not only humility (from the first table of love) but also justice and truth (i.e., all holy practices) are assumed into Edwards' final paradigm of the perfection of creatures. In the society of heaven, all fruits of charity in heart and life— one work of one and the same Spirit— never fail. What fails is only the respect in which Christian dispositions and behaviors were determined by the church's state of militancy against opposition and when Christians were beset by temptation.

Some virtues are patently earthbound. These are left behind with earth's trials and tribulations, for example, long-suffering. But not its kindred virtue, cheerfulness and kindness to others. Bearing all things in the way of duty to Christ, rejoicing not in iniquity, and enduring under all opposition are also virtuous dispositions that have no business in heaven, because the occasion for them has ceased. But only the occasions are removed; human moral agents in heaven have in perfection still the dispositions tested in this life. So cheerfully doing good to others (which was the kindred spirit of long-suffering in face of injuries done to us), envying not, grudging not, vaunting not oneself, not behaving oneself unseemly, thinking no evil, rejoicing in the truth (all holy practices), believing all things, hoping all things are surely virtuous dispositions that are subsumed and perfected (actualized) in the divine love that alone lasts eternally. The sermon series exhibits all these as graces concatenated together even in this life.

The crux of the matter is that for Edwards heaven will be a world in which there remain comparisons that shall last eternally, but there will

-- 98 --

be no comparative spirit in that perfect society. Doubtless John, the beloved disciple, and the apostle Paul shall have precedence over all other saints in their discernment of the inexhaustible riches of God and in the perfection of their love of God and Christ. But everyone's love shall be "perfect in its kind," with a "perfection... proper to their nature," "commensurate with the capacities of their natures."3 Heaven is a world of love that does not require egalitarianism as the precondition of its peace.4

"All that whole society rejoice in each other's happiness." Where love is perfect, "the greater the prosperity of the beloved is, the more is the lover pleased and delighted. For the prosperity of the beloved is, as it were, the food of love; and therefore the greater that prosperity is [in those above us], the more richly is love feasted" with delight and complacence in the moral beauty of another. Love is "in proportion to the perfection and amiableness of the objects beloved." So those who are "highest in glory," "highest in holiness," shall be most beloved by all the saints. The saints of whatever rank shall "love those most who are most holy, and so they will all rejoice in it that they are most happy. And it will be a damp to none of the saints to see them who have higher degrees of holiness and likeness to God to be more loved than themselves; for all shall have as much love as they desire, and as great manifestations of love

-- 99 --

as they can bear."5 So heaven will be a world of love in which envy of those above or near to us or those below us who may yet threaten us will have no place. But charity's contrariety to an envious spirit (a fruit in this life) will be fulfilled.

Likewise there will be no pride there. For those angels, apostles, or saints above us in holiness will also be superior in humility; "for their superior humility is part of their superior holiness." "As some will have greater degrees of divine knowledge than others, will have larger capacities to see more of the divine perfections, so they will see more of their own comparative littleness and nothingness, and therefore will be lowest abased in humility."6 This final sermon is replete with vigorous terms and imagery stripping heaven of any blemishes people had in this life. Like all other objects in heaven, the saints are "all over lovely"; nothing vile or deformed infests heaven. Consistent with sermon six, however, the humility of even the superior saints must consist in both natural meanness before God's greatness and their comparative moral meanness before God's goodness and excellency fully revealed to them. The more sight and sense of God's glory, the more humble shall the most holy be.

As in this life, the more holy the superior saints are in humble love to God, the more they will be humble among their fellow creatures. So charity still promotes love to others directly, and mediately through humility. Edwards at once states this further point. Those who are highest will have more of a spirit of love to others. These superior saints "will love those who are below them more than other saints of less capacity" may be able to love others. Persons lower in discernment and love of God's inexhaustible excellency, Edwards says, "will be most beloved of those who are highest in glory, and the superior in glory will be so far from slighting those who are inferior, that they will have more abundant love to them, greater degrees of love in proportion to their superior knowledge and happiness; the higher in glory, the more like Christ in this respect. So that they will love them more than those who are their equals."7

Why more than their equals? Because more like Christ. Here surely Edwards moves from humility (which as comparative meanness should not be ascribed to God or Christ) to condescension, his not grudging us: these were his more exact and appropriate terms. The superior saints will be "the more like Christ in this respect. So that they will love them

-- 100 --

[the lowest of the inhabitants of heaven] more than those who are their equals."8 The superior saints will share more of Christ's condescension and will therefore ungrudgingly love those of lesser capacity— those with lesser degrees of charity— more than their own equals, more than themselves. When Edwards expresses his point here about humility/ condescension/grudging not by saying that the superior saints will look upon the lesser "as themselves," the reader should recall the explanation of "Love one another as I have loved you" as the "unveiled" meaning of the "as thyself."9 That earlier depiction in sermon seven of the Christian moral life in via is now fulfilled: the greater holiness of some saints in heaven is perfectly formed and directed by God's condescension and his love for the lowest. Therefore no one in heaven has reason for either envy or pride. Everyone there has all the love and happiness they can bear.

Earth's greatest need is satisfied. Here is Edwards on nature perfected: "Love there always meets with answerable returns of love. Love is always mutual, and the returns are always in due proportion. Love always seeks this. In proportion as any person is beloved, in that proportion his love is desired and prized."1 His proof of this point for all saints who in their degree love Christ with inconceivable ardor of heart is that he will "open to their view the great fountain of love in his heart far beyond what they ever before saw." This undoubtedly means that the ardor of human hearts shall be fully satisfied by the love of God and Christ now poured forth to them. But then, not surprisingly, Edwards says that this "love of God to the saints cannot properly be called returns of love, because he loved them first."2 It would, therefore, be impertinent for even St. John or St. Paul to claim that they receive from Christ love answerable or proportionate to their love to him, although they are fully satisfied and shall forever receive all the love they have capacity to receive.

Between them and other near saints there may be love answerable to love. Moreover it might be an answerable or proportionate love by which they are more beloved by saints of lesser degree. But we cannot understand the superior saints' fullness of love to everyone who is lesser in heaven without positing a transformation, perfection, and redirection of what Edwards means by the "answerable returns" of love. He says as much. "The nearer any saint is to him [Christ], the more he is like him in

-- 101 --

this respect, the fuller his heart is of love"3— with "first love," with condescending love, with love that seeks but does not depend upon the answering love it elicits. If the love of God to the saints cannot properly be called the returns of love, then the more the love of superior saints is like that of Christ, the more their love for the lesser cannot properly be called the returns of love. Yet in heaven divine love has become mutual. The higher saints' answer is to the outpouring of the love of God to them, and this suffuses the storied mansions of heaven. The returns of love, familial and other natural loves, golden rule interchanges, and the "as thyself" have been transformed, redirected, and perfected to accord with "love one another as I have loved you." Thus divine love is the mother of all virtues in that she conceives within herself the acts of them all, as Aquinas said.

"Uniting consent" of being to being has attained its goal. "And so the saints shall be Christ's, for he has bought them with a price, and he shall be theirs; for he who gave himself for them, will have given himself to them."4 To be related to one another is not enough; fiduciary loyalty is not enough. We might say, the returns of love perceived on earth to be wanted are not enough. To have "propriety one in another" is heaven's perfection.5 Placing one's happiness in that of others is no longer a needed intention. Finding one's own happiness in others' happiness is in no way contingent. "Other-love derived to self" from first being joined in affection with them is now effortless. The self's love of its own happiness has come to its own when, in heaven, we shall be each others' own.

-- 102 --

The established order of things is that the joy and happiness of all others belong to each and every sainted intelligent moral agent in perfect society.

Next Edwards introduces an essentially Augustinian point. The inhabitants of heaven "shall forever be continued in the perfect enjoyment of each other's love." They "shall be in no fear of any end of this happiness, nor shall they be in any fear or danger of any abatement of it." This "blessed circumstance"6 of the enjoyment of happiness is Edwards' equivalent of Augustine's argument that the summum bonum must also be immutabile. The good that entirely satisfies the human heart must not merely be the highest of all goods in rank of perfection. It must also, Augustine believed, be "something which cannot be lost against the will. For no one," he reasoned, "can feel confident regarding a good which he knows can be taken from him, although he wishes to keep and cherish it. But if a man feels no confidence regarding the good which he enjoys, how can he be happy while in fear of losing it?"7 Indeed, the higher the good the more sorrow, if it were a good that passes away, or a good we fear may pass away despite our heart's will and love of it. In heaven that sorrow is no more. Not only is the good itself eternal. There also shall be no abatement— Edwards adds— through weariness of will or cloyed enjoying faculties. Augustine's surety was in the immutable unchanging goodness of God. Edwards' surety was in the end of the work of redemption: Christ's enthronement as Head of his people made one and the marriage of the Lamb to his promised bride, the church. It is divine love that is the summum bonum et immutabile— et commune. In philosophical language, divine love is the ens realissimum et bonum— et perfectum. Both theologians agree: the most perfect would still be lacking unless it is also most real.

Last, we come to the question raised about the other "abiding" virtues, faith and hope. Are these among the gifts of the Spirit "God was wont to communicate to his church on earth," rendered "needless when love shall be, as it were, the only gift or fruit of the Spirit as being the most perfect"? Speaking of "the way to heaven" as "the way of love," Edwards' final sentence is: "And if ever you arrive at heaven, faith and love must be the wings which must carry you there."8 An alert reader is likely to raise the question whether faith and hope are not the wings. Did the Andover copyist err when he wrote "love"? I think not. The word

-- 103 --

"love" is entirely consistent with the thrust of this brief concluding paragraph on the way to heaven.9 Moreover, there is no intimation of an abiding hope in this sermon.1 To find a place for a hope that abides with love one has to turn to the perorations on progress in heaven in End of Creation.2

But what of an abiding faith? Apparently Edwards does not join those who hold that faith disappears in sight and hope in realization, while only charity lasts eternally. He does not use Dante's vision of faith and hope as two angels leading the soul— first one and then the other— into love's final union with God.3 Edwards surely believed that faith as conviction of the reality of divine things shall be in perfect exercise in heaven. He also believed— though the sermon does not tell us this— in an assured hope of the soul's eternal progress. There may be, of course, a deceptive faith and hope and love that the saints leave behind with their

-- 104 --

common ordinary gifts when they come to die, else Edwards could not have spoken of a false faith that fails, etc.4

These three theological virtues are subsumed in the same sort of concentrated unity in plurality as we have observed of every other Christian virtue. Commenting on 1 Corinthians 13 in his "Notes on the Bible," Edwards wrote that the three "cannot be properly three distributively distinct graces, or saving virtues, because charity or love is the sum of all saving virtue... Love is an ingredient in saving faith, and is the most essential thing in it, is its life and soul, and so it is in hope."5

The manuscript copy is written in a distinct and readable hand. The style itself is obviously closer to that of Edwards than Tryon Edwards' edition. The copyist did his work well— instructed as no doubt he was by his editor to lengthen Edwards' contractions ('em, 'tis, can't), to spell out Edwards' "chap." to "chapter" and his "ver." to "verse," to leave space where he could not read Edwards' writing or where the booklet was defective, etc. The copyist left spaces with some frequency. Most notably, Tryon's edition is needed to complete the third, fifth, sixth, and eighth sermons; and to fill in numerous gaps— sometimes running to several lines— in Andover. The three principal parts of the Puritan sermon form are (1) Text, and explication; (2) Doctrine, with reasons or motives; (3) Application or Improvement, in "uses" of instruction, of self-examination, of exhortation, of comfort, etc. From text through doctrine the sermon moved to practical application. Tryon subordinates these divisions, which the Andover copy correctly makes prominent by using all capital letters for Doctrine and Application, as was Edwards' practice.

Studies in seventeenth— and eighteenth-century English and American preaching have long since demonstrated that sermon form and style were more than literary conventions: they identified theological positions and often also ecclesiastical parties. Within a particular congregation the preacher's prescribed sermon form, and his marginal numbers, facilitated instruction and notetaking. TWO DISSERTATIONS. The letter of Hopkins to Bellamy on January 4, 1764, quoted above,6 throws light also on the collaboration of the two men in the transcription and posthumous publication of Edwards' papers. Having estimated that sixteen sermons combined with his Memoir would be enough to make two hundred pages, Hopkins continued, "I design to proceed in transcribing," i.e., additional unpublished works. Concerning this,

I hear nothing from Boston, which to me is unexpected but perhaps Kneeland and Searl can account for it. Is it not best for me to go down to Boston in February [i.e., next month!], if I hear nothing, or if from what I hear, it appears necessary that I should be there in order to forward the matter. As I may possibly prosecute this proposal, it will be needful that you send me that on Virtue the first safe opportunity, and the sermons you shall transcribe. Mr. Collins, I fear, will not return soon enough for this...

I have just received a friendly letter from Mr. Finley in which he expresses his zeal and good wishes, with respect to the proposed publication of Edwards' works, etc. I have finished that on the last End; should be glad to come down to get your assistance in reviewing and comparing it with the original, but if I go to Boston at the time proposed, it seems impracticable.

These passages indicate a division of labor in preparing Two Dissertations for publication, Hopkins assuming responsibility for End of Creation and Bellamy for True Virtue. We may surmise that Hopkins made the trip to Boston to "be there in order to forward the matter." To consult in person with Bellamy would require Hopkins to go a long journey, down from

-- 114 --

Great Barrington, Massachusetts, south to Bethlehem, Connecticut, where Bellamy lived, and then turn back northeast in order to go (as New Englanders say) "down" to Boston. It seems likely that Bellamy sent Hopkins "that on Virtue" by messenger, and that Hopkins went directly to Boston. If so, there was no time for the two to review together and compare their copies with the original dissertations.

Hopkins was clearly the leader in his and Bellamy's collaborative editorship.7 The preface to Two Dissertations (dated July 12, 1765) is signed simply "The Editor." I can observe no marked discrepancy in

-- 115 --

style, spelling, etc., that might be attributed to the fact that the dissertations were prepared for the printer by different copyists.

Most remarkable, and inexplicable, are the many differences between the original editions of Edwards' works printed in his lifetime by Kneeland in Boston and the text of this work published posthumously by the same house in 1765, only a few years later. It is not too harsh a judgment to say that a contemporary copyeditor or author would reject or radically correct the printer's proofs of Two Dissertations.

There is, it is true, regularity in the use of contractions: thro’, tho’, altho’, possess'd, consider'd, determin'd, design'd, obtain'd, it's. These contractions, which uniformly appear also in works printed in Edwards' lifetime, are replaced by modern spelling or punctuation in the present edition, since the pronunciation is the same. But words ordinarily capitalized, then and now, are lowercased in the first edition of Two Dissertations: holy scriptures, scriptures, christian divines, deists, jews, redeemer, creator, the father and the son, the apostle, judas, moses, and in at least one place, "jesus christ." There are signal discrepancies in spelling: virtue/vertue; inquire/enquire; meerly/merely; favor/favour; honour/honor; supreme/supream; publick/public. Once in a single sentence we read, "His rejoycing therein, is rather a rejoicing in his own acts...," while "rejoicing" is the spelling everywhere else the word occurs. There may, of course, be difference in stress where the original edition reads "independent on"; sometimes "not dependent upon"; and sometimes "independent of."

Some of these characteristics of Hopkins' (and Bellamy's) edition no doubt reflect Edwards' writing habits. After all, he reverently wrote the word "god" in lowercase. And we know that he changed his spelling to "rejoice." So the text may in some respects be a too faithful reproduction of Edwards' manuscript, without enough editing of the copies Bellamy and Hopkins made.

My sense is that there is less repetitious use of italics for the purpose of stressing again and again a concept once it was introduced in italics, than there is in, for example, the first edition of Freedom of the Will. But while this is the case for a considerable number of pages, the reader then comes to page after page sprinkled all over with italics. I do not know how to account for this. It is a fact, however, that in those days printers, who kept letters in italics in one box, Roman in another, would begin to use more italics or more Roman depending on which they saw running short. While I have reduced the use of italics in the present edition, I

-- 116 --

have not done so to the extent this was required in my editing of Freedom of the Will in the 1950s.

Indeed, I have introduced italics of my own to deal with a special problem. In Ch. II of End of Creation, on what is to be learned from Holy Scripture, Sec. III (on places in Scripture which show God's glory was his ultimate end) and Sec. IV (on places in Scripture which show it was for his name's sake and for his praise), the text waxes eloquent by the use of all capital letters for emphasis within Scripture verses quoted. Thus Philippians 2:11: "and every tongue should confess that Jesus Christ is Lord, TO THE GLORY OF GOD THE FATHER"; and Ephesians 1:5–6: "Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children— TO THE PRAISE OF THE GLORY OF HIS GRACE." In no case have I cluttered the page by retaining this excessive use of capitals. I have, however, often reduced one or more of the words the copy-text stresses in this fashion to italics within the Scripture verses, and in Edwards' text as well.

The excessive use of dashes is eliminated. There is one instance, however, of which I am fond, where the repetition of dashes is essential to the cadence and movement of thought. This is where, to show the meaning of subordinate and ultimate ends, Edwards wrote, "As when a man sells a garment to get money— to buy tools— to till his land— to obtain a crop— to supply him with food— to gratify his appetite."8

I come, finally, to the single most important difference between texts published in Edwards' lifetime (or between Original Sin, sent to the press by him) and this 1765 posthumous publication. This difference decisively supports an editorial decision toward which I was impelled, anyway, by the content and argument of True Virtue. In earlier works common nouns were all capitalized.9 Our editorial practice was to eliminate all such capitalization of common nouns.

In Two Dissertations, by contrast, common nouns are not capitalized. Among common nouns, only the word being is capitalized, and words following colons.1 (These practices have erratic exceptions, like the spellings above.) This means that the word being is not a common noun

-- 117 --

as it is used in Two Dissertations. In this instance, routinely to adhere to an editorial directive to eliminate excessive capitalization, which was devised for printed texts that capitalized all common nouns, would be a mistake. So in editing the text I retain "Being in general" and "Being, simply considered" in all instances; and also the capitalization of "Being" alone where it is obviously a reference to God.

Of course, I sometimes judge that lowercase should be used where the original edition uses a capital. This I do when being plainly refers to finite beings, and where the word is plainly used more as a metaphysical concept than as a reference to God. An example of the first is "the Being of beings" (not "the Being of Beings") in "For they must see that consent to Being in general, and supreme respect to the Being of beings, is most just."2 (That sentence is about the Day of Judgment! Before whom do men stand consenting or not consenting from their hearts to Being in general if not God?) An example of the second sort of use of lowercase being in my edited text is: "So that if all virtue primarily consists in that affection of heart to being, which is exercised in benevolence, or an inclination to its good, then God's virtue is so extended as to include a propensity not only to being actually existing, and actually beautiful, but to possible being, so as to incline him to give being, beauty and happiness."3 The first edition capitalizes "being" throughout that sentence; I do not.

Nevertheless, the word being (rarely a metaphysical concept only) most often serves as a reference to God in Two Dissertations, as surely as do Judge and Ruler (which are functional concepts, not names for God). It is true, Edwards never says outright, "Being in general (or Being, simply considered) is God." But Edwards does say that consent to Being is love to God. The whole point of the second chapter of True Virtue is to change the abstract language of the first chapter into biblical or theological language. The first sentence of the second chapter states its thesis: "From what has been said, 'tis evident that true virtue must chiefly consist in love to God; the Being of beings, infinitely the greatest and best of beings." If that is "evident," surely it is equally evident that Being, as Edwards very often uses the term, is God. The force is the same when Edwards says the same thing in reverse: "God himself is in effect Being in general."4 Even in the abstract language of the first chapter Edwards "gives notice" that when speaking of "an intelligent being's having a

-- 118 --

heart united and benevolently disposed to Being in general," he means by the object of such cordial unity no mere metaphysical entity or concept but "intelligent Being in general." Indeed, this must necessarily be the meaning of "being," capitalized or lowercased, since Edwards believed there was no substance, no beings, properly so called, except intelligent or mental beings. Immediately following the characterization of the object of virtuous consent as "intelligent Being in general," Edwards begins a discussion of the loves of benevolence and complacence; and, in this context, invokes the innertrinitarian love of Father and Son!

It is a sufficient defense of my retention of the capitals to argue simply that, where I do so, the word is used in the context as a reference to God, not as a name for God. But also Edwards believed on scriptural ground that "Being," or its equivalent in Scripture, was the name God gave himself and himself revealed to his people in the famous "I am that I am" passage. This was Edwards' interpretation of Exodus 3:14 when he was only nineteen years of age.5 That was still his view thirty years later when he wrote the "Controversies"— draft of True Virtue. In a paragraph6 not marked for use and not in the version left by Edwards for publication, Edwards wrote concerning God that "he possesses being in general, he is an all-comprehending being, his name is Jehovah I am or existence itself, absolute, universal, infinite existence, and all other existence is but a communication from him." When Edwards decided to limit his discussion to the creature's true virtue, to creatures' absolute or pure benevolence to Being (or supreme love to God), the paragraph containing the foregoing sentence was omitted from his final version, since it undertook to prove God's moral perfection and infinite virtue. The sentence containing the words "Jehovah I am" is preceded by "How evident it is that God must have such benevolence." And the immediately following sentence is: "Therefore, as he has benevolence to himself he has benevolence to being in general, and as the creatures are rays of existence from the infinite fountain of light they must partake of his benevolence." Given

-- 119 --

contemporary practice, Edwards himself would have capitalized both God and Being. More often than not, his usage was lowercase in both instances, with no less than supreme respect to "god" or to "being" (in very many or most of its occurrences in Two Dissertations), i.e., to intelligent willing Being itself, the only "capable object" of cordial consent.7

The Andover copy never places parentheses around Scripture citations at the end of the verse quoted. Instead, the copyist (faithful to Edwards' practice) reads: "I saw the Lord sitting upon a throne... and his train filled the temple, Isaiah 6:1." Omission of parentheses can, in addition, suggest to the contemporary reader that Scripture citations were probably voiced by Edwards for the instruction and notetaking of his congregation. The same is the style of the first edition of the posthumously published Two Dissertations. I adopt this common practice, only standardizing the punctuation and abbreviations. In addition, I ordinarily do not use final parentheses when myself citing scriptures in footnotes, for agreement's sake and also in order not to burden many a page with both parentheses and bracketed inserts. Of course, there was never any reason to use parentheses around Scripture citations introducing quotations; nor in the many instances where the citation is simply the middle part of Edwards' sentence, set off by commas.

Another decision favoring uniformity throughout the volume was not to spell out "chapter" and "verse" as did the Andover copyist. I use instead "ch." and "ver." The copy-text of Two Dissertations uses "ver."; its "chap." I shorten. Thus "ch." and "ver." are used throughout this volume for consistency's sake. Whether the reference is to one or to more than one chapter or verse needs no other indication than the one or more following numbers. I also use "par." for one or more paragraphs. The reader should note that the sections of End of Creation are main units corresponding to the chapters of True Virtue.

My copy-texts (and Edwards himself) use "Gen.," for example, as a class name for a sequence of chapters in Genesis connected by "and" until one comes to a period. Then another book may begin to be cited in its order in the Bible. Using "Gen." as a class name, "Genesis 3" could then become a subclass name for verses following in sequence, requiring an "and" before going on to, say, chapter Genesis 24. Thus, "Genesis 3:5–7, Genesis 3:9, Genesis 3:12 and Genesis 24:13." I am persuaded that Edwards (and readers also) thought "Genesis 3" at each enumeration when he set down Genesis 3:1–7, Genesis 3:19–20 following that citation. This was not a nervous habit. (The latter excess I shorten to Genesis 3:1–7, Genesis 3:19–20.) These features of the texts, no less than

-- 120 --

Edwards' listing of biblical citations in the order in which the books appear in the Bible, I called the "concordance" character of his writing. I retain as much of this feature as seems feasible. However, I use semicolons instead of the periods in references such as "Isaiah 42:12 and Isaiah 64:1–5. Jeremiah 31:10. John 12:17, John 12:19 and John 15:1, " in order to avoid an entirely novel style of punctuation.

Perhaps I should say, finally, that in fidelity to our literary heritage and to the integrity of the English language, for accuracy and for fluency in reading Edwards, I use "men" interchangeably with "creatures," as he consistently did.

In Miscell. no. 530 JE contrasted "compounded self-love" with what he called "simple mere self-love." He gave a somewhat confused account of the latter. On the one hand, "simple self-love" was so called "because it arises simply from... the nature of a perceiving willing being." With no more said, such simple self-love would be equivalent to that general capacity or universal meaning of self-love amply endorsed in this entry. On the other hand, "simple mere self-love" is "a man's own proper, single and separate good," and this selfishness is said always to be "what arises simply and necessarily from the nature of a perceiving willing being." Love to God can't be superior to the former, while superior to the latter, "love to God can and ought to be." "Simple self-love" is, to say the least, ambiguous.

He is mistaken, however, in taking "compounded self-love" to be "in an intermediary position in Edwards's theory" (p. 157), a "necessary balance between self-love and social love" (p. 158; cf. p. 167), only "an enlarged sense of one's own good, which may include others to greater or less degree" (p. 159), consequent upon a line of reasoning later called "enlightened self-interest" (p. 160). Fiering then must deem the charity sermons to be idiosyncratic among JE's works, forgetful of his "earlier differentiation [in Miscell. no. 530] of three types of love: simple, compounded and holy" (p. 172). Instances of Fiering's natural, compounded self-love or natural affection (pp. 158–59) allow inroads of "social love," which is love to others from an extended egotism. So he reads the several principles explanatory of the ordinary morality of perceiving willing creatures in True Virtue as, to say the least, too readily overwhelmed by the inroads of nefarious self-love. The other principles of natural morality are stripped of their variety, richness, and mutual support, if not altogether reduced to a single foundation in selfishness.
The alternative is that, when writing Miscell. no. 530 in his nineteenth year, JE already had in place his understanding of universal self-love as a general capacity of willing or delighting in anything, and that this is the meaning to be ascribed to "simple mere self-love." Without this reading, it is difficult to understand the corollary of this entry, that it is "impossible for any person to be willing to be perfectly and finally miserable for God's sake," or for him to love God supremely and have that be "further informative" of his own particular happiness. My reading of Miscell. no. 530 is consistent with JE's argument in True Virtue, that our hearts must first be united to others in affection; then their happiness becomes ours. The other meaning is full integrity and wholeness of will, which for JE was true or "virtuous sincerity." See also the section on sincerity in Miscell. no. 1153. MORAL INABILITY. FREE WILL. If this seems strange, illustrations from the Oxford English Dictionary may help. Sincere means (1) not falsified or perverted in any way; genuine and pure in the case of doctrine; uncorrupted and uncontaminated in the case of motive ("Keeping them pure and sincere against temptations," 1649); of material things, colors, or substances; pure, unmixed, free from any foreign element; unadulterated, genuine. The "sincere fat" in a bloated body, "sincere gall" issuing from an incision, and "wood is cheap and wine sincere outside the city gates" illustrate these meanings. Likewise, sincerity means (1) freedom from falsification in the sense of adulteration or alloy; purity, correctness ("the sincerity of the Hebrew text"; "profess the sincerity of the Gospel") or (2) freedom from dissimulation or duplicity; honesty, straightforwardness. While the second of these meanings is more common today, the two seem to run along together through most of the history of the English language. 4. About seven or eight years before composing the charity sermons JE explored another possible image of heaven, as an interchange of various graces, a community of specialists:
As there will be various members of different degrees in the body of Christ in heaven, so it seems to me probable, that there will be members of various kinds and different offices, as it is in the church on earth, 1 Corinthians 12. That is, there will be some especially distinguished for one grace, others for another; some of one manner of the exercise of grace, others of another; some more fitted for this work others for that. Everyone will have their distinguishing gift, one after this manner and another after that, the perfection of the saints in glory nothing hindering; for that perfection will not be of such a kind, that one saint may not be more eminent than another in grace, or that they shall not be capable of increasing, and so attaining to higher degrees, nor that one grace in the same saint shall not have a more remarkable and eminent exercise than others. And 'tis most probable, if it be so, that they shall excell most in the same graces, and the same kind of works, by which they were most distinguished on earth, God rewarding their graces and works by giving them grace more abundantly of the same kind; as Christ has promised, that to him that hath shall be given. This difference will be for the beauty and profit of the whole: they will profit one another by their distinguishing graces; with respect to those graces, they will not be beyond being profited by one another, as well as delighted; they will still be employing and improving themselves. [Miscell. no. 430. HEAVEN, written shortly before JE preached his first published sermon, God Glorified (1731)].
This was one understanding of the beauty and proportion of one thing to another in heaven. The hierarchy of Sermon Fifteen (Augustine's "many mansions") was another. Common to both was the primacy of society. ↩

3. Alongside the suggested comparison with Dante, Calvin may be of interest here. In opposition to those who proposed that we are saved by "formed faith," faith "formed" by love the "greatest" of the three theological virtues— Calvin framed an extreme counterexample: "Let us imagine that love excels faith in all respects: what man of sound judgment indeed, what man of wholly sound mind— would reason that it justifies more?" That "is as if someone argued that a king is more capable of making a shoe than a shoemaker is because he is infinitely more eminent. This one syllogism gives us ample proof that none of the schools of the Sorbonne has even slightly tasted what justification of faith is" (Institute of the Christian Religion; ed. John T. McNeill [2 vols., Philadelphia, Westminster, 1960] III, xviii, 8, pp. 829–30). He also opposed what he understood to be the teaching of the Schoolmen that love is prior to both faith and hope as "mere madness; for it is faith alone that first engenders love in us" (ibid., III, ii, 41, p. 589). Thus Calvin appears to believe that faith is first of all the foundation of hope, and that faith and hope belong together in having their "seat" in the mind. Each is a different conviction of the mind concerning truth (believed or hoped for) (ibid., III, ii, 42, 43, pp. 590–92). Faith is the substance of things hoped for (Heb. II: I), yet a patient hope nourishes and buttresses faith. The point to be made about "the greatest of these is charity" is that JE believed no less than Calvin in sola gratia. After all, in the "Treatise on Grace" (c. 1741) JE expressed in terms of grace exactly what he said in the first charity sermon in terms of divine love. Despite JE's stress upon the will and the affections, he no more tore human nature to pieces setting heart against head in matters of religion— than Calvin tore Christ to pieces by separating between his two works of justification and regeneration, by which Christ's righteousness is imputed and imparted to men. Edwards stood well within the classical theological tradition of the orientation of the mind upon truth or reality and of the will (or affections) upon good or excellency. While the seat of divine love is in the heart, the first impress of the Spirit is a more than notional conviction of the truth of things in the understanding (Charity, p. 139). One of the distinguishing signs of truly gracious affections is that they "are attended with a reasonable and spiritual conviction of the judgment, of the reality and certainty of divine things." Affections, p. 291, Fifth Sign. ↩

7. Edwards A. Park, in his Memoir of the Life and Character of Samuel Hopkins, D. D. (Boston, Doctrinal Tract and Book Society, 1854), ascribes the lead to Hopkins, but rightly stresses the collaboration of Bellamy, Hopkins, and Edwards during many years prior to JE's departure from Stockbridge:
Edwards had written the Dissertations three years, at least, before his death. He had made a public announcement of his intention to publish, soon, the Treatise on Virtue. The main idea of that treatise he had developed in college life; and had matured it during forty years of study. It was, therefore, incumbent on Hopkins to prepare it as soon as possible for publication. Both he and Bellamy had reviewed, in company with Edwards, the Treatise on the End of God in creating the World; and there is no doubt, that the Treatise on Virtue had been the topic of earnest consultation among these three friends. Hopkins, especially, was so intimate with Edwards, and withal so inquisitive, and eager for information, that he must have ascertained the opinions of his teacher with regard to the practical bearings of the theory... He spent much of his life in defending and applying this theory of virtue. No man had enjoyed so signal an advantage for learning the varied uses which Edwards would have made of it.
This led Park to observe (concerning what Hopkins' preface calls the "brief hints or sentiments, minuted down on loose papers found in the manuscripts") that "if they had been at all inconsistent with the main doctrine of the Dissertations, his honesty would have prompted him to publish the fact. But he knew the contrary" (pp. 218–19).
There is a second letter from Hopkins to Bellamy that is noteworthy, dated April 4, 1768. I have a letter from Mr. Gordon, informing that Edwards on liberty is going through another edition and his letter to Mr. Erskine, relating to Lord Karnes' Essay, etc., is to be printed at the end of it. Edwards' Life, Sermons and Dissertations which I sent to Scotland last year on Mr. Hogg's desire, are ordered to be returned with 40 sterl. cost upon them, with a letter from Mr. Erskine informing that Mr. Hogg is dead and some others of the subscribers, that books are so dear beyond proposals and come so late, that they will not be taken off so are sent back! I am told few of impressions have gone off. Mr. Kneeland's house is full of them, which must soon be sold for waste paper. I believe your letter to Blair about reprinting Sermons, will take no effect. [Printed with permission of the Presbyterian Historical Society, Philadelphia, Pennsylvania]
It seems, then, that the undertaking of Hopkins and Bellamy to publish posthumously an indeterminate number of Edwards' manuscripts ran into trouble rather quickly on both sides of the Atlantic. Park (ibid., p. 219) remarks, "After preparing several other works of Edwards for the press [in addition to Two Dissertations], the disheartened editor became satisfied that they would not be sold, and he therefore turned his mind to other projects," i.e., to Hopkins' own system of divinity. An unfaded, uncut, unbound copy of Two Dissertations— perhaps one of the impressions Kneeland sold for wastepaper— was purchased several years ago by Wilson Kimnach from an antique dealer in Windsor, Connecticut! Jonathan Edwards, Jr., was soon ready to take over from Hopkins and Bellamy and make the second effort to publish more of the large manuscript collection Edwards left to Sarah and to a family line of executors. ↩
