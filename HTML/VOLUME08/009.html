APPENDIX II
JONATHAN EDWARDS ON MORAL SENSE, AND THE SENTIMENTALISTS

EDWARDS' references to Francis Hutcheson in late entries in his "Miscellanies"— there are only three— do not support the notion that his principal aim in writing True Virtue was to refute Scottish "moral sense" philosophy. These entries are Nos. 1289, 1294,1 and 1356. The first is of no consequence. In it Edwards simply copied without comment a brief passage (89 words) from "Hutcheson on the Passions. Edit. 3. p. 191." The title Edwards gave this entry— FUTURE STATE— is by itself sufficient to indicate what was his interest when he copied. The passage is on the possibility of a life after death, so "interwoven with all religions" that not even "the boldest Epicurean" could ever "bring the lightest appearance of argument" against its possibility.2

The remaining two entries also copy passages from Hutcheson. In each of these cases Edwards' commentary as well as his titles tells us the use he meant to make of Hutcheson.

1294. HELL TORMENTS. THEIR EXTREMITY. THE JUSTICE OF IT. Hutcheson's Enquiry concerning moral Good and Evil. Sect. 2. p. 140, 141. Edit. 4. "'Tis true indeed all the passions and affections justify themselves while they continue (as Malebranch [sic] expresses it). We generally approve our being thus affected on this occasion, as an innocent disposition or a just one; and condemn a person who would be otherwise affected on the like occasion. So the sorrowful, the jealous, the compassionate approve their several passions on the apprehended occasion.3

To that quotation Edwards adds only a one-sentence commentary:

-- 690 --

"These things may lead us to see the reason why men don't see that sin deserves so great a punishment." This is consistent with Edwards' view, expressed in True Virtue, that men's minds are cleared of self-justifying inclinations on the Day of Judgment, so that they can then see that God's sentence on unregenerate inclinations of human hearts is just.4 But an intention on Edwards' part to mold Hutcheson's words into a plausible psychological explanation of why, during this time of earthly trial, men generally approve their present passions, why they generalize that into their approvals or disapprovals of others, and why they are so blinded to the punishment sin deserves would be equally consistent with a use of the passage from Hutcheson on a topic only remotely related to "moral sense." JE meant to use the quotation when writing on the justice of "Hell torments."

The final reference to Hutcheson is one of several disjointed paragraphs on the last page of an otherwise consecutive, well-written, and quite lengthy composition, Miscell. no. 1356— fifth from Edwards' last entry in these notebooks. Following one brief sentence from Hutcheson, Edwards' comment is a note to himself concerning a future possible treatise.

Hutcheson on the Passions, p. 77, 3rd ed., says, "No misery is further the occasion of joy to a sedate temper than as it is necessary to some propollent happiness in the whole." Particularly to examine this matter, and inquire whether there be not something in the natural sense of desert which God has implanted in creatures that are moral agents which tends to acquiescence in the pain or suffering of the ill-deserving not merely from a natural desire of good to ourselves or others, or good to the universal system, but as what a sense of desert naturally tends to as a gratification of that sense.5

Again, the argument Edwards sketched is consistent with his account of a sense of justice or desert in True Virtue,6 a sense not derived from natural self-love extended through experience into love of "some propollent happiness in the whole." But the argument of that dissertation was certainly fully written out long before this entry at the very end of the

-- 691 --

"Miscellanies." Besides, we know for what purpose Edwards copied the passage from Hutcheson. Looking for the title to the entry we find it has none; it begins instead with "Join this with p. 120." That leads back to the end of a previous lengthy entry, No. 1348, entitled eternity of hell torments. The two numbers taken together (as Edwards directed) constitute one continuous draft (67 pages in Schafer's transcripts) of a treatise on the topic announced.7 Indeed, shortly before quoting from Hutcheson near the end of the second of these two connected entries, Edwards noted plans for such a treatise in a characteristic, incomplete paragraph:

METHOD. In a treatise of future punishment to show when will be the season of it, and here prove particularly that it will begin with death. And consider the state of the wicked in their separation from their bodies, and then show how the day of judgment is the proper season of a punishment, etc.— 8

Evidently Edwards' restless mind was already turning to the topic of another treatise, which he was drafting in these entries. Each of these late references to Hutcheson falls within this context, not that of Two Dissertations, which must have been substantially finished. Certainly the context was not "moral sense."9 Miscell. no. 1356 was probably written

-- 692 --

late in 1756 or even in early 1757, i.e., not long before Edwards moved to Princeton, while the first quotation from Hutcheson, in No. 1289, was likely not earlier than 1755 or 1756.

The evidence simply does not exist that "in the 1750s he [Edwards] pored over Hutcheson's Inquiry and his Essay on... the Passions and Affections with Illustrations on the Moral Sense."1 The evidence we have shows that,

-- 693 --

even if Jonathan Edwards was avidly reading Hutcheson over the years between the penultimate draft (1753–54) and the final version of True Virtue, this was not to take aim at him on "moral sense." Edwards had his own concept of "moral sense" formulated, we shall see, long before he, late in life, thrice used the term in writings other than True Virtue.

The passage in Original Sin referring to True Virtue "lying by me prepared for the press" used "moral sense" only once, at the beginning. Later in the passage, however, Edwards adopted a different term: "a native bent in mankind to mutual benevolence."2 Between these expressions, Edwards variously characterized those whose objections to the doctrine of original sin have still to be fully answered, e.g., "the reasons they offer from experience, and observation of the nature of mankind, to shew that we are born into the world with principles of virtue." I have already raised the question whether those who rest true virtue upon reason— for example, George Turnbull, treated at length in Original Sin— can be sharply distinguished from those writers whose objections had yet to be "particularly considered."3

There is a second use of "moral sense," in Miscell. no. 1340. There Edwards wrote: "the dictate of the natural, common and universal moral sense of mankind, in all nations and ages." A note attached to that statement refers to Matthew Tindal: "which moral sense is commonly included in what Tindall means by reason and the law of nature."4 The topic of this entry is the necessity of revelation; it begins by citing Tindal's Christianity as Old as Creation, noting that book's exaltation of reason.5 Tindal's work never uses the expression "moral sense"; yet Edwards

-- 694 --

used it broadly to summarize Tindal's view! It can hardly be denied that the same might have been his characterization of George Turnbull or John Taylor, whom Edwards also understood to be rationalistic optimists, not sentimentalists.

The third, late use of the expression "moral sense" before the posthumously published dissertation is in the fourth and final paragraph of the "Controversies"— draft The Mature of True Virtue (1753–54), whose brown ink indicates it was added onto this version at some later date.6 The title of this paragraph is MORAL SENSE. SENSE OF JUSTICE. CONSCIENCE. This "sense men naturally have of justice" in conscience is exactly Edwards' use of the term in True Virtue. Here, too, there is verbal correspondence between the passage of the draft (whose title links "moral sense" with justice and conscience) and the published dissertation's chapter "Of Natural Conscience, and the Moral Sense."7

All these uses of "moral sense" were late in Edwards' life. He was reading Hutcheson, gathering material for another treatise. There is no reason to deny that he seized upon the term in Hutcheson— or took it from the Hutchesonian views abroad in the Atlantic community of

-- 695 --

learned discourse. To claim more than that is to venture rashly into unknown territory and to wander along footpaths no one left. The important thing to keep in mind is that "moral sense" encompassed a host of Edwards' opponents.

We can locate his adoption of the term, if adoption it was. On MS p. 185 of the "Controversies"— draft and integral to the main body of that version, Edwards formulated his thesis concerning one of his four principles of natural morality as follows: "Hence natural conscience or that moral sense that all intelligent creatures good and bad [have] is not the same with a love of true virtue as such." The expression is used twice in this paragraph, which is a "corollary" to the second of two earlier paragraphs expressly discussing conscience. In True Virtue the parallel adoption of the term is on p. 595 above. But in the published dissertation little else of this corollary appears. The reason seems to be that, in the early draft, proof of the thesis ("For if it were it would be a love to true and pure benevolence to being in general") was in terms of the abstract metaphysical language of Ch. I and Edwards wanted to use God-language in his later chapters. In any case, men's "relish" for beauty is treated at far greater length in the earlier draft than is conscience; and nothing Edwards wrote before the published dissertation forecasts the most striking feature of its chapter on conscience: self-union and self-contradiction, to which, with desert, the expression "moral sense" is finally attached.

We need an account of the genesis of True Virtue alternative to the Hutchesonian one and alternative to the minimalist "adoptionist" theory at which we have now arrived to explain Edwards on "moral sense." Such an account will render it plausible to believe that, if Edwards and the sentimentalists did not independently invent the expression "moral sense," nevertheless Edwards' analysis of morality, indeed the moral language he used all his life, made him ready to adopt the term whenever he might hear it or first read it.8 The evidence for such an interpretation exists in Edwards' various notebooks and becomes convincing when these are brought together and the various entries studied in chronological order. A brief sketch of the possibilities this offers can be presented here.

-- 696 --

Among the planning paragraphs at the beginning of the "Controversies"— draft is one which reads: SPIRITUAL TASTE and Natural Men's MORAL TASTE. After one more line on "method," the body of this penultimate version of the dissertation states the proposition: "That MORAL TASTE that is naturally in men is not of the nature of true virtue and essentially differs from [it]." This is the thesis of True Virtue in terms of "taste." Then the 1753–54 draft launches a series of six numbered paragraphs that are substantively and often verbally identical with the posthumously published dissertation. Under the head of natural "moral taste," par. 1 and par. 2 use the expression "natural conscience." Par. 3 is concerned exclusively with our taste for natural beauty, which is an instinct we have in common with beasts and serpents, not the same as moral beauty. Par. 4 links "moral taste" with taste for external beauty. Edwards is already arguing that this is "some image" of true beauty: "so there appears some degree of that natural sense itself which our new philosophers [!] call moral taste in men and assert to be a virtuous principle." This paragraph goes on to use "moral taste" and/or "moral sense" for a "sense of desert" in explanation of natural principles of anger and gratitude, so far as these are not derived from self-love. Then is not "moral sense" a replacement term for "moral taste," meaning desert? In reference to the sense of undeserved injury said to be in anger, Edwards says, "allowing this, such a moral sense— if it be proper to call it by that name— is in some degree in many of the beasts."9

Still under the head, "That MORAL TASTE that is naturally in men is not of the nature of true virtue and essentially differs from [it]," par. 5 and par. 6 have as their topics the approbation of conscience. Then at the end of par. 6 there is a reference forward— over much intervening material— to the corollary where Edwards adopts the term "moral sense" for natural conscience with no qualification or hesitation (as in True Virtue).

In our excavations we have now caught a glimpse of the intellectual

-- 697 --

genesis of True Virtue. We see "conscience" becoming the name for "moral taste." Moreover, the substance of whole paragraphs of the "Controversies"— draft on moral taste, or on our sense of beauty in moral relations (with or without the term "moral sense"), were used in True Virtue's discussion of conscience. (These have been identified in notes to the text above.) In this draft Edwards evidently was trying out his several themes; we catch his analysis in motion. Some of his elaboration of themes will later go to secondary beauty, some to self-love, some to conscience. All are sensibilities, in different ways; all are senses, even "instincts." But Edwards has decided which one should most aptly be called "moral sense."

Edwards had long contrasted natural men's moral taste with spiritual taste. In the "Controversies"— draft we have observed things to be said about conscience emerging from things stipulated of this natural moral taste. Taste was surely a sense. "Moral taste" was the topic of many of Edwards' "Miscellanies" from early youth, too many to be analyzed here. It is sufficient to say that the notion of a sense or senses other than the five senses that perceive our physical environment was never absent from Edwards' thought.1

Conscience itself was always a sense of some sort, specifically called that in Edwards' earliest consideration of the topic. Early entries in the "Miscellanies" treat natural conscience as an objective apprehension of (1) right and wrong and, associated with that, a sense of (2) the fitness of retribution or of others' disapprobation. The warning voice of conscience is not the self's sense of disunion. It is rather a sense of being judged from beyond oneself. In Miscell. no. 471 (c. 1730–31) on the Spirit's assistance of natural principles, for example, Edwards wrote, "The work of natural conscience that it doth of itself, is to give an apprehension of right and wrong, and to suggest to the mind the relation that there is between right or wrong and a retribution."2 To those words he directed that No. 472 be added: "And indeed, the whole work of natural conscience seems to consist in the latter; it gives no other notion to natural men of right and wrong, but only as it suggests the relation or adaptedness there is between such and such things, and a being hated by others and having evil brought upon them [selves]." The rest of this brief entry softens the notions of retribution and of hatred into the "agreeableness"

-- 698 --

between certain actions and others' mere approbations or disapprobations.

We may note a remarkable parallel and a remaining difference between the notion of conscience in these passages and Hutcheson's proposition (distinguishing moral and natural good or evil), "That some Actions have to men an immediate goodness; or, that by a superior Sense, which I call a Moral one, we approve the Actions of others and... are determined to love the Agent; a like Perception we have in reflecting on such Actions of our own, without any view to natural Advantage from them."3 It is approbation or disapprobation that resounds in conscience. The difference is the primacy for Edwards of conscience's inner sense of "being disapproved," of retribution coming upon the self— or of objective desert. Reference to others' approval could be to culturally relativistic evaluations, of course. That was one way "the mind might be sensible that anything is good or excellent": "'Tis a being merely convinced in judgment, that a thing is, according to the meaning of the word, ‘good’, as the word is generally applied" (this view Edwards rejected in related Miscell. no. 489, cited at the end of No. 472). The mind is sensible of good in another, quite realistic sense that is still a "sensibleness." It is interesting that the final chapter of True Virtue confronts once again the question of cultural relativism and moral language.

Miscell. no. 472, which we have been considering, is also cross-referenced to No. 623, concerning the "most perfect exercise" of natural conscience. The consciences of the devils and the damned "are not stupid but thoroughly awakened and fully do their office," unassisted. These have the most clear sense of desert; their conscience is "nothing else but a sense of the equality and answerableness" of God's displeasure and their sins. This entry was marked for use or when used. Its location of the most perfect exercise of natural conscience should be compared

-- 699 --

with the ending of True Virtue's, chapter on conscience,4 where also the sense of desert (disapprobation, retribution) is the "second" element of conscience which, with the "first" formal element of disunion, is finally named "moral sense"— no ifs, ands, or buts.

It is important to notice that Edwards himself marked the development of his early thoughts on conscience by linking together entries 471, 472, 489, and 623— probably retrospectively in the case of the last two. And No. 472 picked up a minor theme in No. 471, whose main topic was the various assistance or influences of the Spirit on both natural and regenerate man. Edwards abstracted natural conscience from the Spirit's work (as I have) for development in the subsequent numbers. The title of No. 471 is SPIRIT'S OPERATION. CONVICTION. CONVERSION, or what could be called "spiritual taste." The identical contrast was later stated in terms of "conscience" and "spiritual sense" in Miscell. no. 1183 (mid-1751) whose title is "CONSCIENCE in Natural Men. SPIRITUAL sense." Here, men's natural conscience concerning the "moral good and evil of their own actions" is summed up in "the dictate of their minds[!], concerning their treatment of others," by the test of exchanging "place and circumstance" with them. There is no explication of "spiritual sense" in this entry; only the name.

This brings us, finally, to two relevant notes on "The Mind." We are now at the deepest level of our sounding, earlier than the "Miscellanies" just reviewed.

Edwards' earliest discussion of "conscience" again confirms the novelty of his analysis in True Virtue. As in the early "Miscellanies," conscience is a kind of sense, a perception of real moral good; it is not defined as self-union or its opposite, self-contradiction. Conscience is not yet distinguished from men's natural sense of beauty. We also find that in his youth Edwards held a far more favorable view of natural inclination than in his final thoughts on the principles of natural morality. In his earliest treatment of the topic, the sense in which conscience consists is identical with our sense of beauty or excellency; and that natural sense may be of the highest excellency. In short, the young Edwards had not yet drawn a radical distinction between spiritual and moral taste, or between conscience's sense of primary and of secondary beauty. He seems to have been one of those optimistic modern writers— whether

-- 700 --

founding true moral good in affection (sense) or in reason— whom later he engaged in mortal combat.

"The Mind," no. [39]5 states, "Besides the two sorts of assent of the mind called will and judgment, there is a third, arising from a sense of the general beauty and harmony of things, which is conscience." Here, the mind's judging and willing is set over against the mind's sense of beauty; and the sense of beauty— conscience— against judging and willing. There is no division in the will, none in conscience; the division is between conscience and choice (or judging/willing). "There are some things which move a kind of horror in the mind which yet the mind wills and chooses; and some which are agreeable in this way to its make and constitution which it chooses not." This then would have been Edwards' explication of Romans 8:15, in contrast to Augustine's "divided will,"6 and to his own later disunited conscience.

While the assent of willing/judging and the assent of conscience may disagree, Edwards continues, the two "assents of will and conscience have indeed a common object, which is excellency." The two sorts of assents (or dissents) "differ" even when they concur. The object of conscience, if I read the number correctly, "is always general excellency, that is, harmony, taken in relation to the whole system of beings." The object of the mind's willing ("whether the excellency be more general or particular") is "that excellency which most strongly affects."7 Thus, the will always "is as is"8 the strongest affection to excellency, but this may be contrary to conscience's apprehension of the most general excellency. When conscience's sense or discernment of greater or most general excellency and the will's greater apprehension of excellency specifically are in conflict, the will prevails. Edwards' concluding formula for any concurrence of will and conscience is that "the degree wherein we are affected by any excellency is in proportion compounded of the extensiveness and the intensiveness of our [the mind's] view of that excellency."

Obviously, Edwards had yet to distinguish sharply between secondary beauty and primary "cordial" beauty. Conscience as a sense that

-- 701 --

apprehends excellency, or beauty, is not distinct from our sense for harmony among natural things. Nor certainly is it self-union (the first element in conscience in True Virtue). The second element— desert or justice— would, however, be a fitting extension of Edwards' early notion of conscience. But so also is the mind's relish for primary beauty or excellency in True Virtue. Finally, the notion of a "proportion compounded of the extensiveness and the intensiveness of our view of that excellency" anticipates the dissertation's definition of the heart's true benevolence to being as a proportion of the degree of that being and the degree of its benevolence, or of greatness with goodness.

The second analysis of conscience in "The Mind" is par. 14 of No. [45]. EXCELLENCE. This must be set in context with the entire number, which brings us already into the moral world of True Virtue. Consent and dissent, for example, are central concepts; spiritual beauty and external beauty are distinguished; the innertrinitarian beauty or consent or love is the paradigm; and love to God is defined as a consent of participation in divine excellence.

External excellency is the consent of bodies, but this is agreeable only to "perceiving being." The very word consent is borrowed from spiritual excellence, of which external beauty is but a shadow. "There is no other proper consent but that of minds, even of their will": when "of minds towards minds, it is love, and when of minds towards things it is choice." As external excellence must needs be agreeable to perceiving being, so "mind's love to mind must needs be lovely to beholding mind; and being's love to being in general must needs be agreeable to being that perceives it, because itself is a participation of being in general" (par. 1–2). Dissent from being, along some scale that includes bodies, must needs be "disagreeable" to perceiving being (which "only is properly being," par. 4). On this understanding of the self's disagreement with being this number's understanding of conscience is built, not on self's disagreement with self.

God's peculiar excellency is his "beauty within himself, consisting in being's consent with his own being, or love of himself in his own Holy Spirit; whereas the excellence of others is in loving others, in loving God, and in the communications of his Spirit" (par. 12). Then in the next paragraph, in order to remove any "aspect and cast of what we call self-love" from God's love, Edwards says that God's love includes "a love to everything, as they are communications of himself"; it is an "infinite general love, that which reaches all proportionately, with perfect purity and sweetness; yea, it includes the true love of all creatures."

-- 702 --

End of Creation will say the same of God's love to creatures. Then par. 13 (immediately preceding what is said about conscience) turns to the divine excellence from the creature's point of view: "and if we take notice when we are in the best frames, meditating on divine excellence, our idea of that tranquillity and peace which seems to be overspread and cast abroad upon the whole earth and universe naturally dissolves itself into the idea of a general love and delight, everywhere diffused."9

This fixes the context for the extraordinary competence ascribed by the young Edwards to natural conscience, for conscience is that "best frame" apprehending that idea of God's excellence. "Conscience is that sense [!] the mind has of this consent: which consists in the consent of perceiving being to such general consent, that is, of such perceiving beings as are capable of so general a perception as to have any notion of being in general, and the dissent of his mind to a dissent from being in general" (par. 14).1

This understanding of conscience enabled the young Edwards to give an account of disquiet of conscience and peace of conscience remarkably different from that of True Virtue. When a mind exerts "an act of dissent to being in general" it "has a sense that being in general dissents from it, which is most disagreeable to it." Disquiet of conscience is the mind's recoil within its sense that the excellency or beauty of "being in general" disagrees or is in conflict with it. Conscious of one's own "dissent from universal being, and of that being's dissent from him, wherever he is he sees what excites horror."2 By doing that which is

against his natural inclination[!] as a perceiving being he must necessarily cause uneasiness, inasmuch as natural inclination[!] is contradicted: and this is disquiet of conscience. But when there is no sense of any such dissent from being in general, there is no contradiction to the natural inclination of perceiving being. And when he reflects, he has a sense that being in general doth not dissent from him, and then there is peace of conscience.

Having a sense that "nature in general consents to him," he has "not only peace, but joy of mind, wherever he is."3

Such was the extraordinary understanding of natural conscience as a

-- 703 --

sense— one might say, a "moral sense"— which Edwards held in his mid-twenties, c. 1726, just before or soon after settlement in Northampton. "These things are obviously invigorated," Edwards concluded, "by the knowledge of God, and his constitution about us, and by the light of the Gospel."

Perhaps, however, there is in the paragraph on conscience in No. [45] a deeper contradiction than that between the mind's willing and its affective conscience of general excellence in No. [39]. There may be a contradiction of some sort in No. [45] between simultaneous consent and dissent. "If by any means," Edwards wrote, "a particular and restrained love overcomes this general consent, the foundation of that consent yet remaining in the nature exerts itself again, so that there is contradiction of one consent to another."4 The mind enamored of creaturely goods cannot "consent entirely" to them, because conscience of general excellency is founded in "the nature" of created minds, even as Augustine could not "will entirely" the love of learning, rhetoric, or his "ancient mistresses," because his soul was so framed that it was restless until it found rest in love to God. Augustine and the early Edwards held in common a doctrine of creation in which was founded a good willing unconquerable by any nilling, a consent in conscience never overwhelmed entirely by dissent from general excellence: man's plight was that he had no power of will to break out of this contradiction. In one way or another this remained Edwards' view; but theologically, redemption displaced creation in his understanding of a holy and whole will or virtuous love.5

The earliest reference to Francis Hutcheson that we have from Edwards' hand is item 4 on p. 8 of his "Catalogue of Books." Dates mentioned in that column suggest that the time of the entry was in the latter months of 1738 (the same year as the charity sermons). The notes on "The Mind" and the entries on conscience in the "Miscellanies" that have just been reviewed were all written, of course, before this first mention of Hutcheson. Moreover, the reference was not to Hutcheson

-- 704 --

directly. It reads: "An Inquiry into the Original of our Ideas of Beauty that seems a very ingenious thing by the specimens of this book in Chambers under the word Common Places where tis mentioned as a Book in Octavo. & the 199 page is Refer'd to also under the word Evil."

There were earlier references to Shaftesbury. The earliest is item 37 on the first page of the "Catalogue." The entry was made in the early summer of 1723. It simply lists Shaftesbury's Characteristics. Another reference is the fifteenth "direction" to himself— his "resolutions" as a young author— inside the coverleaf of "Natural Philosophy." This reads: "Often times it suits the subject and reasoning best to explain by way of objection and answer, after the manner of dialogue, like the Earl of Shaftesbury." This was probably written in the early months of 1726, during the second year of Edwards' Yale tutorship by Schafer's reckoning; or in that year, but soon after the end of the tutorship, according to Anderson.6 So there were references to Shaftesbury roughly from the same general period in which the young Edwards penned the various senses of conscience we have reviewed.

Proving that Edwards' had one of Hutcheson's works in hand takes us up to 1750–51. "Catalogue," p. 22, item 3, reads: "Hutchesons Essay on the Passions cited in his Enquiry into the original of our ideas of Beauty & virtue." Schafer connects the immediately preceding items with a sermon dated November 1750, and so reckons that Edwards had access to Hutcheson's Enquiry at least by the end of 1750 or early in 1751, shortly before he composed the "Controversies"— draft. That mere notation of Hutcheson's books, however, cannot be proof of significant influence upon Edwards' penultimate draft of the second dissertation. The "Catalogue of Books" is notoriously "soft" evidence.

The references from p. 22, item 6, to p. 33, item 1, of the "Catalogue" are passages taken from Turnbull. Among these, two refer to Hutcheson and show that Edwards' access to the works he names was through Turnbull. Page 32, item 7, reads: "Hutcheson on the Nature & Conduct of the Passions often cited with great Approbation by Turnbull"; and item 8, "Hutchesons Illustrations of Moral sense cited & commended by Turnbull."

Miscell. nos. 1209–11 consist of quotations from Turnbull. From the fact that an earlier No. 1200 quotes from the Monthly Review for August 1752, Schafer concludes that Edwards' harvests of book titles from Turnbull, in both the "Catalogue" and the "Miscellanies," were reaped in the latter months of 1752.

-- 705 --

The foregoing confirms— only confirms— what we know already concerning Edwards' reading and active use of Turnbull in the actual preparation of what I have called his "three" or "four" dissertations, written in the years before Original Sin was split off and sent to the press and Two Dissertations was put together.7 What we know of Edwards' early reading until True Virtue was substantially drafted is also entirely consistent with the weight of evidence from late entries in the "Miscellanies": When Edwards began to glean passages directly from Hutcheson, his mind was on an altogether different topic.

Fiering is only the most recent example of interpreters who tempt readers to believe that Francis Hutcheson was, if not the sole figure, the giant among the hosts against whom JE went out to do battle on the nature of true virtue. It is one contention of this book that Edwards's study of eighteenth-century moral thought affected him profoundly and presented him with a major intellectual problem, which his dissertation on the nature of true virtue, written quite late in his life, was an attempt to resolve. The new gospel of benevolence and optimism preached by Shaftesbury and Hutcheson was not simply old-fashioned Christian moralism or a doctrine of salvation by works against which Edwards could hurl the sola fide and his conviction that true virtue is a matter of inner disposition; this new gospel was itself based on a theory of the affections and of human nature that directly rivaled some of the basic tenets of Christian anthropology. [p. 8]
There were more than sentimental versions of that rivalry, more new gospels than the Scottish school.
Again Fiering asserts, "Edwards's problem was to demonstrate, in opposition to these sentimentalists, that natural morality is necessarily always a function of intellect alone (with the exceptions of self-love, which is immoral judged by the highest standards, and certain ‘instincts,’ which Edwards considered to be non-moral)" (p. 66. Ed. italics). Careful readers of True Virtue will recognize more things wrong with that statement than the lack of evidence in support of the words I italicize. Or again: "How far Hutcheson's ethical theory was deficient on its own grounds Edwards tried to show in the second part of The Nature of True Virtue" (p. 337; the first part of it is not really "commensurable with secular ethics"). In support of the proposition that JE "took issue directly [Ed. italics] with Hutcheson's vacillation toward the doctrine that God is the proper end of any true system of ethics," Fiering cites (p. 339) the if-true-virtue-consists-partly-in-a-respect-to-God-then-doubtless-it-consists-chiefly-in-it passage, which JE correctly stated was "an inconsistence in some writers on morality," not one only or mainly (p. 552 above. Ed. italics). Also, Fiering arbitrarily makes Hutcheson the target of an argument which, it is well known, Edwards used over and over again: "In Hutcheson's theory, Edwards said, ‘we never come to any beginning or foundation; it is without beginning, and hangs on nothing’" (p. 341. Ed. italics). That was JE's standard formal argument, used by him in a number of contexts. His preceding words are: "If virtue consists primarily in love to virtue, then virtue, the thing loved, is the love of virtue: so that virtue must consist in the love of the love of virtue... and so on in infinitum. For there is no end of going back in a circle" (See p. 544 above. Here according to Fiering, it seems that the first part of True Virtue also "took issue directly" with Hutcheson!). Nor do I find (in Fiering's n. 8, p. 109) any proof offered for the statement, concerning possible influence of Shaftesbury, that "there is reason to suspect that Edwards's first editors suppressed whatever evidence of this connection may have existed, after Shaftesbury's reputation in later years made him anathema to American clerics" (pp. 108–09). The early and abiding influence of Shaftesbury is plausible enough, without stressing "moral sense." (For JE's early references and reading of Shaftesbury, see below.) Students of JE can profitably ponder the systematic proposition that Edwards was "much more a Shaftesburian in philosophy than a Hutchesonian." Still, in general Fiering needed more Humian skepticism that mere consistency doesn't prove a thing. Too often Fiering's enormously learned book manifests his avowed principles of interpretation not anchored by historical proof, viz., "the absence of evidence in Edwards's manuscripts cannot prove that he had not read a particular work" (p. 45); "Yet we must insist here that even though Edwards probably never read a single word of Leibniz and had probably not read much about him, a knowledge of Leibniz can be more helpful in understanding Edwards than hours devoted to reading Locke's Essay, which Edwards had definitely closely studied... Finding the right context for a thinker, placing him in a school or a tradition or a circle, even when one cannot demonstrate definitively the exact influences, is extremely important for getting the right ‘feel’ of a man" (p. 47). But observing and respecting a distinction between arguments for intellectual affinity and those for literary dependence is central to responsible historical criticism. ↩
