“PAPER ON JUSTIFICATION”

[To] 6 show, first, that it doth [derog]ate from the freedom [of gr]ace, and to show plainly how more grace appears in the other.

[Seco]ndly, 7 to show that it derogates [from] the honor of the Redeemer [and] gives that honor to man that [belon]gs to the Redeemer, contrary to the design of the gospel, which is to abase man and to ascribe all glory to Christ.

To show that we are justified by the imputation of Christ’s righteousness. To show what I mean by imputation. “By the obedience of one shall many be made righteous” [Rom. 5:19]. Whether it be active or passive, yet still it is under the notion of obedience. ‘Tis as his obedience that it makes us righteous; and if so, ‘tis under the notion of a righteousness.

The 8 distinction of active and passive obedience in this case is utterly improper, for all obedience is active. All obedience, considered under the notion of obedience, is active; and considered under the notion of obedience, ‘tis righteousness. ‘Tis contrary the obedience there [is] in suffering. So far as ‘tis in obedience, ‘tis active; if considered as obedience, ‘tis considered [as] 9 act. Indeed, the sufferings of Christ may be considered under a twofold consideration: either as a part of his obedience [or] a part of his righteousness, [and] so it is meritorious; or as properly his suffering, and so it is a satisfaction or propitiation for sin.

Show how is it possible that by the righteousness of one, another should be made righteous, without imputation. One can’t be made righteous by the righteousness of another, unless [th]e righteousness be made his or be looked upon as his. If a man is accepted as righteous, that righteousness on

-- --

the account [of] which he is accepted, so becomes [hi]s righteousness by that acceptance; or, which is the same thing, is accepted as his.

Their scheme is self-contradictory, for it makes a man justified where he is: for it supposes a man [to] have a right to salvation before he is justified. For, first, they suppose that a man is not justified till he is become godly, or has special and saving grace; but, secondly, they hold that he has a right to salvation before he has special grace, viz. by a good improvement of common grace. For they say he has a right to special grace, which implies salvation; but he that has a right to that which imples a right to salvation, has already a right to salvation, and can challenge it. He can challenge it already; as, for instance, he that has a right to be looked upon as the king’s eldest son, he therein has a right to the crown because, being the king’s eldest son implies the other.

So that, according to this scheme, a man is justified not by evangelical obedience, but by the good improvement of common grace. The absurdities, viz.: first, that he is justified before he is; second, justified by that which is no godliness, which in itself is an absurdity; and thirdly, that yet he is justified only by his godliness.

They say that God has promised special grace to a good improvement of common grace, and salvation to special grace. Therefore, I say, that he has in effect promised salvation to only a good improvement of common grace, so that he can then immediately challenge it without further delay. Thus, for instance, if one man promises another that if he will do such a thing, and at the same promises, that if he has the possession of another thing, and that if he has that other thing, he will give him another, and when he has that, another: I say, that in such a case, as soon as ever he has performed the first condition, he has a right to all the other things that follow one

-- --

another, and can challenge ‘em then without further delay; because as soon as that first is performed, that other stands bound to bestow all the rest. So that the proper condition of salvation is the good improvement of common grace, which they themselves allow is no godliness.

But they will say he is not justified before the end of his life.

But they hold that sins are pardoned before the end of life. Vid. Paper on Free Will, no. 8. 10

If 11 eternal life be given for the sake of what we have done, or from respect to our excellency, then ‘tis not given for the sake of Christ. (Here to show the inconsistency of these two). And if it ben’t given for the sake of Christ, then Christ is but a partial Redeemer or Savior; he is but half a Savior. He don’t redeem us unto God, for he don’t purchase eternal life for us.

Here it may be said that [Christ] purchases heaven for us, in that he purchases that our sincere obedience may be accepted as the condition of salvation, and so purchases an opportunity for us. 12

But is that properly purchasing heaven for us, to purchase an opportunity for us to purchase it? But if it be, if Christ’s righteousness or merits ben’t imputed to us, all that he does is to satisfy for our sins and pay our debt for us; there is no positive purchase of any good. His purchasing that our imperfect obedience should be accepted, is only his satisfying for the imperfections of our obedience; or, which is the same thing, satisfying for the sins mixt with our obedience. He only sets us free from hell, and leaves us to purchase heaven for ourselves. So that he don’t properly purchase heaven for us in any respect, as he that only pays a price to deliver another from slavery can’t be said to purchase an estate for him too, merely because thenceforward he has opportunity to work for himself, and

-- --

so by the price of his own hard labor to purchase an estate for himself.

So that according to this scheme, the possession and inheritance of the saints has not been ourchased by Christ, contrary to Scripture.

According to their scheme, our own obedience is properly the price of heaven: for ‘tis that upon the account of our own goodness, or for the sake of it, that heaven is bestowed. Now that which is rendered to God, upon the account of which he gives the possession, is certainly the price of that possession.

So that the saints in heaven have no reason to thank Christ for purchasing heaven for ‘em, or redeeming them to God by his obedience, and purchasing for them to be kings and priests; and therefore we find ‘em in the Revelation praising Christ without a cause. So that all that Christ does for us, is only to deliver us from us; indeed, that is an occasion of our having opportunity to do something for ourselves. But he does no more for us by his redemption.

Their notion of a new law is absurd. They say that Christ died to satisfy the law for us. I ask, what law? If they say, the old, I answer, how then is free grace manifested in accepting our imperfect obedience, if God can’t justly not accept it? He could not die to satisfy that for us, for that we never were under. (Vid. Reflections Concerning Free Will, [no. 6].) 13

“On Free Will”: “I answer: how could Christ die to satisfy the first law for us, when, according to their scheme, we never broke that law? For according to them, we never broke that law in any sense. We never broke that law under Adam, for they say Adam was not our representative, and the guilt of his breaking of the law don’t belong to us. And we never broke it in our persons, for according to their scheme we never were under it: and how can we break a law that we were never under? And therefore, how could Christ die to satisfy the law for us, who never broke it, nor even were under it?

“It may be they will answer, Christ died to satisfy the law for us that we might not be under the law, and that we might be delivered from it. But what need of Christ’s dying that we might not be under the law, when, according to their scheme, ‘twould in itself be unjust that we should be under that law, because in our present state we are not able to keep it? What need therefore of Christ’s dying, that we might not be under a law that it would be unjust that we should be under, whether Christ died or no?”

If they say that he died to satisfy the new law, I answer, that according to their scheme [it] is to be answered by us in our sincere obedience. Christ abolished the old law and procured a new constitution, which man is required to fulfill himself. He is to stand for himself, and to fulfill that new law himself by sincere obedience, in the same manner that Adam was to have fulfilled the old law by perfect obedience.

If Christ satisfies the new for us, as well as the old, then I would

-- --

inquire why he don’t deliver us from the new law too by satisfying for it, as well as his satisfying the old. If we have broken the new law too, as well as the old, so that Christ must satisfy it for us, why don’t his satisfaction deliver us from that as well as the other, and so make way for a third law? They say Christ’s satisfying the old law abolishes it; and if so, why don’t his satisfying the new law abolish that too?

They say that Christ came into the world to die, [to] make way that our imperfect obedience might be accepted. But what need of that, when it would be unjust in itself that any other obedience than impartial obedience should be accepted? What need of Christ’s dying to make way for God’s accepting such an obedience? As it would be in itself unjust in him not to accept, is there any need of Christ’s dying to persuade God not to do unjustly?

According to their scheme, God would do unjustly not to accept of our imperfect obedience; and that because, say they, ‘tis the best obedience that we have power to perform, and because it was none of our fault that we have not power to perform better, because Adam’s sin is not imputed to us. Vid. Reflections on Free Will, [nos. 5-7]. 14

IMPUTATION of Christ’s righteousness. They perhaps may answer to my argument for the imputation of Christ’s righteousness, the need of fulfilling the law, that Christ by his obedience fulfilled the law for us, that the law, being fulfilled, might be abolished.

Answer: this don’t alter the case whether the law be abolished or not, yet if that obedience was performed in our stead--which is the thing I have been arguing for--then it is accepted in our stead, or, which is the same thing, accepted as if we had performed it; which is what I mean by imputation.

If it was performed in 15 our stead, then 16 Christ performed it as our

-- --

representative, and if he had not performed it, we should have been obliged to perform it ourselves: for there was no need of Christ’s performing in our stead that that did not belong to us; 17 but now, because he has performed it in our stead, are not obliged, then ‘tis accepted for us, or accepted as if we had performed it.

In 18 the beginning of my argument about imputation, to show what I mean by imputation of Christ’s righteousness, and to add that I don’t insist on the word “imputation” if the thing be allowed agreeable to this interpretation.

FAITH NOT GOSPEL OBEDIENCE, as is evident by the 4th [chapter] of Romans: for there in that chapter, speaking of Abraham’s being justified by faith, he explains it to be a dependence on the sufficiency and faithfullness of God with reference to the promised seed. What can be plainer? ‘Tis exceeding evident that the Apostle is speaking of the same faith that Abraham before was spoken of as justified for: for there at the end of that explanation, he adds, vv. 22-24, “And therefore it was imputed to him for righteousness.” God in that promised seed had respect chiefly to Christ, by this very Apostle’s own testimony. And we have no reason to doubt but that Abraham knew and understood that Christ was respected in it, saw Christ’s day held forth in that promise, and rejoiced in a dependence on the fulfillment of the promise as it respected Christ, from what Christ says: “Abraham saw my day: and he saw it, and was glad” [John 8:56]. Abraham by that very act of faith received Christ, by the Apostle’s own testimony, Heb. 11:19--speaking there of Abraham’s faith in the very same promise with respect to his seed as is here spoken of, adds, “from whence also he received him in a figure.”

And it need not seem strange, for by Christ’s own testimony Abraham saw his day and rejoiced in the sight of it [John 8:56].

IMPUTATION. 19 We are expressly told that we are justified by the obedience of Christ as a righteousness, in the 5th [chapter] of Romans. Compare 18th and 19th verses.

If Christ has suffered the penalty of the law for us or in our stead, then it will follow that his suffering that penalty is imputed to us; i.e. that his suffering the penalty for us is accepted for us and in our stead, so that we have the benefit of it.

And so, if Christ fulfilled the law by obeying of it for us and in our stead, it will in like manner necessarily follow that his obedience is imputed or accepted for us and in our stead, as though we had performed it. [...] 20

IMPUTATION. 21 Another argument is that justification is by the righteousness or obedience of Christ. Our being received into God’s favor and to a right to eternal life, if it be in reward for the obedience of Christ, then ‘tis not the reward of our obedience. If our own obedience be the thing on the account of which we are acepted, then we are not acepted on the account of the obedience of another.

In what respect soever Christ is our Savior, that doubtless excludes our being our own saviors in the same sense. And therefore, [if] he be our Savior in that sense that he works out a righteousness on the account of which we are accepted and justified, then ‘tis not we ourselves that work out a righteousness on the account of which we are accepted and justified; because if we can be our own saviors in the same sense that Christ is, then Christ’s salvation is in vain, according to the Apostle’s own way of arguing: “If righteousness come by the law, then Christ is dead in vain” [Gal. 2:21]. Christ is rendered of no effect to you. Doubtless it is Christ's prerogative to be our Savior, in that sense wherein he is our Savior.

-- --

It will follow that Christ’s righteousness is imputed to us, because Christ is our second Adam; and as Adam’s sin is imputed to us, so is Christ’s righteousness imputed to us (I Cor. 15:22). And the Apostle himself testifies that we are justified in Christ as we are condemned in Adam, and by the obedience of one as we are by the disobedience of the other; but we are condemned by the disobedience of Adam as that is imputed to us (Rom. 5:12). 22

FREE GRACE. 23 It certainly shows a great goodness and grace, and that by which the creature is more obliged, for God to show mercy to it and to give it a right to eternal blessings, upon an odious, sinful, unworthy creature, without being drawn or influenced by any loveliness or excellency in the creature, than if it were drawn or influenced.

For an explanation of the term IMPUTATION, see Mastricht’s de Justificatione , §4. 24

The word IMPUTE is used in Scripture for putting upon one’s account. Philem. 1:18, “Put that upon my account,” the same word as in Rom. 4. ‘Tis in the original, “impute.” So Rom. 4:8, sin is not imputed, that is, is not reckoned to our account; and Rom. 4:6, the word must be used there in the same sense: righteousness is imputed without works, is the same as reckoning righteousness to a man’s account that has none of his own; for to be without works and without righteousness are the same things. But if he has no righteousness of his own, then imputing righteousness to him must be reckoning some other righteousness to his account that is not his own: for if righteousness is imputed to him, then there is a righteousness that is his. But if it ben’t of his own working, it must be some other.

The word in the original, as a certain excellent divine observes, is taken from the custom of merchants, whereby anything in their account, either debt or credit, is transferred from one man’s account to another. Vid.

Mastricht, de Justificatione , §12. 25 [...] 26

[ Obj. 27 1. If Christ has the] reward of his own obedience, how came we to have the reward of it?

Ans. Christ wrought out his obedience as a public person. He died for Christ’s mystical, etc. Vid. “Miscellanies.” 28

Obj. 2. If the saints have the happiness of heaven as the reward of Christ’s righteousness, then it will follow that they all have an equal degree of happiness in heaven.

Ans. Vid. “Miscellanies.”

It will be said perhaps that the salvation ben’t given directly as the REWARD of our obedience, but ‘tis our interest in Christ’s satisfaction and righteousness is given as the reward of our obedience. But how absurd is that! This is to ascribe as much to our obedience, as if we ascribed salvation to our obedience immediately without any righteousness of Christ: for ‘tis as great a thing, ‘tis as great a reward, to have Christ given us, and his righteousness as a reward, as to have salvation immediately given as a reward. ‘Tis evident, because it implies it. If this ben’t too great a reward for our obedience, then salvation is not; and so there would be no need of Christ’s righteousness. That which is given as a reward for something, is evermore given out of respect to the excellency of that thing: for that is the very notion of a reward.

REWARD. The thing that the Scripture guards against is an imagining that ‘tis our own goodness that recommends us or entitles us to the favor of God. But to suppose that God gives us an interest in Christ as a reward of any of our goodness, such a reward of our goodness is as great an argument that ‘tis our goodness that recommends us to the favor of God, as if God immediately bestowed salvation as the reward of our own goodness. And indeed,

-- --

it implies it: for if God gives us an interest in Christ and his benefits as a reward of our obedience, then he gives salvation as the reward of our obedience, for the former implies the latter; and indeed, it implies it, as the greater does the less. So that indeed it exalts our obedience more to suppose that God would give us Christ and his benefits, than if he should give us salvation immediately as the reward of our obedience: for to have Christ’s salvation in him is a greater benefit than to have salvation alone without him.

If God gives men an interest in Christ as a reward of their obedience, then it follows that God don’t accept of persons into his favor for the sake of Christ’s righteousness: for this argues that God has accepted into his favor, already antecedent to their being in Christ. For according to this scheme, they have an interest in Christ’s righteousness, given as a fruit of God’s favor; and therefore, God’s favor is not the fruit of that. Christ’s righteousness is given as a fruit of God’s favor, because ‘tis given as a reward for what is in them. If God bestows an infinite blessing on a person, surely ‘tis an evidence that he is already in his favor and is already accepted.

’Tis a contradiction to suppose that anything should be rewarded, and not out of respect to the excellency of it. To suppose justification given as a reward of anything in us, is the very thing that the Apostle opposes. To give justification for anything in us as a work, can’t be better explained than to give justification in reward of what we do.

IMPUTATION. 29 It was necessary that his obeying the law should be accepted for us, instead of our obeying, for the same reason as that his suffering the penalty of the law should be accepted as though we had suffered the penalty, viz. to answer the law. One is as much required in order to

-- --

answer the law as the other. This is given as the reason why Christ “was made a curse for us”: because the law threatened a curse to us. Gal. 3: 10-13, “For it is written, Cursed is every one that hangeth on a tree.” But Christ will deliver us “from the curse of the law, being made a curse for us.” But the same law that says, “Cursed is every one that continueth not in all things which are written in the book of the law to do them,” hath said, he that doth these things “shall live in them” The law does as much fix obedience as the condition of eternal life, as it does disobedience as the condition of eternal death; one is as much established as the other. Therefore, there is exactly the same need from the law of obedience’s being fulfilled in order to the obtaining the reward, as there is of death’s being borne in order to escape a punishment. There is just the same necessity by the law of perfect obedience preceding life, as there is of death’s following disobedence. The law is without doubt as much an established rule in one case as ‘tis in the other: 30 for surely, if God bestows an infinite reward on a person, he bestows it as a testimony of his acceptance; so that this supposition of God’s giving persons an interest in Christ as a reward of their obedience, if it be scanned, destroys itself: for it supposes that men’s own obedience is sufficient so far to recommend ‘em to God’s favor and acceptance, that God bestows an infinite blessing upon them in reward for it, as great a blessing as salvation itself, yea, a blessing that includes it. So that there can be no need of Christ’s righteousness to recommend a man to God’s favor to that degree as to have an infinite reward; for he has that by his own obedience, antecedent to his interest in Christ’s righteousness. If our own righteousness be sufficient to entitle us to a reward as great as salvation itself, then there is no need of Christ’s righteousness: for we don’t need any thing to entitle us to anything greater than salvation.

-- --

IMPUTATION. 31 And again, the word is not only used in this sense, but ‘tis used in this sense with respect to the righteousness of Christ. There is no more needful in order to prove this, but these two things, viz.: first, that the Apostle says that righteousness is imputed; and second, to prove that when he says righteousness is imputed [Rom. 4:22], he means the righteousness of Christ. This I prove two ways: [first,] it can’t be our own, because saved without works; second, ‘tis certainly the righteousness [of Christ], for ‘tis doubtless that righteousness by which we are made righteous. But the same Apostle tells us that is the righteousness of Christ.

Whether it derogates from grace, [“Miscellanies”] no. 191. 32

’Tis not to put upon a new trial in the same manner as old, but Christ stands instead of the tree of life, [“Miscellanies” no.] 498. 33

The 34 cause of the futurition not the cause of the thing.

Christ’s obedience meritorious, for he is not obliged.

To show how a man is altogether hateful. Notwithstanding that faith, which is an holy excellent thing, is antecedent to justification, he is still looked upon as altogether hateful, because nothing is acceptable till he is in Christ and is looked upon [as] righteous in him. Even truly good acts are not acceptable, or, which is the same thing, is not looked upon as good; the good is hid from God. ‘Tis reputed as no goodness.

Whether they hold any proper merit or no, yet that won’t help out the cause as they long as they maintain that ‘tis our own moral excellency that is what we are justified by. 35

Indeed, ‘tis out of respect to a fitness that by faith a man has to such a state. But that don’t agree that ‘tis from any respect to faith as a

-- --

good or excellent qualification, or from any respect to the excellency there is in faith. There is a twofold fitness to a state.

Obj. When it is said, “For I was an hungred, and ye gave me meat” [Matt. 25:35], to mention in answer those places, “Her sins, which are many, are forgiven; for she loved much” [Luke 7:47].
