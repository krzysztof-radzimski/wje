"NOTES ON CONVERSION FROM VARIOUS AUTHORS"

Edwards evidently began this folio notebook (BRBL, Box 21, f. 1262) shortly--very shortly, to judge by watermark, ink, and handwriting--after the death of his grandfather in February 1729, since Stoddard is referred to in the past tense several times. The notebook seems to have been conceived as a primary source book to contain quotes from reputable authors on the subject of conversion, perhaps for use in an intended essay on the subject. A close examination of the themes reveals that the notebook is related to Edwards' ongoing scrutiny of his grandfather's views of conversion, as seen especially in "Miscellanies" entries on humiliation, conversion, and related topics dating from 1729 and 1730. Here, for instance, Edwards collects quotes that militate against Stoddard's idea that one should be able to locate the time of one's conversion and that humiliation is not gracious.

The works and authors referred to in this document are all standards of the Reformed and Puritan traditions. Two works quoted here are mentioned early in Edwards' "Catalogue," namely, Dutch Calvinist Peter van Mastrict's Theoretico-Practica Theologia (1699), which Edwards came to regard as one of his favorites, and English Dissenter Joseph Alleine's Alarm to Unconverted Sinners (1672). William Ames' famous Medulla Theologica was a text that Edwards studied in college. Edwards also quotes from Giles Firmin's Real Christian (1670), an important statement on conversion. A more obscure work that passed under several titles was The Life of Mr. Henry Gearing, or, The Character and Trial of a Real Christian, published by John Shower. Edwards probably obtained this book from his father, Timothy, since it is listed in his library inventory.1 The names of Puritan worthies such as John Preston, Thomas Shepard, and John Nalton are cited, but the references to at least the latter two of these authors were attained secondhand through Gearing's Life.

*****

Edwards quickly abandoned this notebook and did not make subsequent entries. It is composed of a folio single-folded signature, which has a London Arms/Crowned GR(L) watermark. This watermark first appears in a sermon on Deuteronomy 7:10, dating from April or May 1729, and then in a batch of sermons from July to December, a fact which supports a starting point for this notebook very soon after Stoddard's death. Edwards divided each page down the middle by a vertical line. He numbered only columns 1-6 on pp. 1-3, with column 7 on the left-hand side of p. 4 being a continuation of column 2 on p. 1. Some of the columns have several entries, while others have only a title. The right-hand column on p. 4 is completely empty.

"NOTES ON CONVERSION FROM VARIOUS AUTHORS" (edited)

1. CONCERNING KNOWING THE TIME OF CONVERSION.

Dr. Preston, an author that Mr. Stoddard greatly admired14 for his skill in matters of experience, advises that this be no stick with persons about their conversion, that they don't know the time.15

Mr. Firmin speaks of it as a strange doctrine, that men must know the time, and says to this purpose: Did ever any divine, worthy of the name of a divine, teach such doctrine?16 Mr. Williams of Hatfield17 says that Mr. Stoddard liked this author.

Mr. Alleine, in his Alarm to the Unconverted, p. 84, seems to suppose that a man may be converted and not know the time. His words are these: "When was the time, where was the place, or what was the means, by which this thorough change of the new birth was wrought in his soul? Speak, Conscience. Or if thou canst not tell time and place, canst thou show Scripture evidence, that the work is done?"18

2. WHETHER HUMILIATION BE NOT GRACE AND BEN'T ANTECEDENT TO CONVERSION, AND OPINIONS ABOUT THE NATURE OF HUMILIATION.

Dr. Ames accounts, 'tis repentance that usually goes before a sensible act of faith (Medulla, ch. 26, §35).19

Mr. Nalton, a famous London minister that flourished about the middle of the last century, speaks of humiliation as sorrow for sin. His words are: "After convincing, the Spirit of God humbles the sinner; he is humbled to the dust. This sorrow and humiliation is in some more, some less." See in Mr. Gearing, his Trial of himself, p. 45.20

Van Mastrict supposes there to be three things preparatory to actual faith and repentance, viz. 1. Contrition, which the Apostle calls "the spirit of bondage to fear" marginal, which he supposes is meant when the Psalmist speaks of a contrite heart, and Christ, when he speaks of those that labor and are heavy laden; which consists in the sinner's sense of the filthiness of his sins, and the anger of God and fear of punishment, and sense of his own folly and madness in sinning as he has done, and great grief and sorrow accompanying of it, and confession of sins before God and man and indigation against himself. 2. Humiliation, by which the contrite person, in the thoughts of his sins and misery that he is in danger of, is vile and abject in his own eyes, and condemns himself and acknowledges he deserves eternal damnation, is filled with shame and acknowledges himself to be wholly unworthy of any mercy. 3. Saving desperation, whereby a person is sensible that he has nothing to pay for his own deliverance, becomes poor in spirit, and that we can do nothing for our own deliverance.

And he supposes in those that are to be converted, those preparatory exercises flow from a principle of grace already infused in regeneration. Though there may be after a sort materially the same things in reprobates, and that there are the very same things after actual faith and repentance, and to a much greater degree.21

Mr. Alleine of Taunton, in his Alarm to the Unconverted, speaks of a man's being brought off from his own righteousness, as what is wrought in conversion and by converting grace. Speaking of the terms from which we are turned in conversion, he enumerates them thus, p. 32: "The terms from which we turn in this motion of conversion, are sin, Satan, and the world, and our own righteousness." Which fourth he speaks to particularly, in p. 38 of the last, in these words:

Fourthly, your own righteousness. Before conversion, man seeks to cover himself with his own fig leaves (Philippians 3:6-7), and to lick himself whole with his own duties (Micah 6:6-7). He is apt to trust in himself (Luke 16:15 and Luke 18:9), and set up his own righteousness, and reckon his counters for gold, and not submit to the righteousness of God (Romans 10:3). But conversion changes his mind; now he casts away his filthy rags, and counts his own righteousness, but a menstruous cloth: he casts it off, as a man would the verminous tatters of a nasty beggar (Isaiah 64:7). Now he is brought to poverty of spirit (Matthew 5:3), complains of and condemns himself (Romans 7), and all his inventory is poor, and miserable, and wretched, and blind, and naked (Revelation 3:17). He sees a world of iniquity in his holy things, and calls his once-idolized righteousness, but flesh, and loss, and dog's meat, and would not for a thousand worlds be found in himself.22

3. WHETHER A DISTINCT ACT OF FAITH IN CHRIST BE ALWAYS THE FIRST SENSIBLE AND REMARKABLE ACT OF GRACE.

Dr. Ames says that "repentance is wont to be perceived first before faith," in his Medulla, ch. 26, §35.23

4. WHETHER THERE BE NO DIFFERENCE BETWEEN THE GODLY AND SOME OTHER MEN WITH RESPECT TO A CONSCIENTIOUS BEHAVIOR.

Mr. Alleine, who was accounted eminent for his skill in the nature of conversion, gives this as a sign of true grace: he says,

Where there is true grace, there is an hearty willingness to part with every sin. Wherever there is this breach made between sin and the soul, it is grace that hath made it. marginal Reason saith, "I ought to turn"; Conscience saith, "I must turn," and yet nothing [may] follow: but when the heart saith, "I will turn to God," then the work is done. Reason saith, "These idols ought not to stand"; Conscience saith, "These lusts must be subdued": but when the will saith to them, "Get you hence," there is a work of grace begun. This willingness discovers itself to be prevailing---1. When a man is truly willing to part with sin, there will be resolutions against it; he takes part with God against sin, and uses all means for the conquering of it. 2. This resolution will bring forth resistance.

See in the Life of Mr. Gearing, in the Character and Trial of a Real Christian, pp. 18-19.24

Mr. Shepard himself mentions it as one of the three things wherein a child of God goes beyond an hypocrite, viz. "no hypocrite, or unregenerate man, though he goes never so far, let him do never so much, but he lives in one sin or other, secret or open, little or great." As Mr. Gearing gives an account, in his Trial of himself, p. 22.25

5. WHETHER STRONG TERRORS ARE NECESSARY TO GO BEFORE CONVERSION.

6. OF THE MANNER OF CONVERSION, AND CIRCUMSTANCES OF THE CONVERTED.

In the account that is given of the conversion of Mr. Gearing's niece in Mr. Gearing's Life, under the title of The Character and Trial of a Real Christian, she gives an account that, under hearing a sermon upon that text, Matthew 11:28, "Come unto me," etc. she was much affected in the hearing of it. She felt sin to be a burden to her conscience, and would fain have rest to her soul, was sensible of her want of Jesus Christ, of her absolute necessity to be interested in him. She began to see his worth and excellency, as the chief of ten thousands, altogether lovely; and then had hungrings and thirstings after him, so that she could say, "None but Christ, give me Christ, or else I die." And afterward, in hearing a sermon upon that text, Matthew 13:45, "The kingdom of heaven is like a merchant man seeking goodly pearls," etc. she received much light and comfort, and saw more of the transcendent excellency of Jesus Christ, who was the pearl of great price, and thought she could most willingly part with all, even life itself, for him. And that after this she was tempted to question the truth of the holy Scriptures, and after that was assaulted with the most horrid atheistical and blasphemous temptations; and that she was under great terrors and apprehensions of the wrath of God and fears of hell. And after all this, first began the practice of the duty of secret prayer by the direction of one that she advised with. But after this, she had the most remarkable comforts, and the gracious and joyful influences of the Spirit which she experienced were very great and remarkable.26
