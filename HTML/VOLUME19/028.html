UNDESERVED MERCY

Were it not for Edwards' note above the citation and text on the first leaf of the manuscript in the same ink as the rest of it, we could easily read this sermon on the twentieth of Ezekiel simply as another example of how mercy seasons justice, personifications notwithstanding: "Justice said, 'Cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?' But Mercy stepped in and said, 'Let it alone this year also.'"1 Accordingly, Edwards explicates his text for just cause and free grace. God showered mercies upon the children of Israel, redeeming them out of Egypt and making them his "peculiar people," yet they rebelled against him, polluting his sabbaths; then, as God appeared "ready in a most awful manner to destroy 'em," his mercy saved them. But Edwards also explicates for tropes, recasting God and the "children" of his text, an "endearing appellation," into a stock, if rather intricate, figure of parental chastisement. "As a loving father is very loth to come to correct his child with severity, and so sometimes, when he is going about it, repents in the midst of it, his bowels yearn over his child. And though he lifted up his hand, as with an intention to strike a severe blow, yet his affections do as it were hold it back, or so abates the force of the blow, that the child is but lightly hurt." In the first of two preaching units, Edwards plumbs divine displeasure, especially at the point of sudden and terrible vengeance; in the last of six propositions, he defines the mercy that turns such justice aside, God's compassion for his covenant people and for his Son, his "name's sake" of the text. Still, the sermon speaks to more than mercy and metaphor.

Northampton obviously knew, as Edwards had noted, that it was met that Wednesday morning "for a day of prayer appointed on the occasion of the front gallery's falling, which fell on March 13,1737," three days earlier. It happened after the beginning of his sermon on Acts 13:41, "Behold, ye despisers, and wonder, and perish," the Word of God overcome with "dolorous shrieks and crying" and the thunder of timbers

-- 629 --

cracking.2 Somehow no one was seriously injured, a "remarkable and almost miraculous preservation," and now in the afternoon of thanks-giving Edwards uses his "very applicable" text to tally both the "unspeakable" mercies and "awful" rebukes visited upon its residents. A professing people, they were "eminently so," granted greater mercies than any "town now on earth"; partakers of spiritual mercies, they were heirs to temporal ones, spared the "sweeping sickness" of neighboring towns, whose mournful streets were emptied of children; kept from death, they were miraculously without serious injury, "not a bone broken that we know of."

Yet backsliding persisted among them, backbiting and strife, a "violent spirit after the world" and a "drowsy, sleepy spirit" at worship; for surprising conversions, "surprising providence[s]," especially on sabbathdays, the time of Joseph Hawley's suicide, Thomas Stebbins' attempt at it, and the falling gallery. All this grows bitter and uncommonly personal when Edwards considers the new meetinghouse under construction and the "backward spirit" of its antagonists. "Can any think it is pleasing to God to see a people that he has been so exceeding bountiful [to], when they are laying out to build an house for his worship, to do it with much fright at the charge and complaining; and not only so, but reflecting on one another for being the occasions of it?" Though he admits that many in town are "very much pinched," they distrust God their estates at their own peril, he warns them now, as he had a year ago; then they paid for it with a "backward" spring of floods and a fall of sere harvests, God's "wasting judgments" that were more costly probably than his new house. That Edwards is vulnerable to attack himself because he "bear[s] none of the charge" for construction provokes a spirited defense and a rueful admonition. "God is witness whether what has been said ben't warranted by his Word," and so he is sure to "confirm and justify it" in his providence, as he had in the past. "Let none imagine that I say these things in reproof of others," Edwards adds later, "because I would wipe myself clean." As God's messenger, he is called upon to "reprove and warn" his people and to humble himself before him and with them, that they may accept his reproof as God's.

-- 630 --

Aside from the numerous dashes of omission or repetition, the twenty-eight duodecimo pages of the manuscript betray little of the necessary haste of their composition. There are four deleted passages (LL. 12r., 18r., 20r., and 23r.) and one transposition (LL. 21r.–21v.) among the usual instances of struck and inserted lines; the last leaf is blank. The sober note that heads the manuscript may even account for that.

UNDESERVED MERCY
Notwithstanding the children rebelled against me: they walked not in my statutes, neither kept my judgments to do them, which if a man do, he shall even live in them; they polluted my sabbaths: then I said, I will pour out my fury upon them, to accomplish mine anger against them in the wilderness. Nevertheless I withdrew mine hand, and wrought for my name's sake, that it should not be polluted in the sight of the heathen, in whose sight I brought them forth. Ezekiel 20:21–22

In the beginning of the chapter, we have an account how the elders of Israel came to the prophet Ezekiel to inquire of the Lord, as though they had a desire to know what the will of God was. They set3 before the Prophet, as though they would hear the word of God from his mouth; but God said to 'em by the Prophet, "Are ye come to inquire of me? As I live, saith the Lord, I will not be inquired of by you." God was provoked by the hypocrisy of the pretense and show they made by coming and sitting before the Prophet, as if it were to hear the word of God, and had a mind to know his will. To see 'em sitting there with such a pretense, was abominable in God's eyes, because he knew they were hypocritical in it. Though they set before the Prophet, as if it was to hear God's word and to know his will, yet they did but dissemble in it; for they took no care to do what they did know, and what they had often heard already. And therefore God gave 'em an awful rebuke, as they set before Ezekiel. He told him he would not be inquired of by 'em, and bids the Prophet to judge them, and make them to know the abominations of them and their fathers, and put [them] in mind of their unworthiness to be allowed to come before God, to inquire of him or hear his word.

And [he] directs him particularly to put them in mind of the wonderful mercies God had bestowed upon them, and great things he had done

-- 632 --

for them; and how unworthily they had behaved themselves notwithstanding; and how he had rebuked them, and yet delivered them, and they still went on in their abominations; and particularly God's great work in redeeming them out of Egypt, and his giving them his statutes and judgments, and entering into covenant with them in the wilderness As Ezekiel 20:5–6, "In the day when I chose Israel, and lifted up mine hand unto the seed of the house of Jacob, and made myself known unto them in the land of Egypt, when I lifted up mine hand unto them, saying, I am the Lord your God; in the day that I lifted up mine hand unto them, to bring them forth of the land of Egypt into a land that I had espied for them, flowing with milk and honey, which is the glory of all lands";and Ezekiel 20:11–12, "And I gave them my statutes and showed them my judgments, which if a man do, he shall even live in them. Moreover also I gave them my sabbaths to be a sign between me and them, that they might know that I am the Lord that sanctify them." God declares to 'em how they notwithstanding, from time to time, grieved and provoked him by their evil ways. And in the words of the text may be particularly observed the following things.

1. The sinful and unworthy carriage of God's people Israel is declared, first, in general: they "rebelled against me: they walked not in my statutes, neither kept my judgments to do them, which if a man do, he shall even live in them"; second, a particular sin is mentioned, viz. that they polluted God's sabbaths. This in particular is much insisted in the chapter, Ezekiel 20:12, "Moreover also I gave them my sabbaths to be a sign between me and them that they might know that I am the Lord that sanctify them"; Ezekiel 20:13, "And my sabbaths they greatly polluted"; Ezekiel 20:16, "Because they despised my judgments, and walked not in my statutes, but polluted my sabbaths"; Ezekiel 20:24, "Because they had not executed my judgments, but had despised my statutes, and had polluted my sabbaths."

2. We may take notice of the aggravations of this their sinful carriage, which we may be led to observe from the word notwithstanding: "Notwithstanding the children rebelled against me," etc., referring to what had been spoken of before; viz. the great things God had done for them in redeeming them out of Egypt, and calling them to be a peculiar people to himself, and which is expressly mentioned in the last words of the text, "in whose sight I brought them forth."

3. We may observe how the displeasure of God was manifested towards them, viz. that God appeared as if he were about to destroy 'em. He appeared just ready to cut them [off]. This is expressed in two things, viz. that God said he would pour out his fury upon them, and also in that he

-- 633 --

had actually stretched forth his hand to do it; as is implied in that when it is signified that God spared 'em, and did not do as he was about to do, 'tis expressed that he withdrew his hand, which implies that his hand was stretched out, as one that was going to cut them off. God appeared as if he were about not only to destroy them, but destroy them in a very terrible and awful manner; as is implied in these expressions, "I said, I will pour out my fury upon them, and accomplish mine anger." The fury of God signifies a great and dreadful degree of wrath.

4. We may observe what wonderful mercy was mixed with, or rather took place of, this manifestation of God's displeasure. He withdrew his hand, and spared, and delivered 'em. He did not destroy them, as he appeared to be about to do, but wrought for them for their preservation and deliverance. while God appeared in awful displeasure, just ready in a most awful manner to destroy 'em, mercy stepped in. Mercy drew back that hand of judgment that God's anger had stretched out, and so spared his people. The same is expressed often in this chapter.

5. We may observe what was the ground of this wonderful exercise of sparing Mercy. God did not thus spare them for their worthiness, but to glorify his own free and sovereign grace. "I wrought for my name's sake, that it should not be polluted."

It could not be for anything else; for God had before declared how far they were from being worthy. So God was very particular in instructing his people in the wilderness, at the time wherein he wrought these great deliverances for them, that it was not for their righteousness. Deuteronomy 9:6–7, "Understand therefore that the Lord thy God giveth thee not this good land to possess it for thy righteousness, for thou art a stiffnecked people. Remember, and forget not, how thou provokedst the Lord thy God to wrath in the wilderness: from the day that thou didst depart out of the land of Egypt, until ye came unto this place, ye have been rebellious against the Lord."

6. We may observe the appellation that God calls 'em, viz. children: "Notwithstanding the children rebelled against me." An endearing appellation it is. It seems to be used in this place for two reasons; viz. first, the more to show the aggravatedness of their behaving themselves so ill: "the children have rebelled against me." It was much more aggravated than if slaves had rebelled; as Isaiah 1:2, "Hear, O heavens, and give ear, O earth: I have nourished and brought up children, and they have rebelled against me." And, secondly, because God, in these dealings with them here spoken of, dealt with 'em as children. He was very angry with them, and said he would accomplish his anger, and stretched out his hand. But

-- 634 --

then his pity and affection towards 'em (to speak of God after the manner of men), wrought the bowels of a father; and he drew back his hand again, and did not do as he seemed to be going to do: as a tender father, when about in anger to chastise a child, will in the midst of it sometimes feel the bowels of tenderness and pity working towards his child that may make him stay his hand.

DOCTRINE.

When God's professing people behave themselves unanswerably to great things that God has done for 'em, God sometimes appears ready in [an] awful manner to destroy them, and yet in undeserved and wonderful mercy withdraws his hand and spares them.

This point may be distinctly considered under the following propositions.

Prop. I. God sometimes does great things for a professing people. He sometimes does great things for them of a temporal nature, sometimes in preserving them in times of great public dangers, and distinguishing of 'em from others, by preserving them from great calamities that others suffer. So God distinguished the children of Israel in Egypt, which is one of these great things implied in what is referred to in the text. When it was a dismal time in Egypt, so that there was not an house among the Egyptians where there was not one dead, the children of Israel were safely preserved. God sometimes does great things for a professing people in delivering of them from great calamities. So was that deliverance of the children of Israel out of Egypt, which is the mercy spoken of in the text.

And God sometimes does great things of a spiritual nature for a professing people. He pours out {his Spirit upon them}. These are great things indeed: the greatest things that ever God does for any people, and the greatest things that can be done for a people; more than all temporal mercies, more than if he made the rocks to pour out to 'em rivers of oil, or showered down a plentiful shower of silver and gold and pearls upon them.

That deliverance that is granted in the carrying on of such a work from the bondage of sin, and Satan, and from eternal death, is more than all temporal deliverance. That deliverance of the children of Israel out of Egypt was a type of this.

Sometimes God distinguishes a visible people very much by such a

-- 635 --

mercy from most other people. Sometimes he distinguishes a land, and sometimes distinguishes a particular town, by the remarkable bestowment of such mercies. When he does so, he does a greater thing for 'em than if he took them and set them up above the greatest and most opulent cities in the world in outward prosperity.

[Prop.] II. Sometimes the behavior of a people so blessed, is in many respects very unanswerable to the great things God has done for 'em. Their love and obedience don't only fail of being in proportion to the greatness of the mercies they have received; for such mercies are so great that nothing in us can be proportionable to them; nor is this expected, at least not of fallen creatures. But what is found in them, in their carriage and behavior, is not answerable or suitable in any wise as to the quality of it, as well as not proportionable in degree. They don't behave themselves, as might justly be expected of God and man. There is not such a behavior as manifests a suitable sense of the great things God has done for 'em; but, on the contrary, a great insensibleness. There is a manifestation of much of an unthankful spirit: their carriage in many respects tends to the dishonor of God, that has done such great things for 'em, in the eye of other people that behold 'em, and in whose sight God has put honor upon them, by what he has done for 'em.

[Prop.] III. When it is thus, this is very displeasing to God. 'Tis very displeasing to him, because they are a professing people. They are visibly his children, but especially as he has laid 'em under such obligation, by the great things that he has done for 'em. The ill behavior of such a people is much more displeasing to God than the like behavior in another people that han't received such mercy. God is provoked by the sins of other people, but is much more so by theirs. God manifests himself in his word not only to be made angry, but as though his Spirit were grieved by the sins of such a people. Psalms 78:40, "How oft did they provoke him in the wilderness, and grieve him in the desert!" It follows in the Psalms 78:42–43, "They remembered not his hand, nor the day when he delivered them from the enemy. How he had wrought his signs in Egypt, and his wonders in the field of Zoan"; and Psalms 95:9–10, "When your fathers tempted me, proved me, and saw my work. Forty years long was I grieved with this generation." So God represents himself as having his Holy Spirit vexed by the provocations of a people to whom he has been so wonderfully gracious. Isaiah 63:9–11, "In all their afflictions he was afflicted, and the angel of his presence saved them: in his love and in his pity he redeemed them, and carried them all the days of old. But they rebelled, and vexed his Holy Spirit: therefore he was turned to be their enemy, and he fought

-- 636 --

against them. Then he remembered the days of old, Moses, and his people, saying, Where is he that brought them up out of the sea with the shepherd of his flock? where is he that put his Holy Spirit within him?"4

[Prop.] IV. God therefore sometimes manifests his displeasure towards such a people by appearing ready to destroy them. He is not only displeased and made angry, but his displeasure is manifested. God manifests himself, as if he were about to accomplish his anger in some dreadful work, [and] as it were rises up out of his place in anger; yea, and stretches out his hand, as if he were just ready to cut them off. And the greatness of God's displeasure in such a manifestation of it, often appears in two things.

First. In the dreadfulness of the calamity that he seems to be going to bring upon [them]. He seems to be about to cut them off, or at least many of them: to cut 'em off from the enjoyment of means of grace, as worthy to enjoyment no longer: to cut 'em down out of his vineyard, as cumberers of his ground: to cut 'em off from his house, as being not worthy to be in his house any longer: to cut 'em off out of the land of the living, as unworthy to live upon earth: to take away at once all those great privileges that he has bestowed upon 'em in this world. So it was with the children of Israel in the text. He said he would pour out his fury upon them, to accomplish his anger against them in the wilderness. He said he would consume them, as [the] Ezekiel 20:13 of the context.

The greatness of God's displeasure appears in the severity of the blow that he seems to be about to strike, and in the dreadfulness of that rod that he lifts up, and seems to be going to strike with.

Second. In his appearing as if he were about to do this suddenly and immediately. The greatness of his displeasure appears in the suddenness and swiftness of the destruction he seems to be about to bring, as though his hand were actually lifted up, and he was just going to give the fatal blow; as though he were resolved to wait no longer, not to give 'em any more time. And it may be not so much as to ask for mercy, but as if he were to destroy them in a moment. Thus it was with the children of Israel, in that which the Prophet refers to in the text. Numbers 16:21, "Separate yourselves from among this congregation, that I may consume them in a moment." This holds forth the greatness of divine displeasure, as one whose anger5 is greatly excited is for immediate vengeance.

God thus manifests himself sometimes, just ready to execute some terrible judgment upon them two ways. Either,

-- 637 --

1. By immediate revelation, as he did in the instance spoken of in the text. He revealed himself to Moses, and spoke to 'em; and in what he said to them, he manifested, as though he were about immediately to destroy the people. Or,

2. In his providence. God may manifest this in and by his providence, as well as by his word, or by revelation. God may, and often does, threaten a people or particular persons in his providence, as well as in his word. And since immediate revelations have ceased, this is the only way in which God thus manifests himself; and that is by bringing particular persons or a people into such circumstances, as that they are in eminent and immediate danger of awful destruction.

[Prop.] V. God notwithstanding as it were withdraws his hand, and spares and delivers them. Though he be greatly displeased, and though he manifests his displeasure, as though he were just going awfully to accomplish his anger, and even actually stretches out his hand; yet he draws it back again, and don't strike that stroke that he seemed about to strike. He mercifully spares the lives of his people. Thus God often doth in such a case, as has been mentioned. God does as it were repent him of the evil that he was about to do. His pity and mercy prevails, and takes place of his wrath and displeasure. This is one character ascribed to God, that he is a God that repenteth him of the evil. Joel 2:13, "He is gracious and merciful, slow to anger, and of great kindness, and repenteth him of the evil." So it is said, God did towards Israel in the wilderness. Exodus 32:14, "And the Lord repented of the evil which he thought to do unto his people." So it was with Jerusalem. A drawn sword was lifted up over the city: the angel of the Lord had actually stretched out his hand to strike the fatal blow: and then it is said, God repented him of the evil. 2 Samuel 24:16, "And when the angel of the Lord stretched out his hand upon Jerusalem to destroy it, the Lord repented him of the evil." And so it [is] with the fig tree in the vineyard. Justice said, "Cut it down; why cumbereth it the ground?" But Mercy stepped in and said, "Let it alone this year also" (Luke 13:7–8).

Not that God really changes his mind, or properly repents. But there is such a manifestation of mercy in God's works, as there is [in] men of tender affections when about terribly to punish, but in the midst of it are overcome by bowels of pity. God manifests himself to men after the manner of men. And it livelily sets forth the mercy of God to his people, to compare it to that tender love that makes a father's heart relent, when he has lifted up his hand severely to strike a child. What a pathetical representation is there given of it in Hosea 11:8–9. "How shall I give thee up, Ephraim? how shall I deliver thee, Israel? how shall I make thee as

-- 638 --

Admah? how shall I set thee as Zeboim? mine heart is turned within me, my repentings are kindled together. I will not execute the fierceness of mine anger, I will not return to destroy Ephraim: for I am God, and not man; the Holy One in the midst of thee: and I will not enter into the city." God does sometimes as it were actually come forth in a whirlwind of wrath, as if to destroy a professing people. He actually raises the storm; but in the midst of it, while it is blowing, he mercifully abates the roughness of it, and stays it from those fatal effects that God seemed to be about to bring by it. Isaiah 27:8, "He stayeth his rough wind in the day of his east wind." God does as it were turn aside his own sword that is lifted over the heads of his people. As it comes down, Mercy turns it aside; so that it does as it were go beside them, and they are saved. Justice's displeasure throws a dart that is levelled at the heart; but Mercy interposes as a shield between, and causes it to glance aside, and the precious life is preserved. And therefore we read of God's turning away his anger. Psalms 78:38–39, "But he, being full of compassion, forgave their iniquity, and destroyed them not: yea, many a time turned he his anger away, and did not stir up all his wrath. For he remembered that they were bur flesh; a wind that passeth away, and cometh not again."

[Prop.] VI. When God doth thus withdraw his hand and spare his people, 'tis the effect of his undeserved and wonderful mercy. 'Tis because he is full of compassion, that he thus turns aside his anger, as is said in the place just mentioned.

It can't be from anything else, as the circumstances of its bestowment do make especially manifest. It can't be because of their worthiness; for 'tis their unworthiness that is the thing that God is provoked by, thus to appear ready to destroy them. It can't be because they ben't so unworthy as others; for 'tis the exceeding aggravation of their unworthiness, in that 'tis in his children, and those that he has done such great things for, that is the thing that especially displeases and grieves him. It can't be because God don't take notice how unworthy they be, and is not much offended at it; because he is then, in that very moment, taking notice of their unworthiness, and manifesting his great displeasure, even so that at that time he appears ready to accomplish his anger against them.

So that there is nothing left whence it should be that God should withdraw his hand, but only sovereign and infinite mercy. The consideration of two things may illustrate how this is of free and undeserved mercy.

First. It is because the mercy of God is in itself so exceeding great. From the greatness of his mercy, he repents him of the evil. As Psalms 106:45, "He remembered for them his covenant. He repented according to the multitude

-- 639 --

of his mercies." God by this shows himself as it were loth6 to hurt his people, though they so much deserve [it]: he is backward to be severe with them. Judgment is "his strange work": "he delights in mercy." As in the forementioned place in Joel, he is slow to anger, and repents him of the formentioned place in Joel, he is slow to anger, and repents him of the evil.

As a loving father is very loth to come to correct his child with severity, and so sometimes, when he is going about it, repents in the midst of it, his bowels yearn over his child. And though he lifted up his hand, as with an intention to strike a severe blow, yet his affections do as it were hold it back, or so abates the force of the blow, that the child is but lightly hurt.

This seems intimated in the text, in God's covenant people being called his children.

God often manifests in his Word that his delight is showing mercy, and not exercising judgment towards his people. Deuteronomy 5:29, "O that there were such an heart in them, that they would fear me, and keep all my commandments always, that it might be well with them, and with their children forever!" And Psalms 81:13–14, "Oh that my people had hearkened unto me, and Israel had walked in my ways! I should soon have subdued their enemies, and turned my hand against their adversaries." And Isaiah 48:18, "O that thou hadst hearkened to my commandments! Then had thy peace been as a river, and thy righteousness as the waves of the sea." So Christ is represented as weeping over the city of Jerusalem, to think what destruction they would bring on themselves by their evil ways, and pathetically wishing they had carried themselves better, that it might be well with them. Luke 19:41–44, "And when he was come near, he beheld the city, and wept over it, saying, If thou hadst known, even thou, at least in this thy day, the things which belong unto thy peace! but now they are hid from thine eyes. For the days shall come upon thee, that thine enemies shall cast a trench about thee, and compass thee round, and keep thee in on every side, and lay thee even with the ground, and thy children within thee, and shall [not] leave in thee one stone upon another; because thou knewest not the time of thy visitation." And God thus delighting in mercy; mercy does as it were overcome wrath. James 2:13, "Mercy rejoiceth against judgment."

Second. God exercises such mercy towards his people, not for their sakes, but for Christ's sake; as it is said in the text, "for his name's sake." It is for his name's sake, in that it is for Christ's sake, in that God's "name is in him" (Exodus 23:21). He is that name of the Lord that is a strong tower,

-- 640 --

where God's people are safe. Even at the time when God seems to be about to pour out his wrath, this tower shelters 'em from his wrath. So that though it may in some sense be said that mercy overcame justice, and rejoices against judgment; yet in Christ Jesus justice rather willingly yields to mercy. Justice withdraws its hands, and goes away satisfied, without the blood of God's offending people; because 'tis satisfied in the blood of their surety. For in Christ Jesus, "Mercy and truth are met together; righteousness and peace have kissed each other" (Psalms 85:10).

So that indeed into this is this wonderful dealing of God towards his people to be resolved, that when he seems to be about to cut them off, he yet stays his hand. It is because Christ interposes, as Moses, the type of Christ, did of old. Psalms 106:23, "Therefore he said that he would destroy them, had not Moses his chosen stood before him in the breach, to turn away his wrath, lest he should destroy them."

Christ is the shield that comes between the breasts of God's people and the sword of God's wrath, that turns it aside, that it don't give their hearts the justice levelled at them.

When God lifts up his hand to strike a professing people, Christ many a time as it were steps in, and holds back the hand of justice; so that there is no blow, or if there be, 'tis but a light one; so that they are corrected in measure, and are not delivered over unto death.

When God stretches forth his hand to cut them off, then oftentimes Christ as it were at that instant presents his blood to God's view; and that is always prevalent. God's wrath does as it were upon this at once fall, and he withdraws his hand. He beholds the face of his anointed, and turns away his hand from beholding their transgressions.7

APPLICATION.

I proceed now particularly to apply the doctrine that has been considered to ourselves of this town, with relation to that extraordinary providential event that we have lately been the spectators of, and many of us the subjects of, the last sabbath; which, considered in all its circumstances, is on many accounts exceeding surprising and wonderful. We were in a general surprise in the time of it. And when we consider the evident and remarkable hand of God in the disposal of the accident, we have reason to stand astonished still, and to wonder all the days of our lives. And indeed

-- 641 --

the more we consider, the more shall we see cause to stand and wonder. What is said in the text of God's people Israel, and his dealings with them, seems very applicable to the present case; and we have been remarkably the subjects of what is expressed in the doctrine.

We are a professing people of God; and not only so, as all other people professing the Christian religion are, but we are eminently so. 'Tis notorious to the world that we make a very high profession of religion, and that is manifest far and wide that there are multitudes among us that profess and hope that we are the children of God.

And that is most evident that God has done great things for us: God has done wonderful things for us of a spiritual nature: he in a remarkable manner has poured out his Spirit upon us, and showered down saving blessings amongst us; not only formerly from time to time, but especially of late. This is most evident and plain, and what we in general profess to have no doubt of. And probably there is not a town now on earth that has received greater mercy than we have. And there is scarce a family in the town, but what look on themselves to have been partakers of the saving benefits of this great work of God that has lately been wrought among us. One head of a family and another has seemed to rejoice and admire at the great things that God had done for his family, and spoken much of the greatness and inexpressibleness of God's mercy to him and his house, how he could never be thankful enough, and the like.

And besides spiritual mercies, we have on some accounts had remarkable and distinguishing temporal mercies; and particularly in that we have been so spared, and have rejoiced in so great a measure of health of late, from year to year, while other parts of the land all around, have been in distress with sore and mortal sweeping sickness.8 We have been wonderfully preserved from that distemper that has proved so terrible to other places far and near. God has executed wrath in other places, and poured out his fury, and his eye has not spared hundreds. And thousands of families have been covered with the shadow of death, and multitudes of houses have been almost emptied by it. Many parents have had all their dear children snatched away from them; have had several laid out dead in their houses at the same [time], and others sick and drawing near to death; and they have seen several laid in the same grave. How bitter and

-- 642 --

sore must the affliction of such parents be, and how distressing and doleful a time was in such houses, and how little do we know what it means. Many towns have in a great measure [been] emptied of their young people. How mournfully and sorrowfully do such streets appear, and how have they as it were mourned that used to be filled with children playing in them, but now are emptied of 'em: there is scarce any children left to play in them. And many places nigh us, though not visited to such a degree, yet God's dealings have been very awful in them. But we have been all the while preserved. And though the distemper has been amongst us, but one person has been taken away by it. We have rejoiced, while others have mourned; have had our children about us in health; have lain down and arose in quiet; and have had no such bitterness mingled with our meat and drink.

Such great things has God done for us of late. But whatever has been the cause, since he of late bestowed that unspeakable mercy of so remarkable a pouring out his Spirit, God has, besides mercies bestowed, appeared in many awful rebukes; and now, in the late surprising providence, has in an awful manner appeared just ready to destroy a multitude of us, but in a most wonderful manner has spared us.9 His leaving us to be insensible of the state that the meetinghouse was in, that we should take any care for our own preservation; and his ordering it that it should fall at such a time with so many persons upon it and under it in a sudden manner, without any warning, before any could have time to do anything for their own preservation, was as it were God's stretching forth his hand against us. But his so wonderfully taking care of every individual person that was exposed that not one life is hitherto lost, either of young or old, and not a bone broken that we know of, was a remarkable withdrawing his hand, and turning aside his wrath.

So that we have two things to consider and improve in this providence, viz. the rebuke and manifestation of divine displeasure in it, and the wonderful and surprising mercy of our preservation. And therefore I would consider and improve 'em, first, distinctly and, secondly, jointly; and that, first, to all of us in general and, second, particularly to those that have been the more immediate subjects of this providence.

I. Let us consider and improve the awful rebuke of God upon us and manifestation of his displeasure in this providence. Threatenings are a

-- 643 --

manifestation of divine displeasure as well as executions. When God said concerning Israel, as in the text, "I will pour out my fury upon them," it was a manifestation of displeasure, though God withdrew his hand, and wrought for his name's sake. In this providence, we were immediately threatened with the most awful blow that perhaps ever happened in the town. What an awful thing would it have been, if great numbers of people had been dashed in pieces, and we had seen our friends and neighbors lien1 dead in heaps, as amongst the timber and rubbish. It is a wonder we did not. What a mournful and sorrowful time would it now have been in the town. And this great destruction we were threatened with immediately and [in] a moment. When the part of the house began to sink, then those that were on it and under [it], were threatened with being sent into eternity in a moment. It was very doubtful with respect to all of them whether the next morning they should not be in eternity. Their life was in God's hand: God's hand was that moment lifted up over everyone's head, as if it was to strike a fatal blow. The dart was even within a few inches of their hearts. And the rebuke was the more awful that it should be on God's day, and in his house. When we were come into God's presence to worship him, and wait on him in the ways of his appointment for his blessing; even then, when we were appearing before God, before the throne of God, did God so rebuke us, as unworthy to come into his presence. He sent us out of his presence with an awful rebuke, as he has sent us out of his house, as not worthy to set our feet within the doors of it; and sent us away, many of us grievously wounded, groaning under pain from the sudden shake of his hand.

We were sitting before God, as the elders of Israel in the context, to inquire of God, to hear his word; but we had [an] awful shock from God. He as it were told us, as he did then, that he would not be inquired of by us; and he by his own awful hand stopped the preaching of the Word. And instead of hearing the voice of his word, he made us to hear the voice of shrieking and crying in all parts of the meetinghouse, a dolorous sound to be heard in the voice of [God]; an evident token that God did not dwell in our solemn assembly, and that he was not amongst us, but in a way of rebuke and displeasure.

The doctrine therefore leads us to improve this rebuke and manifestation of God's displeasure, by considering wherein we have carried ourselves unsuitably to the great things God has done for us. And we shall be sottish indeed, if we don't make this improvement of it. And therefore,

-- 644 --

First. Let us consider in the general how greatly we are backslidden; what a great alteration is there for the worse, from what there was here the year before last; how dull (we are now); in how great a degree have we forsaken God and Christ {from what we were}. Then we in general seemed to be lively {to God's word}. Religion was the great concern with everyone {then}. But why is it so much otherwise now? Then persons were eager to attend all opportunities, as if they could not have enough [of] 'em {Then} if a sermon [were] preached, how crowded {was the meetinghouse}. But now, don't religion begin to grow burdensome to many? {Many are} lifeless in private duties: {many are lifeless} in secret duties, and [in] worshipping God together with others: {many are} inclined to find out excuses {for themselves}.

Objections did [not] arise then. {Many} now can find objections: {many} don't know whether it be a duty {or a} superstition. {But now} 'tis inconvenient {to many} to spend lecture days and sabbath-day nights in religious, social worship: {now it is a} time not so proper as some other times

It may be we have been ready to lay the blame of this to God, and say that God don't pour out his Spirit now as he did then; and so excuse our backsliding with that, and say, "People used to grow dull, when God withdrew his Spirit; a lively spirit for religion won't last any longer than God continues to pour out his Spirit." But that is no excuse for our wickedness that God don't prevent it by his Spirit. We are mistaken if we think God is not provoked by it, and don't lay the blame of it to us, and will not require it of us because of that. Backsliding is a sin [that] especially provokes God with a people, as God often manifests by his prophets. And it is a sin that [he] is wont awfully to rebuke and punish a people for, as appears by the scripture history.

Don't let us excuse ourselves, for our shiftlessness and laziness in religion and the service of God, by laying of it to God. This will be the way to have God provoked the more to deal the more terribly with us.

'Tis no wonder that God has rebuked us for our backsliding. 'Tis agreeable to what he has threatened. Jeremiah 2:19, "Thine own wickedness shall correct thee, and thy backslidings shall reprove thee: know therefore and see that it is an evil thing and bitter, that thou hast forsaken the Lord thy God, and that my fear is not in thee, saith the Lord God of hosts."

Second. Let this rebuke of God upon us lead us to reflect on that worldly spirit that has of late prevailed amongst us. Have we not shamefully departed from God and Christ, that our hearts seemed to be so engaged after, and gone after, the world? Then the world seemed to be regarded only as a thing by the by. God and Christ and heaven were the great things then

-- 645 --

What is the matter? Is Christ and heaven less worth now than then?2 Do we need 'em less?

A worldly, covetous spirit is manifested two ways; viz. first, by inordinately seeking to get the world; and, secondly, in an inordinate lothness to part with it. Has there not been too much of a worldly spirit of late manifested in us in both these ways? Is there not a too violent spirit after the world? I trust this is so evident that not one in the town will deny it, and will say, "It is to be lamented; 'tis a sorrowful [time]; and we have cause to be ashamed." But han't the persons that say so also cause to be ashamed of their hypocrisy in talking so? If you acknowledge that there is so much such a spirit manifest, who is it is so worldly? Who is the man? Who is it has cause to be ashamed? Don't let us content ourselves with condemning things in general, and think that will suffice for us. We shall act an hypocritical part in so doing.

And let us inquire whether or no there has not been too evident an appearance of a worldly spirit in a lothness to part with the world for public uses, and for pious uses, in ways in which we have really been called to part with it. Don't let us spare ourselves, and cover and excuse our faults, at such a time as this, when God is rebuking of us. If we do, God won't spare us. And particularly, the late surprising providence of God is of such a nature that we shall be exceeding inexcusable, if we don't strictly inquire whether or no there has not been too much of a backward spirit to be at the expense of building an house for the worship [of God]. I don't deny but that persons must be allowed to speak their own judgments in such affairs; and persons might judge that there was no need to build a new meetinghouse through mistake, and not from a covetous spirit. But has there not been a great deal more than this, when it was determined in a regular way that there should be one built? Has there not been a manifestation of an edge upon some persons' spirits? And han't those that have determined it should be, been unsuitably reflected on; as if it were only from a spirit of pride that they would have a new one built? Though we must be allowed to have our own judgments in such affairs, yet is there any manner of need of our judging and reflecting on others that are of a different judgment from us? And has there not been, from the beginning of this affair, apparent too much of a backward spirit in some? Han't many showed a great lothness to be at the charge, and been frighted, and been too apt to complain of the heaviness of the burden? Han't what has been done been done, not with becoming cheerfulness, and with that freedom

-- 646 --

with which it becomes us to give our estates to God, but with complaining? Has it not at least been so with many?

Can any think it is pleasing to God to see a people that he has been so exceeding bountiful, when they are laying out to build an house for his worship, to do it with much fright at the charge and complaining; and not only so, but reflecting on one another for being the occasions of it? God did not bestow grudgingly {his wonderful mercies}. These did not come hard the year before last. {These did not come} to your children and families {grudgingly}.

And when such a backwardness was gently reproved from the pulpit, was it not taken ill by some persons? Did not they show that they did not like it?

It is probable many will now be ready to say in excuse for themselves that the charge is exceeding heavy: they must be very much pinched. And there are many in the town that now don't know which way in the world to pay their rate; and they will be very much put to it; and many will suffer under their burden. I answer that I believe it is so indeed: I believe you are much pinched, and put to it. And 'tis not at all to be wondered at: no other could be expected. You have taken a course to make it hard. When you have manifested yourself so distrustful of God, and so fearful and backward, how can it be expected that God should help you? How full is the Word of God of encouragements to us that we shall be no losers by laying out our estates for religious uses; that if we honor God with our substance {and estates}, how much encouragement that God will take care, and carry us through in ways that we never thought of; that he will contrive for us, and help us along, and provide for us, and give us much more. And what a pitiful thing is it for Christians to be so fearful of trusting God with their estates, that they durst run no ventures for God on the encouragements of his promises. If you have been frighted at the undertaking, and been full of complaints about it, no wonder you are pinched. Remember what was told you from the pulpit the last year, viz. that such a complaining of the heaviness of the burden, was the way to make it much heavier;3 and that, as God had been frowning on us in outward concerns then, by the backwardness of the spring and by the destructive flood and grand4 drought; so if this was continued, we might depend upon it, it would be so still. It may be some of you disliked this in the time of the [worship],

-- 647 --

not said without warrant from the Word of God. But how has it proved? I suppose that God took away more from the town by wasting judgments the last year, than all the charge comes to, [that] you have hitherto been at about the meetinghouse. I suppose hundreds of pounds were taken away by the flood in bridges, and mills, and damage to the seed in the ground. And much was taken away by the exceeding backwardness of the spring, and afterwards by drought, by which, and other means, the fruits of the earth were greatly cut short; as proved when the harvest came to be gathered in, by which the town produced many hundred pounds less than they used to. And again, I suppose [God] took away in the latter end of the year, several hundreds of pounds in our cattle in an unusual manner, and hundreds of pounds5 in the sheep. And we may expect that God will take away our substance still, by one judgment or other, if we behave no more answerably to God's expectations than we have. And let it be remarked and remembered, for God will fulfill his word. If we trust God, and go on cheerfully with our duty, God will help us and provide for us. But if we durst not trust him, he will leave us to take care for ourselves as well as we can, and in this way [can] expect to be pinched exceedingly in the remaining part of the charge of the meetinghouse.

Very likely you may be ready to say that I that speak thus bear none of the charge; and that therefore it is no wonder I am forward to leave others free to bear it. But whatever influences the speaker has, God is witness whether what has been said ben't warranted by his Word, and what the present circumstances of the town requires. And if it be so, God will require of you an acceptance of this word, as if spoken by himself. And however any may dislike what has now been spoken, God will still in his providence confirm and justify it; as he has the like word that has been spoken in time past.6

Most certainly, so many remarkable frowns of divine providence on a town are not for nothing. God's meaning in his external providence towards a people, is much more easily discerned than towards a particular person; because, as to persons, all things in many respects come alike to all in this world. The time for distinguishing persons is in another world, but 'tis not so as to a people. They are rewarded or punished according to their deeds in this world.

The year before last year was when religion was so lively in the town. We were most remarkably blessed in our outward concerns, {our} health,

-- 648 --

{our} plenty, {our worldly spirit}. But since we have in such a measure left God to pursue after the world, what have we got by it? Do we get the world faster {by it}? Are we better provided for? {Do we have} a greater fullness? {Do we have} less want and difficulty {by it}? Does our care, now [that] our cares are so much taken up about {the world}, keep off losses.

Some may be ready to wonder that God should frown upon town this so, when there are so many good people; but it is not at all to be wondered at. We are the most likely people to be frowned on in our temporal affairs, if we don't carry ourselves as becomes the gospel, of any people in the land, and the more, for the great things God has done for us. Amos 3:2, "You only have I known of all the families of the earth: therefore I will punish you for all your iniquities."

It is never at all to be expected that this town will flourish in their temporal concerns in any other way, than seeking first the kingdom of God, and his righteousness.

Third. Has there not been manifest, from time to time, too much of a heat of spirit, and a disposition to contention, in our public affairs? It is shameful there should be such a spirit manifest amongst such a people as we are that make such a profession, and that God has done such things for.

The year before last {was a} time of love and peace.

What is the reason then, whenever anything is moved of a public nature by some that is contrary to the judgment and opinion of others, that there is apt presently an edge upon persons' spirits, and one side reflecting on the other, one calling the other proud, and they calling them covetous; and, it may [be], something of ridiculing one another behind each other's back? Is it not a shameful thing that a Christian people can't differ in opinion about their temporal concerns, without their spirits raised, and reflecting one on another?

Fourth. Let it be inquired what frame did this surprising providence {of God} find us in. Was there not consideration of this spirit of strife, spirits of many greatly edged, {many} reflectings {on one another}; yea, something of ridiculing in a paper set up. Who the person was that did it, I know not. I hope he is now ashamed of it. Let people act their own judgment in public affairs; and let [them] offer all the reasons that they can for it; and in all ways [be] consistent with meekness and charity. Let 'em endeavor to have that done that is agreeable to their judgments. But what good is promoted by reflection, and backbiting, and ridicule {of one another}? Does love to God require {it}? Does a spirit of love to our neighbor require it? No wonder that God gave us such a rebuke in a time when we are in a frame so displeasing to him.

-- 649 --

Let none imagine that I say these things in reproof of others, because I would wipe myself clean; or think that I am free from any share in provoking God to frown on us. This should be far from me; and it would be very provoking to God indeed in such a day of rebuke, when we are called to humble ourselves for our own iniquities.7 But as I am the messenger of the Lord of Hosts to you, God calls me to reprove and warn you at such a time of rebuke as this, at the same time that he calls me to humble myself before him with you. And he requires you to accept the reproof as his reproof.

Fifth. Let it be inquired how we have improved our sabbaths. From time to time God has given us surprising and amazing rebuke on a sabbath: { the death of} Mr. Hawley {by his own hand}; and a little while ago one {failed attempt by another};8 and now in the midst of public exercise {the falling of the gallery}. Certainly we are called upon to inquire how we keep sabbaths. Don't God see something amongst us on sabbath days that is very unbecoming?

Is there care about the beginning of the sabbath {days and nights}? And how is the sabbath spent by us at home before and after meetings? This is one thing mentioned in the text—"they polluted my sabbaths"—and particularly insisted in the context, as has been shown.

Sixth. Let it [be] particularly inquired how we have of late attended on God's public worship. This we are abundantly called to by such a rebuke in the time of worship. In the late wonderful time of the pouring {out of the Spirit}, you were in general very attentive {and of an} amiable appearance. Has there not here been too much of drowsy, sleepy spirit {of late}? You have often been told of it:9 how dishonorable it was to God; what an unsuitable behavior for God's house, which is no place to sleep in; {how dishonorable} to sit in God's presence, while his Word is preached in his name. {You} have been told what notice strangers would take of it {if you slept then}. If God's speaking to you in his Word won't keep you awake, God has shown you how he can speak so as to wake you effectually. If there was any that began to drowse at that time, without doubt they were thoroughly waked. If you will sleep, God has shown you sleep. God has shown you, how he can cause that you shall never wake more in this world; [and] strike you in a moment into your last sleep, that your first waking shall be in eternity.

-- 650 --

[1.] Let us search and try all our ways and doings. Have we been careful not to pollute God's name in the sight of others, in whose sight has [judged us]? Let us each inquire what particular way of sin we {have followed}. 'Tis common to overlook the main [way].

Some may possibly be ready to think we do pretty well, because there are a great many religious meetings kept up in the town, and because there is a pretty deal of religious discourse. But what signifies religious talk and religious meetings in God's sight, without judgment and mercy and faith, without a spirit of charity and fruits brought forth in an holy life? Certainly 'tis of no value. As God says, "When ye come to appear before me, who hath required this at your hands,…wash ye, make ye clean, put away the evil of your doings from before mine eyes; cease to do evil; learn to do well" (Isaiah 1:12, Isaiah 1:16–17).

2. In the second place, let those particularly whose lives were more immediately exposed by that surprising providence, be put upon it to search and try their ways. We are all of us loudly called upon, but you especially. If you ben't affected, and don't seriously reflect on yourself, and ben't humbled; great indeed will be your provocation in the sight of God. If you are senseless, what will make you sensible?

Inquire whether or no you han't been guilty in those things mentioned, and in other things.

(1) Those of you that are in a natural condition. Han't you been secure? {Han't you been} carelessly neglecting your souls? {Han't you been} careless in God's house? {Han't you been} negligently hearing God's word? Han't you been asleep of late? If you have, this providence does most remarkably show you how that, if you sleep in a natural condition, you sleep on the brink of hell. And it is a wonder that you that fell then, when you dropped with the gallery that fell, that you did not drop into hell. 'Tis a wonder that you stopped before you got to hell. And if you were under [it], 'tis a greater wonder that you was not struck into hell by that blow. A wonder of divine preservation it was, that it was not so.

This providence, one would think, should be sufficient to wake you up. If you are asleep still, 'tis to be feared you never will wake, till you wake in hell.

How awful would that have been, to have been taken directly out of the house of God, and from hearing a sermon, and sent in a moment to hell.

(2) Many of you [that] hope yourselves to be converted. Consider your ways. Han't you backslidden, shamefully carried yourself unsuitably {and} proudly in a senseless, careless, negligent frame; {carried yourself in a} carnal frame, minding vanity; thoughts taken up about trifles {of the world};

-- 651 --

lost your first love {of God}; swallowed up with other things? If so, God was doubtless greatly displeased and offended. No wonder he lifted up his hand thus against you.

Particularly inquire in what manner you have lately attended the public worship. How have you heard sermons, {your} eyes wandering, {your} thoughts in the ends of the earth? How was it with you then at that time?

II. Let us improve the great mercy of God to us in so wonderful a preservation, to praise and thankfulness of so many that were so much exposed by his accident. There are other things that pertain to this providence that are remarkable, but this is the most remarkable thing belonging to it by far, the wonderful preservation.

If people abroad should hear that such an accident fell out; [that] such a part of the meetinghouse fell at once, with all {the timber and rubbish} on the heads of so many {under it}; and all so suddenly, that there was not time for those whose seats were not that way; and should be left to guess at the effect, would they have a thought of any other than {God stretching forth his hand against us}? If we consider the nature of the thing, we shall see cause to think that there was, in the falling of the gallery, a particular merciful care of the Watchman of Israel over every particular person that was above and below to order his place so, and the motion of all the timber about him, to preserve him.1

What wonderful mercy is here! What an affecting, endearing tenderness of God towards [us], a lothness as it were to hurt us, or to see us hurt. How gracious was the watchful, omniscient eye of God, even when his hand was lifted up. How [he] has dealt with us, though so unworthy, as a parcel of little dear children. He took care that no life should be lost. Mercy stepped in at that time, and had its effects in all parts of the falling gallery, and the space under it. God took care of them that fell as it were to ease 'em down, lest they should fall too hard. And he took care of all that were beneath, to turn the heavy timbers aside in all those places where they came with peculiar force, that they might not dash them in pieces. He was tender, and took care, lest they should have too hard a blow.

How sottish indeed shall we be, if we ben't affected with thankfulness at such a mercy. And you in particular, that have been the immediate subjects of such a remarkable and almost miraculous preservation, what cause have you of praise. Is there one heart among you all that is not affected with such an instance of the kindness and mercy of God to you? If

-- 652 --

there be, 'tis to be feared God will set a mark upon that person. Do you consider who it is has preserved [you]; or are you so stupid, as to attribute all that to blind chance? If you han't a heart of flint, yea, of adamant, certainly such mercy, such tenderness will melt it. O, take heed that you give God the glory he expects. Call on your soul and all {your heart}, and use care and prayer that the impression may never be wore off; and that the effect may always abide {with you}. How apt are we to lose impressions of such things, {his wonderful preservation and mercy}.

We have all great reason to praise God for other mercies, {such as our} health {that was} preserved in so great and dangerous a work as raising the new meetinghouse. But there is that which is peculiarly affecting in this wonderful mercy.

And the consideration of our great unworthiness, should the more affect {us} to consider how much we deserved a more awful rebuke; how just, if scores of people had been dashed in pieces. If one person had been found dead,2 it would more than double the awfulness of the accident. But so has God ordered that everyone {was preserved}. What cause is there on this occasion to use that expression of the Psalmist, "O that men would praise the Lord for his goodness, and for his wonderful works to the children of men!" (Psalms 107:8).3

I proceed now, in the third and last place,

[III.] Jointly to consider both the rebuke and mercy, and make improvement of both together. And the improvement I would make of it, is of exhortation to hearken to and obey the call of God in it. There seems to be

First. A loud call. It is to us to reform all our evil ways, and to walk more becoming the great things God has done for us. The manifestation of God's displeasure in bringing such an accident, and his marvelous mercy in preserving us in [it], do both aloud call to this. There scarcely could have been a providence so circumstanced, as to contain greater incitements to this; much greater than if we had had judgment without mercy. For now God has manifested his awful displeasure at our sins, to deter us from them; and yet in such a way, as yet to spare and show great mercy to us. He has awfully warned us, and endearingly drawn us, both at the same time. It also contains greater incitements to this than if there had been only mercy, without such an awful manifestation {of his displeasure}. For now God has at the same time that he has shown us such great mercy, awfully

-- 653 --

put us in mind of our unworthiness of any mercy: our unworthiness to be in his house; {our unworthiness} to come to the throne of grace; {our unworthiness} to be on the earth. If we still continue to walk as unsuitably as we have done, we shall show ourselves as both exceeding daring and monstrously base and ungrateful at the same time. We shall show hardness of heart with a witness. By no means let there be such obstinacy and irreclaimableness manifest in us. Can we be any other than affected with such a providence, so wonderfully circumstanced as this is? And when we consider our sins that have procured the rebuke, and by which we are unworthy of the mercy, can we do any other than loathe ourselves for our unsuitable behavior?

Let us rise up with one accord, with indignation against our sins. Let this be an effectual alarm to us. And let us all not dispute the case at all, but all go forth as one man against those vile lusts. And let us make thorough work with 'em, and never leave till we have crucified 'em. God has dealt tenderly with us that we might not deal tenderly with ourselves. We can't serve our sins too roughly: let us not be afraid of being too cruel to 'em.

Let God's rebuke and wonderful mercy that are thus mingled, have the same effect on us as the Apostle's rebuke had on the Corinthians of which we read. 2 Corinthians 7:11, "Behold this selfsame thing, that ye sorrowed after a godly sort, what carefulness it wrought in you, yea, what clearing of yourselves, yea, what indignation, yea, what fear, yea, what vehement desire, yea, what zeal, yea, what revenge!"

Let us be filled with a spirit of indignation against ourselves for our sins, and revenge against ourselves. This is certainly the improvement God expects that we should make of providence.

Let us consider that God won't always deal in such tenderness with us, if we won't hearken. Isaiah 7:9, "If ye will not believe, surely ye shall not be established." So it was with Israel. God brought rebukes upon 'em, and yet spared 'em in the wilderness a great while, when they carried themselves unworthily under those4 great things God had done for 'em. But at last he swore in his wrath that their carcasses should fall in the wilderness. God has [been] following of us with one judgment after another, and correcting us in measure, and we would not hearken. And now God seemed to be just going to bring a far more awful judgment than all the rest upon us, but seemed loth to do it. He repented in the midst of it, and turned away his anger, and has wonderfully spared [us]. But if we still won't regard, let us not expect to be spared any more.

-- 654 --

God has spared us hitherto, and seems as though he were determined to wait upon us a while longer, to see if we won't regard.

If we think to escape divine judgments as much as other people, with living no better than other people, we are much mistaken. No such thing is to be expected. We are a city set on an hill, and the honor of religion, and the honor of God, doth greatly depend on our behavior. But if we won't take any care of God's honor, God will take care himself by executing vengeance on us, that his name may not be polluted amongst those in whose sight he brought them forth.

Our obligations were exceeding great before by reason of the great things God had done for us, but now 'tis greater; for God has done another great thing for us. And if we go on to behave unsuitably, our provocation will be far greater than ever, and God won't always deliver [us]; but we must expect that the next time, when God's hand is lifted up, that it will come down upon us with its full might.

Second. Here is a loud call to us better to improve our sabbaths and sermons, and to prepare for death. God but a little while ago, by suddenly taking away one by death between meetings, that was here well in the forenoon in the house of God hearing his word, put in mind how uncertain we are, when we are at meeting, whether ever we shall be allowed to set foot {in another}; when we are hearing a sermon, whether ever [we shall] hear another. But now he has shown that we are hearing a sermon that is begun, how uncertain that we shall live to hear that sermon out; {how} but that we shall [be] sent immediately out of God's house into eternity.

I would also particularly apply myself, under this head, to those that have been the more immediate subjects of the late remarkable providence. Be exhorted to hear the call of God to you in it. 'Tis a call to all, but more especially to you.

1. Be exhorted to comply with the call in the things already mentioned. Now God has only warned you: he han't cut you off. Beware what shall come next. God certainly won't give this warning for nothing. Remember, and depend upon it. Reform your evil ways: better improve your opportunities for time to come.

2. Be exhorted now with your whole hearts to devote your lives to God. What will you not now make an entire dedication of your lives to God, when God has so remarkably preserved your lives?

Your lives were in his hand. How easily could God have diverted an heavy piece of timber with force upon your head, that it should have dashed in pieces in a moment. What opportunity had you to take care for your own safety.

-- 655 --

If you have never given yourself to God, now do it.

If you have, do it now renewedly, and with greater ardor of soul than ever; otherwise you surely won't behave answerable to what God has done for you, and will show yourself ungrateful. And if you now won't give yourself to God, how can you expect that for the time to come he should [take] care of you as his; but leave you to yourself, to preserve your own life, and preserve yourself from destroying calamities, as well as you can.
