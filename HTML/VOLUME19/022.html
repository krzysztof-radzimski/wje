THE FREE AND VOLUNTARY SUFFERING AND DEATH OF CHRIST

COMMENTARY on a sermon manuscript usually is no more than an account of its physical condition and sometime history, an inventory of its leaves, damage, deletions, transpositions, preaching units, and the like, as well as its proximate date of composition and the number, possibly the sites, of its repreachings. Now and then, something else crops up, an unexpected form, that may influence the reading of the whole; an enhanced, isolated phrase like "LOVE TO GOD" is one instance of it (p. 487), a repetitive shorthand notation might be another. Just above the text on the first leaf of each of three sermon manuscripts in this volume, Edwards entered a series of successive symbols in later ink, a "decibel rating" of sorts, a gauge (and recollection) of its rhetoric: in Light in a Dark World, a Dark Heart (pp. 704–33) he logged four such symbols to signify a fairly powerful rhetoric, in The Many Mansions (pp. 734–46) six symbols to signify a powerful rhetoric, and here fully nine to signify a most powerful rhetoric, a level of imprecation rarely exceeded or seldom met in the extant sermon manuscripts.1

Intensity permeates the prose: various forms and combinations of free, voluntary, and willing cascade through it, each word repeated between twenty and thirty times; initial phrases like "It glorified," "It was the sins," "Here it appeared," "That he," follow hard upon one another, growing strident. Since Edwards eschews punctuation of even a rudimentary kind in the sermon manuscripts, perhaps the best way to mimic the inflection sentiment is to string together affined groups of sentences with colons, a device that recognizes the conjunction of structures and the rush and fever of argument, and one he later adopted to great effect in revising sermon manuscripts for publication in Five Discourses. Nine symbols or none, there is matter enough in the narrative of Christ's passion in the gospel accounts or in Edwards' impassioned retelling for his throes of rhetoric and the devices that serve them.

-- 492 --

"Christ did not kill himself: the death of Christ was a murder." So much is plain; but just as plain, and more to Edwards' purposes, is that Christ was a willing agent in his own death, laying down his life for sinners "with great delight," a perfectly free and voluntary act pledged before the world began, part of his priesthood as God-man. As God, his death and resurrection were prefigured in types and shadows in the Old Testament—the unconsumed burning bush in Exodus, the ascending flames of the sacrificial

-- 493 --

kid in Judges, the return of the captured ark of the covenant in I Samuel—but not so his birth: born of a handmaiden of "low degree," a carpenter's son, vilified in a corrupt age. As man, he suffered an agonizing death so sinners would glorify God, "a divine contrivance," Edwards calls it, that is both a tribute of the Son to the authority, holiness, justice, grace, and wisdom of the Father and a testimony of his love for the elect. To advance their glory by his suffering and disgrace was "sweet" to him, so much so that he "took as no kindness" Peter's objection to it (Matthew 16:23). Still, if Christ had not known beforehand of his coming pains and torments, he could not will himself to endure them; but he perfectly knew from eternity that suffering awaited him, though not the "dreadful cup" of it until Gethsemane; and he willingly sanctioned it, because he knew as well the depravity of man, though not, again, with a "lively sense" of it until Golgotha. So complete was his love that he died not only for the awful sins of his crucifers but for the common failings of his disciples, a "specimen," Edwards reminds the saints, of the corruption even of the godly. Because Christ was both passive and active in his death, that is, in his obedience to God and in his willingness to die, it was both satisfactory and righteous, on the one hand a redemption from sin, on the other a redemption to God. And because his last act was obedience to a positive precept, namely, the laying down of his life, he merited heaven for himself and purchased it for sinners.2

* * *

The manuscript is, in fact, two manuscripts, one of twenty duodecimo leaves augmented by two and a half leaves of another manuscript of sixteen, the first preached in April 1736 (L. 1r.), the other undated but probably before 1733. The additional leaves (LL. 6v.–8v.) come from a sermon on Matthew 16:21–23, as Edwards notes in the later manuscript (L. 8v.)—"vid serm in math 16.21. &cp. 6th &c"—and become the argument for the second proposition of the Doctrine, which ends the first preaching unit of each sermon (L. 8v.), just before the Application in each (L. 9r.).3 Of the major manuscript, there is a shorthand notation after the text, "preached doctrine part of this the second time," but no indication of where or when; there are three signs of transposition (LL. 6r., 6v., and

-- 494 --

7r.), relatively few strikeouts or insertions, a pair of brackets (LL. 2v.–3r.), and no extended deletions; much of L. 19v. is blank, all of LL. 20r. and 20v. is. Of the other manuscript, decibel symbols follow the citation (L. 1 r.), but at three, they cause only a momentary stay against the nine of The Free and Voluntary Suffering and Death of Christ.

THE FREE AND VOLUNTARY SUFFERING AND DEATH OF CHRIST
No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. John 10:18

CHRIST in this chapter makes comparison between himself and false teachers and impostors. To show the great difference, he tells us that those false teachers were thieves and robbers, but that he is the shepherd of the sheep, as in the John 10:1–2. He tells us the difference there is between them in the respect that the sheep have to them. As to him, the sheep know his voice and follow him; but as to them, they being strangers, the sheep will not follow them; "for they know not the voice of strangers." The difference also appears in the different end for which they come. Those false teachers, who are but thieves and robbers, they come for no other end but to steal, and to kill, and to destroy; but he comes that the sheep may have life, and that they may have it more abundantly. And another thing wherein they differ is that he is the owner of the sheep. The sheep are his, but those false teachers are hirelings. The sheep are not their own, and therefore don't exercise any further care in keeping the sheep than only just to get their hire. And another thing wherein they differ is their different behavior towards the sheep. False teachers, when they see danger coming, their only concern is for themselves to save their own lives, and therefore leave the sheep to be destroyed. But he, being the good shepherd, will expose his own life, and lay it down to save the lives of the sheep; as in the John 10:11, "I am the good shepherd: the good shepherd giveth his life for the sheep. But he that is an hireling, and not the shepherd, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth: and the wolf catcheth them, and scattereth the sheep." He that is an hireling, whose own the sheep are not, when he sees the wolf coming, all that he is concerned for is to get out of the way to save his own life, and cares not what becomes of the lives of the sheep. But Christ, being the good shepherd, he stands between

-- 496 --

the wolf and the flock to defend them, though he exposes his own life by it. Yea, he receives the mischief all upon himself to defend the sheep from it: he yields up his own life to save their lives. And this is that which Christ speaks of in the text, and insists on [in] an argument of his being the good shepherd, viz. his laying down his life for the sheep. And to show how that this evidences his being a good shepherd, he declares that he is free and voluntary in it; and this is asserted two ways in the text.

1. That no other takes it from him. 'Tis not meant that others were not active and voluntary in taking away Christ's life, or that those that had an hand in Christ's crucifixion were at all to be excused, as if it was not their act; for they were guilty of the murder of Christ. The guilt of this is often charged upon them. Acts 2:23, "Him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and with wicked hands have crucified and slain"; and many other places.

But what is intended is that no man took it from him without his voluntarily subjecting himself to death, and yielding up his life. Without this, no man could take it from him; and the reason is given, viz. because he had the power of his own life: "I have power to lay it down, and power to take it again." Christ is the Prince of Life: he has life in himself, and is the sovereign disposer of life and death. And therefore 'tis impossible any should have such power over him, as to deprive him of his life, unless he voluntarily subjected himself to their power to that end, that his life might be taken away.

2. 'Tis positively affirmed that he lays it down of himself. Though Christ was cruelly murdered by his enemies, yet there was no act that Christ was more perfectly free in than laying down his life.

DOCTRINE.

Christ was voluntary in subjecting himself to these sufferings that he underwent.

Christ did not kill himself: the death of Christ was4 a murder. It was wholly and entirely done by those wicked men that from hatred to him sought his life, and imbrued their5 hands in his blood, and by Satan that instigated them. But as it was a sacrifice, so it was entirely Christ's own free

-- 497 --

and voluntary act. Nor was there ever anything done more voluntarily and freely by Christ than offering that sacrifice of his own life. To show this I would first, show that it was a voluntary thing in him as God and, second, as man.

1. Christ as God was perfectly free and voluntary in this thing. The life that was laid down was not the life of the divine nature; but notwithstanding, it was the life of a divine person that was laid down. And though it was not the divine life but the human life of that person that was laid down, yet there was the act of Christ as God in it as well as man. It was not only the human nature of Christ that was concerned in offering up that sacrifice to God.

The offering up the life of Christ, though it was only of the human life, yet the offering was made by Christ not only as man but as God-man. It was an act of the priesthood of Christ. But all acts of the priesthood of Christ are performed by Christ as God-man. It is that which renders the act meritorious, because it is not only the act of Christ as man, but also as God.

If Christ had offered the sacrifice only as man, and it were to be looked upon as the act of the manhood, it would not have been meritorious, nor would it ever have availed for our salvation.

That life that was offered up, though it was only the human life of Christ; yet being the life of a divine person, it was that which the whole person has a propriety in. And therefore not only the man but God is said to have laid down his life. 1 John 3:16, "Hereby perceive we the love of God, because he laid down his life for us." And so Christ's blood is called God's own blood. Acts 20:28, "Feed the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood." But that Christ as God was perfectly free and voluntary in this, may appear by the following things:

First. 'Tis inconsistent with his Godhead that he should be any other wise than perfectly free and voluntary in it. Compulsion is a thing that is not compatible to the Deity. The divine nature which exists and acts of itself, and is absolutely supreme, independent, and self-sufficient, can't be liable to any force, or to do anything, or be subject to anything against or besides a perfectly free choice or act of the will. And it being so, as we have just shown, that the subjecting of the person of Christ to the sufferings that he underwent was an act of Christ as God; so it must be a perfectly free act, because he was God. The Deity can as soon cease to be, as in any wise to act otherwise than with perfect freedom.

Second. As it was inconsistent with the divine nature, so it was also inconsistent with the right of a divine person, that he should be any other

-- 498 --

wise than perfectly free and voluntary. Christ as a divine person is originally of his own right in an absolute manner, and in no respect subject to any. Christ is not originally in any subjection to God the Father; for "being in the form of God, he thought it not robbery to be equal with God." 'Tis true that Christ in his office as Mediator is subject to the Father, and was so before the human nature of Christ had any being. But he became so only of his own free and voluntary act.

He was not subject to the Father before he had undertaken the office, and he was no way obliged to take upon him that office. It was his own free act, who in doing of it, perfectly knew what belonged to that office, and what must be done and undergone in it. This is the argument that Christ makes use of in the verse of the text, viz. that [he] had his life in his own right, had power to lay it down, and power to take it again.

Though we often read of Christ's being sent into the world, and appointed to the work of a Mediator, and given and delivered up of the Father to die; and that he received this commandment of the Father that he should lay down his life, and that he laid it down in obedience to him. Yet that don't argue that it was [not] his own free act; and that two ways. It was a free act of obedience, for Christ was willingly obedient. Psalms 40:7–8, "Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy will, O my God."

And not only so. Christ was not only willing in consequence of his Father's commandments, but he was free and willing with an absolute independency, and not as subject to a rule. He was willing antecedent to his Father's commandment; for it was in consequence of this willingness of his, that he became subject to the Father's commandment in this affair. For, as has been already observed, it was by his taking upon him the office of a Mediator that rendered him subject to the Father's commandment; and he undertook this office, because he was willing to die for sinners. This was the reason why he became subject. And therefore Christ mentions his having his being voluntary in laying down his life, and having his right in his own life, and also his laying it down in obedience to the Father, all together in the verse of the text, as not being inconsistent the one with the other. "No man taketh it from me, but I lay it down of myself. I have power to lay it down, and I have power to take it again. This commandment have I received of my Father."

Third. Christ manifested that it was what he chose and was voluntary in, by what he from time to time spoke of it of old, long before it was accomplished. I would here mention two places in particular. The first is what Christ says of himself, speaking how it was with him before the foundation

-- 499 --

of the world. Proverbs 8:31, "Rejoicing in the habitable parts of his earth; and my delights were with the sons of men." Christ here has doubtless respect to what he had undertaken for men before the world was created. He had delight in the foresight of it: he delighted in men then as he was their Savior, and as they were to be saved by his death and sufferings.

And the other place is that in the Psalms 40:6–8, "Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ear hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not required. Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy will, O my God; yea, thy Law is within my heart."6

Here it is Christ that speaks, as is evident by the Apostle's attributing the words to Christ in Hebrews 10:5–7. And he is here speaking of his laying down his life, instead of offering those sacrifices and burnt offerings that are mentioned; which is also evident by the application the Apostle makes of them in the forementioned place. And how does Christ here speak of this, his laying down his life? As his free act, and that which he undertook and subjected himself to, of free choice. Or as it might have been rendered, "Mine ear hast thou bored," alluding to the law of the servant that had his ear bored, who, when the year of release came, had his liberty; but if he voluntarily chose his master's service, he should have his ear bored, and serve him forever. And so the servant learnt obedience by the things that he suffered. But Christ here declares that he did not only freely choose to obey God in thus laying down his life, but that he did it with great delight. He delighted in this act of obedience. "Lo, I come," says he, "and I delight to do thy will, O God; and thy Law is within my heart."

The same appears by speaking so often of his own sufferings by his spirit in the Prophets; for it was the spirit of Christ in them that they spoke by, when they spoke of his sufferings; as appears by 1 Peter 1:11, "Searching what, or what manner of time the spirit of Christ which was in them did signify, when it testified beforehand the sufferings of Christ, and the glory that should follow." It appears that the consideration or foresight of his death and sufferings for sinners was what he delighted in, by its being a theme that he so much dwelt upon of old, long before the appointed time of his suffering.

Fourth. The same appears by Christ's representing his death of old in types and shadows. There are abundance of typical representations of the death of Christ in the Old Testament, which were of Christ's own appointment; for it was this was the angel of God's presence that redeemed

-- 500 --

them, and carried them all the days of old; as Isaiah 63:9. It was Christ that was wont to appear to the children of Israel, that brought them out of Egypt, and appeared in the pillar of cloud and fire, and appointed those typical ceremonies and representations of his own death. This showed that Christ had delight in the foresight of his death, that he so many ways shadowed it forth. But I would mention two or three typical representations of Christ's death in particular, wherein the representation consisted in Christ's own immediate act.

One is Christ's appearing to Moses in the burning bush, which we read of in Exodus 3:2, "And the angel of the Lord appeared unto him in a flame of fire out of the midst of a bush: and he looked, and, behold, the bush burned with fire, and the bush was not consumed." This burning bush represents the church in Egypt that were persecuted there, and yet not consumed. But not only so, but it represents Christ in his passion; for it was Christ that appeared to Moses in that bush; as is evident, because he is called "the angel of the Lord." And it was not a created angel, but the angel of the covenant; as is evident, because he tells Moses that he is the God of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob, and that his name is "I am that I am." This bush that he appeared in represents the human nature of Christ that is called the branch, and is the growth of the earth. It was not an high tree, but a low bush, to represent Christ in his state of humiliation, when he was as "a tender plant, and a root out of a dry ground."

Christ was said to have dwelt in this bush. Deuteronomy 33:16, "With the good will of him that dwelt in the bush." So Christ dwells in the human nature, betabernacled in flesh. This bush burned with fire to represent how the human nature of Christ suffered in the fire of God's wrath. As the bush burned and was not consumed, so Christ's human nature suffered extremely, endured the wrath of God, but yet did not utterly perish, but revived again out of the flame. God did not leave his soul in hell, nor suffer his Holy One to see corruption. The fire spent all its force upon the bush; but yet because of the divinity that dwelt in it, it was impossible it should be consumed. So the wrath of God spent all its force on the man Christ Jesus; but yet because of the divinity that dwelt in him, it was impossible that he should be utterly destroyed, but came to life again on the third day, and ascended up to heaven.

Another is what Christ did when he appeared to Manoah, that we read of in the Judges 13. That angel was no created angel, but it [was] Christ the angel of the covenant; as appears by what Manoah said, when the angel disappeared, in the Judges 13:22, "We shall surely die, because we have seen God." We read in [the] Judges 13:19–20

-- 501 --

Judges 13:19–20 that Manoah by his direction "took a kid with a meat offering, and offered it upon a rock unto the Lord," while the angel was present; and that "the angel did wondrously, and Manoah and his wife looked on. For it came to pass, when the flame went up towards heaven from off the altar, that the angel of the Lord ascended in the flame of the altar."

Christ, by thus going into the flame in which the kid was sacrificed, and ascending up in it, signified that he was the great sacrifice that was represented by that kid that was then burning in the flame; and that he was the sacrifice that was to ascend up as a sweet savor to God from off the altar. In the flame of God was [Christ]. And therefore while the kid was burning, Christ went into that very flame and ascended up in it.

Another thing whereby Christ of old represented his delivering up himself to his suffering, was his delivering up the ark, the symbol of his presence into captivity, to the Philistines; which we read of in the 1 Samuel 4, and which is also spoken of in [the] Psalms 78:61, "He delivered his strength into captivity, and his glory into the enemy's hand." He that dwelt in the ark was Jesus Christ, which was a type of the human nature of Christ. This Christ delivers into the hands of his enemies, who take it and carry it captive, and carry it away in triumph, counting that they had taken captive the God of Israel. And they carry it into the temple of Dagon, presenting of it to Dagon as their god, as Dagon's captive, accounting that their god Dagon had proved the strongest, and had overcome the God of Israel. And so they carry the ark, and deliver it up to Dagon in Dagon's temple. This typified Christ being delivered up into the hands of his cruel enemies, and into the hands of the devil as captive, as Christ says to his enemies, when he was arraigned by them. Luke 22:53, "This is your hour, and the power of darkness."

It was to Dagon's cost that he had such a captive; for he fell, and lost his head and hands before it. The bringing this captive unto Dagon's temple proved the ruin of Dagon: Christ that dwelt in the ark ruined him in his own temple. So when Satan had as it were taken Christ captive, and procured his death, and held him prisoner, it was to his cost: it was the very thing that proved Satan's ruin, and the ruin of his kingdom. Thus Christ in dying bruised Satan's head. Dagon and the Philistines were sick of their captive, and were forced to send it back; as the whale was sick of Jonah, the type of Christ, that he had swallowed down, and was forced to vomit him up again on the dry land the third day, as Christ rose from the dead the third day.

-- 502 --

But this is what I aim at by these things, viz. that Christ, by thus representing of old from time to time his own death and suffering, showed how well pleased he was in the consideration thereof.

Fifth. Christ came into the world for this end. It was the principal end of Christ's assuming the human nature. It was chiefly for that that there was need of Christ's becoming incarnate, viz. that he might be capable of suffering and dying. As he was God, he could not die; and therefore he became man, and came into the world for this very end. Matthew 20:28, "Even as the Son of man came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many."

Sixth. His coming into the world in these circumstances that he did, shows that it was a thing chosen and designed by him to suffer in the world. If he had another design, he would not have come in such mean, low circumstances. He might have appeared in great earthly splendor and glory, as the Jews expected he would. Then he would have been admired, and followed, and adhered to, and not despised and presented as he was. But he came in mean circumstances, was born of a mother of low degree, as she herself takes notice in her song. Luke 1:48, "For he hath regarded the low state of his handmaid"; and [the] Luke 1:52, "He hath put down the mighty from their seats, and exalted them of low degree." And his reputed father was a person of low degree. He was a carpenter, so that Christ was called in contempt, the carpenter's son. Matthew 13:55–57, "Is not this the carpenter's son? And they were offended at him." He was born in obscurity, and he spent [a] great part of his life in obscurity. And when he appeared in the work of his public ministry, he appeared as one in low circumstances. "The foxes have holes, and the birds of the air have nests; but the Son of man hath not where to lay his head."

By this he was exposed to contempt, and became liable to the reproval and malice of his enemies. He appeared "as a tender plant, and as a root out of a dry ground, without form or comeliness"; i.e. without outward pomp and glory. This plainly showed that it was his choice and design to suffer, and therefore would be in such circumstances as to be exposed to it.

And it also appears by his choosing that age to be born in, when it was so exceeding corrupt a time amongst the Jews, and that nation were so full of prejudices against such an appearance as he made in the world, and such doctrines as he taught. The scribes and Pharisees that then prevailed were of principles and dispositions to the highest degree opposite to Christ and his religion. If it had not been Christ's choice to suffer, would doubtless have chosen some other time, when the nation of the

-- 503 --

Jews were in better temper, and more disposed to receive him, and treat him well.

Having thus shown that Christ was voluntary in subjecting himself to his sufferings as God, I proceed,

II. To show that he was also voluntary in it as man, and this appears by the following things:7

First. He took great delight in the thoughts of his suffering for sinners, because he knew that that would be a thing greatly to God's glory. Jesus Christ loved the Father with an infinite love, and he above all things set his heart upon his glory. He came into this world to glorify the Father, and when he had done this, he spoke of it as having done the work which the Father gave him to do. John 17:4, "I have glorified thee on the earth: I have finished the work which thou gavest me to do." This was what Christ earnestly prayed for, when he was going to Jerusalem. Says he, "Father, glorify thy name" (John 12:28). Christ had respect to his sufferings in that request; for he was then speaking of his sufferings. He glorified him in his death. John 21:19, "This he said, signifying by what death he should glorify God."

It was a most divine work, a divine contrivance: it all over savored of divinity, and God's glory every way shone forth in it. Therefore was Christ offended by Peter's objecting against it. "Thou savorest not the things that be of God, but those that be of men."

Christ by his death in a transcendent manner glorified the authority of God, as it was to atone8 for the injury and offense done to God's authority by men's sins. Christ hereby gave his testimony that so sacred was the divine authority, that nothing less than his own blood would atone for the contempt of it. He who was wisdom itself manifested how sacred he looked upon the authority of God. If he had not known that nothing less would have been sufficient to repair {God's authority}, he never would have consented {to atone for the contempt of it}.

And then it glorified God's authority, as it was in obedience to the divine authority. Psalms 40:6–8, "Sacrifice and offering thou didst not desire; mine ears hast thou opened: burnt offering and sin offering hast thou not

-- 504 --

required. Then said I, Lo, I come: in the volume of the book it is written of me, I delight to do thy will, O my God: yea, thy Law is within my heart."

[It] glorified God's holiness and hatred of sin: his hatred of sin never so appeared [more glorious]. [It] glorified the justice of God. [It glorified the] grace of God. [The] unsearchable riches of grace opened to view the infinite fountain of God's love. [It glorified the] wisdom of God.

And [it glorified] the glorious excellency of God in general, as Christ therein showed his infinite esteem of God and love to him; in that when he had a mind that sinners should be freed, he had rather bear such great suffering, and be so exceedingly humbled, than that their salvation should be to his dishonor. Now, it is exceedingly to the glory of God that so honorable a person as Christ should so love him.

And Christ took great delight [in] the thoughts of his sufferings for sinners upon this account: he had his heart very much upon this way of glorifying [God]: he greatly rejoiced to think how God would be glorified in his sufferings; how this would be an occasion of showing forth his perfections to the views of men and angels. It rejoiced him to think how this would be an occasion of God's being praised by the angels; and of his having praise out of the mouth of babes and sucklings; and how he would be praised by all the host of heaven, the church of saints and angels forevermore. And therefore it was exceeding offensive to him that any should object against his suffering. It was an unpleasing thought to him, that God should miss of all this glory that he had so set his heart upon and settled upon for ages and generations.

Second. He delighted in the thoughts of it, as he hereby should give a testimony of his love to his elect. Love, especially great and strong love, desires and looks to show itself, to give some testimony of itself in the fruits of it. Christ had very great love to those that the Father had given him, and he desired to give a testimony of it, to give some manifestation or testimony that should be adequate.

A lover that has a very strong love to another can think of undergoing difficulties for another with pleasure, because thereby he testifies his love to its object.

Christ suffering so much for sinners, it fully showed how great his love was, as9 nothing else ever did. Other things show Christ's love to his people—his bringing of the people of Israel out of Egypt, and many other things he did for the church under the Old Testament, blessings of common providence—but not fully. [It] did not show how great his love was,

-- 505 --

but this did. Therefore Christ settled upon it, and that any should object anything against it [offended him].

Third. Christ knew that hereby he should save sinners. He knew that God would accept of his sufferings, instead of the eternal damnation of sinners; and that poor sinners hereby would escape that dreadful misery, and would be saved. He knew that by his sufferings, as an act of obedience, he shall purchase glory for sinners; and therefore he took great delight in the thoughts of [it], and his heart was much upon it. It was exceeding pleasant to him to think how the justice of God would be satisfied, and perishing souls would have safety and deliverance through his suffering. He thought of their being brought up from their depths of woe, and being advanced to glory by his pain and disgrace with great satisfaction. The thought of it was most sweet to him.

He did [not] at all begrutch1 his blood so that they might be redeemed and made happy. On the contrary, it rejoiced his heart to think that he should in that way deliver sinners from so great a misery, and bring them to such glory. And therefore it was exceeding offensive to Christ that any should so much as suggest of any such thing as that he should not suffer, or that it did not become him to suffer for sinners. Christ was not at all inclined to listen to any such thing: that suggestion was not pleasing to him: he desired not to hear any such talk as that which was against what he had any pleasure in. He knew that if he did not suffer, sinners could not be delivered from hell, and obtain that great glory which he would have them enjoy. And therefore when Peter said, "This shall not be to thee," he replied, "Get thee behind me, Satan: thou art an offense to me."2

Christ had no delight in thinking that his life should be spared, and sinners lost; that his blood should be spared, and he enjoy his ease, and poor, unworthy sinners suffer eternal misery. Peter meant to show his respect and love to Christ, and esteem of him in objecting against his suffering for sinners. He would signify to Christ that he had such an high esteem of him that he looked upon such disgrace as unworthy of him. A man that is going to be put to death, and is loth and unwilling, he would look upon as a kindness in any to oppose it. But Christ took [it] as no kindness in Peter to object against his suffering, pain, shame, and death for the salvation of poor sinners.3

-- 506 --

APPLICATION.

I. Hence we may learn the greatness of Christ's love to sinners. If Christ was willing to undergo such sufferings, or was voluntary in it, it may be inquired what made him so free in it. I answer, it was his great love to poor sinful men.

Suffering is the greatest trial and manifestation of love. And the sufferings of Christ were a great manifestation of love in proportion to the greatness of his sufferings, and the dignity of the person suffering. So much the greater the sufferings were, so much the stronger love was there needed in order to his being willing to undergo them. And that he should be willing to undergo so great sufferings was the more wonderful, when we consider the dignity of his person; that he that was "in the form of God, and thought it not robbery to be equal with God," should take on him the form of a servant, and humble himself. This is spoken of as a marvelous manifestation of Christ. 1 John 3:16, "Herein perceive we the love of God, because he laid down his life for us." But that greatness of Christ's love, as manifested in his voluntariness in his suffering, will appear more abundantly, if we consider what trials Christ had of his willingness to undergo them, even at the time when he endured them.

First. Christ had a clear view of his last sufferings on the cross just before he underwent them, when he was in his agony in the Garden. Christ did not deliver up himself to the sufferings of the cross not knowing what they were. If he had so done, it could not have been said in all respects to have been his own voluntary act, that he became subject to those sufferings. Because if he had not known beforehand what those sufferings were, there could have been no explicit act of the will about them. He would not have had the trials, whether he was freely willing to deliver up himself to undergo them or no. But Christ knew beforehand what the sufferings were that he was to undergo. They were not kept hid from him, that he should be at all disappointed by finding them more dreadful than he before imagined. As he was God, he knew from all eternity perfectly what they were. But it was seen meet that Christ's subjecting himself to his sufferings should be a voluntary thing in him, both as God and also as man. And therefore it was fit that he should, as he was man, know beforehand what his sufferings were that he was to undergo.

Christ's sufferings were very extreme and extraordinary. Besides the sufferings of his body, there were ineffable and inconceivable sufferings that he underwent in his soul. Now, Christ as man would not have known beforehand what those were, had there not a view been given him of them.

-- 507 --

If any of us were to [be] crucified, or burnt to death, or put to any other very terrible death, we should not know beforehand what we were to suffer. We should have terrible ideas of it without doubt, but yet we should be very ignorant of the pains and torments that we were to undergo; and especially if, besides bodily torture, we were to suffer dreadful things in our souls. This could not be known beforehand, unless a prospect or view of our sufferings were immediately given us beforehand.

And this seems to have been what was given to Christ in the time of his agony in the Garden, in the night before he was actually crucified. There and then it pleased God to give Christ a sight of what he was to undergo, and to show him the cup that he was to drink: he had a clear view of those dreadful things that he was to undergo: the dregs of the cup were not concealed. Therefore Christ then speaks of the cup, as what he had then before him in his immediate view. Matthew 26:39, "O my Father, if it be possible, let this cup pass from me: nevertheless not as I will"; Matthew 26:42, "O my Father, if this cup may not pass away from me, thy will be done."

And this seems to be the very thing that occasioned his agony in the Garden. We find that Christ had two turns of extreme sufferings.

1. In the Garden the night before his suffering on the cross. And the occasion of it seems to be an extraordinary view that God gave him of the dreadful cup that he was to drink, that the human nature of Christ might know beforehand what it must suffer, that so it might be the free act of the human nature to submit to those sufferings. Christ had then given [to] him such a lively and full view of the dreadful sufferings that he was to undergo the next day, that [it] filled his mind with agony; for his sufferings were so great, that the very view and prospect was exceeding dreadful to the human nature. We read in the Matthew 26:37, that he "began to be sorrowful and very heavy." In Mark 14:33, it is said he "began to be sore amazed." The sight of his approaching terrible sufferings were amazing to him, and he said to his disciples, "My soul is exceeding sorrowful, even unto death" (Matthew 26:38).

Christ was to suffer the wrath of God, and the wrath of God is so dreadful that the nature of man can't bear the sight of it. The foresight of it was so terrible to Christ that he put his soul into a dreadful agony, so as to cause him to sweat blood. Luke 22:44, "And being in agony he prayed more earnestly: and his sweat was as it were great drops of blood falling down to the ground."

Now this was a great trial of Christ's freeness and voluntariness in undergoing those sufferings for sinners: when he thus had it given him beforehand clearly to see what they were, and had set before him in a full

-- 508 --

view what the cup was that he was [to] drink, while he yet had it in his choice whether he was subject himself to them or no.

This was a greater trial, because it was so near the time of his last sufferings, being but the night before; a greater trial than if this view of his sufferings had been given them, while his sufferings were at a great distance. For sufferings that are looked at as at a distance are not wont so to affect human nature, as those that are just at hand. The near approach often makes nature to shrink, when the consideration of their distance is oftentimes support and relief. Men sometimes think beforehand that they could be willing to undergo such and such things, while they look on them as at a distance; and yet when they come nigh are wont to shrink back, they find a difference. But the cup was set in Christ's view just before he was to drink, when he was in the Garden, while he had opportunity, if he had pleased, to have got out of the way, so that his enemies should not have found him.

But yet he delivered up himself. His love to sinners did not fail. When he saw how dreadful his sufferings were, he voluntarily undertook them. He stayed there till Judas came with his band of men, and he asked them whom they sought. And when they said Jesus of Nazareth, he replied, "I am he," and delivered up himself into their hands (John 18 at the beginning).

2. Another trial of Christ's willingness to suffer for the sins of men, was that he had a great and extraordinary view of the sin of mankind at the very time that he suffered for it. There are these two things that make Christ's sufferings wonderful, viz. that he should voluntarily endure so great sufferings, and that he should do it to make atonement for so great wickedness, or for those that were so vile.

But in order to its being properly said that Christ of his own free act and choice endured so great sufferings to atone for those that were so wicked and hateful, two things were needful; viz. first, that he should be sensible beforehand how great those sufferings were to be that he should have a clear, preceding view of them, and this was given him in his agony; and, second, that he should be sensible when he offered himself to suffer, or when he suffered, how great and hateful that sin and wickedness of men was that he suffered to atone, or how unworthy they were that he died for.

Christ as God perfectly knew the sins of men all eternity. But it has been meet that he also as man should have an extraordinary view of the wickedness of men given him at the time when he died for the wickedness of men, and this he had given him in the cruelty of those that put him to death. That so Christ might, at the time when he suffered to make atonement for the sins of men, have the sins of men set before him in its own color, it pleased God so to order it that these suffering should be caused by the

-- 509 --

sins of men. It was the wickedness or corruption that is in men that Christ died to make atonement for, and Christ had enough to impress upon him a sense of the vileness, and hatefulness, and unreasonableness of men's corruption and wickedness in the time of his suffering; for it was the wickedness of men that contrived and effected his sufferings. It was the wickedness of men that agreed with Judas: it was the wickedness of men that betrayed him, and apprehended him, and bound him, and led him away like a malefactor; and [it] was by men's wickedness that he was arraigned, and falsely accused, and unjustly judged; it was by men's wickedness that he was reproached, mocked, buffeted, and spit upon; it was by the sins of men that Barabbas was preferred before him. And it was the sins of men that scourged him, and led him away to crucify him; it was {the sins of men} that laid the cross on him to bear: it was {the sins of men} that nailed him to the cross, and put him to so cruel and ignominious a death. This tended to give Christ as man a lively sense of the greatness and hatefulness of the sins and depravity of men, and what wickedness there was in the heart of men, at the same time that he died to make atonement for men's wickedness. It tended to make him then to see clearly what that sin was that he suffered for; and that upon these accounts:

(1) Hereby he had in the time of his suffering the corruption and wickedness of men set before him as it is, without disguise, in the cruelty that was exercised towards Christ. Men's wickedness appeared to Christ in its true unreasonableness, injuriousness, falseness, baseness, and ingratitude, in its maliciousness and contempt, and in its devilish nature.

Here the sins of men appeared in its true nature, which is the utmost hatred and contempt of God. It now appeared in this hatred and contempt of the Son of God, who was God as well as man.

Here the sins of men appeared in its ultimate aim and endeavor, which is to kill God. Here it appeared what sin would be at, viz. to exercise cruelty upon God himself: here it appeared what the sin of man seeks, viz. no less than the life of God; in that when God became man, so that his life came within its reach, it did not spare it, but actually took away his life: it shed the blood of God.

(2) This wickedness that wrought these sufferings of Christ, was but a sample of that corruption and depravity that is naturally in all mankind. The corruption that was in the heart of Judas that betrayed Christ; and the Jews that apprehended him and cried, "Crucify him, crucify [him]," and preferred Barabbas before him; and of Pilate that condemned him; and the soldiers that mocked, and buffeted, and spit upon him; and in all that had a hand in crucifying of him, is the very same corruption that is

-- 510 --

naturally in the hearts of all mankind. 'Tis the same wickedness. It all has just the same tendency, and under like circumstances, and without restraint, would act just so: "As in water face answers to face, so the heart of man to man." So that Christ therein had a view of the sin of his elect that he then died for. Such corruption, such sin and wickedness as this, that put Christ to such ignominy and torment, was that which suffered this ignominy and torment to make atonements for. And not only so, but

(3) Christ suffered to make atonement for that individual, actual wickedness that wrought his suffering, in that he died for some of his crucifiers. That very wickedness that reproached, and buffeted, and spit upon him and crucified him, he then suffered to make propitiation for; with respect to some of them that were guilty of it, whom Christ prayed for, while they were in the act of crucifying of him, that they might be forgiven, and who were afterwards, in answer to his prayer, converted and forgiven; as we have account in the Acts 2. 'Tis evident that those that the apostle Peter especially spoke to in his sermon in the chapter, were men that had had a hand in crucifying Christ. It appears by the Acts 2:22–23, "Ye men of Israel, hear these words; Jesus of Nazareth, a man approved of God among you by miracles and wonders and signs, which God did by him in the midst of you, as ye yourselves also know: him, being delivered by the determinate counsel and foreknowledge of God, ye have taken, and by wicked hands have crucified and slain"; [the] Acts 2:26, "Therefore let all the house of Israel know assuredly, that God hath made that same Jesus, whom ye have crucified, both Lord and Christ." And these very persons, some of them, were pricked in their hearts by these regrets, and it proved a means of their conversion; as is evident by the words that next follow: "Now when they heard this, they were pricked in their heart." What was it they were pricked in their heart about? Why, about that sin that Peter had been telling them of, viz. crucifying Christ. And being pricked in their heart, they cry out, "Men and brethren, what shall we do?" And then Peter directs 'em to "Repent, and be baptized everyone of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the remission of sins." And they did so; as we have account in the Acts 2:41, "Then they that gladly received his word were baptized." And so they had the remission of sins as Peter promised them, and so Christ's prayer was answered when he cried,"Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do."

This shows how freely Christ suffered for the sins of men, and how wonderful his love to sinners was, in that he would die for such sin and wickedness, such corruption that was of so hateful a nature, and did at that time so appear in its hateful exercises and effects; that he was willing to undergo

-- 511 --

great sufferings for that sin and wickedness, which at the same time was exercising all its venom against him, and at the same time that he felt such terrible effects of it; that he died for those who were not only so wicked, but whose wickedness consisted in being enemies to him; and that he was willing to die for his enemies at the same time that he was subject to the utmost exertions and efforts of their enmity, in the greatest possible contempt and cruelty towards him, and at the same time that he was feeling the utmost effects of it in his own greatest ignominy, torment, and death; that he should be in such an high act of love to them at the same time that they were in so high an act of mortal hatred and cruelty to him; and that he should express his love at that time by bearing those very cruelties, that so he might take away the guilt of their cruelty: that he was willing to atone for their being his enemies in those very sufferings, by that very ignominy, torment, and death that was the fruit of it. How astonishingly wonderful was this! Where is anything that can parallel this love? When Christ thus had the exceeding hateful nature of the sin of men set before him, and when he began to feel the effects of its malice, and had the rest of his dismal sufferings in his view; he did not say within himself, "Why should I undergo so much to save such vile miscreants that do so hate me, and are so cruel to their Savior? Why should I undergo so much for them that are such mortal enemies to me? Why should I expose my face to be spit on, my body to be torn and tormented for them that are so vile or base, as thus to treat me? Let them perish. They deserve no better. I stand in no need of them. If they are enemies to their own good, and will treat those that show them such kindness at this rate, let 'em e'en do as well as they can for themselves." Jesus Christ did not say thus within himself, and so call for twelve legions of angels to deliver him out of their hands, or repel them by his own immediate power, as he might have done, and so rescued himself. But he patiently endured. His love stood this trial. He bore it all. He waded through the sea of blood and wrath. His love failed not when he began to enter it, nor when he was in the midst of it. He willingly and freely gave himself up a victim for divine wrath for those his enemies.

Second. Another trial that [Christ] in the time of his suffering had of his voluntariness in sufferings, was the great sins and infirmities of those that were already actually entitled to the benefits of his sufferings that were then set before him, viz. the great failings of his own disciples at that time. The remaining corruption of Christ's true disciples scarcely ever appeared as they did at that time. They never treated their Lord and master so unworthily as they did when he was going to die for them. When he was in his agony in the Garden, they acted as if they cared not for their master. He told

-- 512 --

them what a time of distress it was with him, how his soul was exceeding sorrowful even unto death; and therefore he desired that they would keep awake that he might be comforted with their company in the time of his sorrow and distress. But they seemed careless about it, and soon fell asleep.

And then Christ waked them again, and gently reproved them; but yet they fell asleep again. And while he was wrestling with dismal agonies, there they lay carelessly sleeping, as if they were wholly unconcerned about it. They would not so much as deny themselves to keep awake for him while he was sweating blood for them. And so they slept on, till Judas and others with him came and apprehended him, and then in a cowardly and base manner, they all forsook him, and fled. They would not stand by him in his affliction; but everyone was for taking care of himself, as they cared not what became of their Lord and master.

And Peter, though he seemed mighty resolute for a while that he would not forsake him nor deny him, though he should die with him; yet when it came to [it], though he did go to the high priest's house after him, yet it was in a private manner: he durst [not] let anybody know that he was one of Christ's disciples. And when he came there, he denied him: he was ashamed and afraid to own him. Now he sees him stand as a malefactor, and proceeded so far as [to] abjure him, or deny him with oaths and curses.

These were those that Christ was going to die for, and that already were entitled to the benefits of his death. And this was a specimen of the infirmities of believers in general, a sample of the remaining corruption that is [in] the hearts of the godly, and the unworthy carriages that they often fall into, after they are made partakers of the redemption that Christ has purchased.

But Christ, though he saw things thus, yet all this did not make him unwilling to die for his elect. Whom he loved, he loved to the end. Though their love to him was wavering, and subject to decays and shameful interruptions, yet it was not so with his love to them. He was willing to go on and die for his disciples notwithstanding. But I proceed,

II. To an Inference from the doctrine. If it be so that Christ was voluntary in subjecting himself to those sufferings that he underwent, then hence we may learn that Christ's death may be considered as an act of obedience, by which he purchased heaven as well as a satisfaction for sin. Christ's death, as it was his passion, or as he was passive in it, it was a propitiation for sin, and delivers us from punishment. But as he was active in it, as it was what he was voluntary in, so it was an act of obedience, and was a part of his positive righteousness by which he purchased heaven. Christ's death has both the nature of a propitiation and also a righteousness.

-- 513 --

As he was passive in it, it has the nature of a propitiation; and as he was active in it, it has the nature of righteousness.

So that the death of Christ is both satisfactory and meritorious. As it was his passion, it was satisfactory: he underwent our punishment, and set us at liberty from it. As such, it was a paying our debt. But as he was voluntary and active in it, it was meritorious. For so it did more than merely pay our debt for it made a positive purchase for us. Hence Christ's blood is spoken of in Scripture not only as a satisfactory price, but as a purchasing price. It is plain that besides paying a debt, there is a positive purchase in it; by Acts 20:28, "Feed the church of God, which he hath purchased with his own blood." If the death of Christ did only deliver us from hell, there would not be but one half of redemption to be attributed to the blood of Christ; for there is but one half of redemption that consists in being redeemed from hell. For besides that, there is the obtaining heaven, which is as great a thing as that. But we often in Scripture find the whole of redemption attributed to the blood of Christ. 1 Peter 1:18–19, "Forasmuch as ye know that ye were not redeemed with corruptible things, as silver and gold, from your vain conversation, received by tradition from your fathers; but with the precious blood of the Son of God."

There are two parts of redemption. One is being redeemed from sin; therein we are delivered from evil, which is by Christ's satisfaction. And the other part is a being redeemed to God, and brought to happiness; and that is by Christ's righteousness. But not only a being redeemed from sin, but a being redeemed to God, is attributed in Scripture to the blood of Christ. Revelation 5:9, "For thou wast slain, and hast redeemed us to God by thy blood out of every kindred, and tongue, and people, and nation."

And the bringing of us to heaven is particularly attributed to the blood of Christ. Hebrews 9:12, "Neither by the blood of goats and calves, but by his own blood he hath entered once into the holy place,4 having obtained eternal redemption for us." The holy place there intended is heaven, and Christ entering there spoken of is his entering as our priest and representative, and as making way for us to enter; and therefore 'tis added, "having obtained eternal redemption for us."

But Christ's blood purchases heaven no otherwise than as his voluntary offering up his blood, was an act of obedience. For Christ tells us in the text that he had received this commandment of the Father, that he should lay down his life. "Therefore doth my Father love me, because I lay down my life, that I might take it again." As an act of obedience, it is part of the righteousness

-- 514 --

of Christ, and the principal part of it. Christ yielding himself to suffer death in Scripture is mentioned as the principal part of Christ's obedience. Philippians 2:7–8, "He took on him the form of a servant, and humbled himself, and became obedient unto death, even the death of the cross." Hebrews 5:8, "Though he were a Son, yet learned he obedience by the things which he suffered." And this is that great act of obedience spoken of in [the] Psalms 40:8, where Christ says, "Mine ear has thou bored. I delight to do thy will, O God, and thy Law is within my heart."

And this act of obedience is part of the righteousness by which heaven is purchased for us.

To this it may be objected that that righteousness of Christ that we are justified by, is his obedience to that Law that we broke; but that commandment of laying down his life was not part of the Law that we broke. I answer that it must be the same law as to the moral precepts; but there is no need that it be the same as to the positive precepts. That great positive precept by which we fell, viz. not to eat of the tree of knowledge of good and evil, was what Christ never was under. And he was under other positive precepts that Adam never was under, such as the precepts of the ceremonial law, which Christ was subject to, and obeyed for us. He was made under the ceremonial law for our sakes. Galatians 4:4–5, "He sent his Son, made of a woman, made under the Law, to redeem them."

And as that act of disobedience by which we fell, was disobedience of a positive law that Christ never was under, viz. the not eating {of the tree of knowledge of good and evil}; so that act of obedience by which principally we are redeemed, is an act of obedience to a positive precept, viz. laying down his life.

All positive precepts are virtually contained in the moral law. For that is a general precept of the moral law implied in the first commandment, that we should obey God in whatsoever positive precepts he shall see cause to give us. There are many things that are included in the law that was given to our first parents only conditionally.

We are saved by Christ's death as much as an act of obedience as a propitiation; for as it was not the only act of obedience that merited, so neither was it the only suffering that was propitiatory.

All Christ's sufferings from his first incarnation were of a propitiatory nature, as every act of obedience from his first incarnation was meritorious. Indeed his last sufferings were his principal sufferings, and so 'tis by them principally that we have propitiation. So it was by his last act of obedience, viz. in yielding himself to death, that was as much his principal act of obedience, and so that by which principally he merited heaven.
