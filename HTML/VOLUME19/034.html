THE TERMS OF PRAYER

The word begrutch, a dialectic variant of begrudge, occurs more than twenty times in this sermon, the first instance of it in the Doctrine, "God never begrutches his people anything they desire, or are capable of, as being too good for 'em." Although other peculiarly eighteenth-century word forms occur from time to time elsewhere, most, like the single occurrence of begrutch in Five Discourses, might slip by unremarked were they not by convention annotated. Furthermore, Edwards had the other, increasingly standard, form available to him, as grudging on the last page here suggests, but he seems to prefer what is more likely the regional, if hoary, form of the verb.1 Whatever his reasons, or lack of them, the repeated word not only conjures up quills and inkpots but stubbornly forces the Doctrine.

Edwards reads his text antiphonally, first one psalm then the other, the blessing of the twenty-first answering the prayer of the twentieth, verse for verse, an implied metaphor for the whole. He also reads his text typically, Christ and his church answering to David and his people, and so authorizes an understanding of its words "either way." Thus he limits his first proposition of the doctrine to man and God and the Old Testament, a verse from Job, another from Psalms. "God does not begrutch his people anything they need or desire," he argues, even as they, out of envy or contempt or resentment, "begrutch others anything as being too good for 'em." But with the second proposition, he fixes upon Christ's petition for eternal life for himself and the faithful as the life asked for (and given) in

-- 769 --

the text. Everything—providences, decrees, dispensations, even creation itself—is "subordinate" to God's design in the "great affair" of redemption through Christ, and to make his grace "more conspicuous" God chose "inferior" mankind, not the fallen angels, to glorify the greatness, riches, and freeness of it. That the Father "begrutches nothing as too good for his own Son" means he denies nothing to those Christ represents as himself, "even to a communion of names." That God gave him in death to redeem them makes their happiness even more wonderful "to answer the wonderfulness" of his act. Such happiness, and the pursuit of it, is the subject of the second half of the sermon.

Edwards insists that only "wrong notions" of God keep some people from happiness. Their unworthiness and want of "good estate" misconstrue the free and infinite grace of God, he explains, and their proud spirit mistakes the "eternal dying love" of Christ. Nothing they do, or fail to do, can alter the design of redemption. Yet when God stirs them up and quickens their "spiritual appetite" for the "sweetness of divine objects," their desire for happiness grows, and God readily satisfies it. They have only to open their mouths to be heard, only to pray to be answered. To the objection that prayers often go unmet, Edwards answers that God withholds things "not best" asked for and delays others to the "best time." Some lack sincerity, their prayers raised not in "the voice of the heart, but only of the mouth" others lack humility, acting less like "beggars that supplicate" than "creditors that demand their dues." At the end, he exhorts the ungodly to repent and the godly to labor, not to "begrutch the pains and self-denial" required by God. Such are the ancient terms of the covenant, and of prayer.

* * *

Often signs of omission in sermon manuscripts, the horizontal lines in many of these twenty-four duodecimo leaves appear rather to be signs of uncommon haste. As usual, passages are deleted (LL. 1r., 1v., and 3r.), inserted (LL. 3v. and 4r.), or transposed (L. 7v.), and any number of strikeouts and carats are spread over the pages. But there are five partially blank pages in the manuscript (LL. 5r., 5v., 22r., 22v., and 23v.) and an overall airiness to it. Only the date on the first leaf, May 1738, hints at a possible cause. On May 2 and May 27, Edwards wrote to Benjamin Colman about, among other things, the forthcoming corrected American edition of Faithful Narrative and the reception in Maine of an earlier edition of it. But in both he is beset by worry and grief: Colman's affliction, his uncle

-- 770 --

Williams' failing health, the disabling illness of other Connecticut Valley ministers, his own "poor state," a seven-year-old child's death "by a cart 's running over him," "the dying time amongst us," the "oppressive designs of the Church of England against us."2 Who would begrutch him those many lines?

THE TERMS OF PRAYER
He asked life of thee, and thou gavest it him, even length of days forever and ever. Psalms 21:4

This psalm seems to be a song of praise to God for answering the prayer made in the foregoing psalm. The foregoing, which is the Psalms 20, is a prayer to God for his blessing upon the king, as appears by the Psalms 20:6; where the king himself, and the church on his behalf, express their faith in God, as one that had heard and would answer the prayer that was put up: "Now know I that the Lord saveth his anointed."

And this Psalms 21 is a song of praise that he had heard that prayer for the king, and given him the desired blessing. In the Psalms 20:4, it is prayed that God would grant the king his desire: "Grant thee according to thine own heart, and fulfill all thy counsel." In this Psalms 21, God is praised that he had granted the king's desire: Psalms 21:2, "Thou hast given him his heart's desire, and hast not withholden the request of his lips."

In the Psalms 20:5, 'Tis prayed that God would fulfill all his petitions, and requests "the Lord fulfill all thy petitions." And in the Psalms 21, in the verse of the text, God is praised that he had fulfilled his petitions.

In the Psalms 20:5, hope is expressed that the king and the church should rejoice in God's salvation: "We will rejoice in thy salvation." In this Psalms 21:5, 'Tis declared that the king does rejoice in "thy salvation." And so other things might be observed, wherein this psalm, that is a psalm of praise, answers to the foregoing, which [is] a psalm of prayer.

Both the psalms are said to be psalms of David. And when he speaks of the king in them, we may understand him as having respect more immediately to himself, but ultimately to Jesus Christ, "the root and offspring of David"; which is very common in the psalms of David. David in them

-- 772 --

speaks more immediately of himself as the head of the congregation or church of Israel; but yet 'Tis plain that the Holy Ghost in David has a further meaning, and that David speaks in the person of Christ, and that 'Tis he that is principally intended. Multitudes of instances might be given, if here were room for it, and those instances be confirmed by the application of 'em in the New Testament.

The king that is spoken of in those psalms, is spoken of as the head of the church; as appears by the Psalms 20:9: "Save Lord: let the king hear us when we call." And they are represented as the prayer and song of praise, both of the church and her king as united, or of the church concerning her king. The church is represented as speaking. Psalms 20:1, "The Lord hear thee in the day of trouble"; [the] Psalms 20:5 "The Lord fulfill all thy petitions."

The words of the text are an acknowledgement of God's great goodness in hearing and answering the request of the king; in which we may observe,

1. What the request was: "He asked life of thee."

2. God's answer of this request: "Thou gavest it him."

3. Something is taken notice of, wherein the superlative goodness of God appears in answering this request, viz. the transcendent excellency of the gift that was asked and bestowed. And that appears in two things.

(1) Of what kind the blessing was. It was life that the king asked of God, the greatest blessing. What is looked upon more precious than life? And life is a word that is commonly used in Scripture for a comprehension of all good, or all that appertains to an happy existence.

(2) In what degree this blessing was bestowed. He asked life, and God gave it him in full, and to the utmost degree that he desired it in; yea, to the utmost degree that it was possible for him to desire it in. He did not only give him a very long life, but a life without end, a life infinite in its duration, "length of days forever and ever." It was impossible that his desires should stretch themselves beyond this; for there is no such thing as anything beyond that which is without bounds.

DOCTRINE.

God never begrutches3 his people anything they desire, or are capable of, as being too good for 'em.

Whether we understand the words of the text as applied to David as to Christ (as they are indeed applied to both, the one as a type of the

-- 773 --

other); yet either way, this doctrine may justly be drawn from the words.

For if we understand 'em as spoken of Christ, yet they must be understood of him as Mediator and the representative of his people. Christ asks eternal life of God, and God gives it him not merely as God; for so he is essentially and independently possessed of an eternal, unchangeable, and infinitely blessed life.

But Christ asked eternal life of God, and had it bestowed upon him as the surety and head of his church. As he was put to death as a public person, so he asked life, and had eternal life given him as a public person; not only for himself, but for all that should believe. He as the first fruits, and they in their own order and time, shall be partakers with him.

I. [I would] say something tending to explain the doctrine in speaking to this. I would, in the

II place, mention some things as evidences that it is so that God never (begrutches his people anything they desire).

III. Give the reason why it is so.

I. I would say something briefly for the clearer understanding of the doctrine. And to this end I would observe that when any are wont to begrutch others anything as being too good for 'em, it is upon one of these three accounts: first, either because they are unwilling that others' happiness should be in such proportion to their own; or second, because they are looked upon so little and mean that it is thought too good for 'em upon that account; or third, because they have offended, and are looked upon guilty, and resentment makes 'em esteem it too good for 'em.

Or to express it starker: when anyone begrutches another anything as too good for him, 'Tis either, first, from envy; or second, from contempt; or third, from resentment. When any [one] begrutches another anything as too good for him, because he is unwilling that his happiness should be in such proportion to his own, that is from a spirit of envy. And when he begrutches him anything as too good for him, because he looks on him as little and despicable, that is from contempt, and because he despises him. But when it is because he holds him as an offender, that is from resentment. But God doth not begrutch his people anything as too good for them on neither of these accounts.

First. Some do begrutch others good things as being too good for them, because they are unwilling that their happiness should be in such proportion to their own; either because if they were possessed of such a good, they would be above them in happiness, or would have some kind or some degree of happiness that they have not. And this is what is more commonly

-- 774 --

called envy. Or it is because that they are not willing that others should be equal or near to them in happiness. This though it is not so commonly called envy, yet is indeed from the very same spirit; viz. a spirit of opposition against others' comparative happiness, or being an enemy to another's happiness as compared with his own.

There is a disposition in men to singularity in honor and happiness. They naturally love to [be] alone or distinguished in it, and to be above all others. And the more there is of this distinction, the more is this disposition gratified. But the fewer there are above them, and the fewer there are equal to them, and the further others are from being equal to them; or the more they are below them, and the less they are partakers with them; the more are they distinguished in this respect. There is such a selfish, confined, and penurious spirit naturally in men, whence 'Tis common with them to begrutch others these and those good things they have or might have, as being too good for them.

But God does not begrutch his people anything they need or desire, as being too good for them on any such account. Indeed God is alone in his happiness and glory. It is impossible that any should be above him or equal to him. The creature is not capable of it. Nor is it fit that God should in any respect give his divine glory and honor to another; for he therein would deny himself, and would be the author of confusion. Nor is it possible that the happiness of any creature should be in any proportion to the happiness of God, because God's happiness is infinite, and the happiness of a creature cannot be any more than finite. And however great a finite is, yet it can bear no proportion to an infinite. Yet the creature is capable in some respect of being a partaker with God in happiness, and having a kind of conformity to him in this respect. He is capable of receiving a communication of that infinite good which God possesses. And God don't begrutch his people anything as too good for them, lest they should share too much with, or be in a too great conformity to, him in this respect; or lest he should not be so much distinguished from them, as if they were not so much in conformity to God in happiness.

Second. Some begrutch others good things, because they are looked upon too little and mean to be possessed of those things that are so excellent. And so it is from contempt of them, because they are looked upon as little and despicable on the account of their inferiority. And hence it is disagreeable to them to see such great good and so mean a subject united.

God don't begrutch his people anything that they desire and are capable of, as being {too good for them} on this account. For though they are indeed in themselves very mean and despicable, they are poor, feeble,

-- 775 --

shortsighted creatures. They dwell in houses of clay, and are crushed before the moth; they are but worms of the dust. But yet God don't despise 'em on this account, so as to esteem anything they need, or desire, or are capable of, too good for 'em. God despises none merely for their littleness, and the inferiority of their nature. Job 36:5, "God is great, and despiseth not any." It is indeed very wonderful, that we that are but worms are not had in contempt of God, and that he condescends to take any gracious notice of us. But yet so it is, that he is pleased to magnify man, and set his heart on him. Psalms 8:3, "When I behold thy heavens, the work of thy fingers, the moon and the stars, which thou hast ordained."

Third. Another thing on the account of which {God don't begrutch his people anything that they desire}, is that they are looked on as offenders, and resentment makes 'em think such things too good for them. They are looked upon as deserving no good but ill; and on that account they are unworthy, they should receive good. God might justly be unwilling ever to bestow any good at all on men on this account; for they, as they are in themselves, are exceeding guilty. They have provoked him to anger exceedingly: they have made themselves infinitely abominable in his sight: they have rebelled against him, and been enemies to him. And God's people or saints, while in this world, have still abundance of sin in their heart's principles remaining, that God's holy nature is infinitely contrary to, and are every day offending and sinning against God. And they deserve that God should infinitely resent it. Every sin deserves God's eternal wrath and indignation. But yet God is willing to bestow unspeakable blessedness on them, and begrutches 'em nothing {that they desire}.

I now proceed, in the

II. [Second] place, to speak of some things that are evidential of the truth of the doctrine, and then confirm it to be so, that God begrutches {his people nothing that they desire}.

First. We may argue from God's design in the whole of his contrivance and dispensation in the great affair of man's redemption, which is to manifest the infinite riches of his grace. God's intention of redeeming a number of the fallen children of men by Jesus Christ, is the chief [of] all God's designs that ever was made known in this world. 'Tis an affair to which all other of God's designs and works known to us, seem to be subordinate. All God's providences from the beginning of the world to the end of it, seem to be all as it were so many appendages and events subservient to this great work. God's design, even in the work of creation itself, so far as made known to us, seems to be subordinate to this great affair. And all the eternal counsels and decrees of God, so far as God has been pleased to inform

-- 776 --

us concerning them, seem all subservient to that grand purpose of God, the redemption of the elect, and are all in some sort, appurtenances of the eternal covenant of redemption.

But God's declared design in this grand affair is to magnify the infinite riches of his grace. Romans 9:23, "That he might make known the riches of his glory on the vessels of mercy." Ephesians 1:5–7, "Having predestinated us unto the adoption of children by Jesus Christ to himself, according to the good pleasure of his will, to the praise of the glory of his grace, wherein he hath made us accepted in the beloved. In whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace." And Ephesians 2, Ephesians 2:4–7, "But God, who is rich in mercy, for his great love wherewith he loved us, even when we were dead in sins, hath quickened us together with Christ, (by grace ye are saved); and hath raised us up together, and made us sit together in heavenly places in Christ Jesus: that in the ages to come he might show the exceeding riches of his grace in his kindness toward us through Christ Jesus."

The riches of God's mercy is not made known in other dispensations and works of God: his mighty power and wisdom; {his} goodness in creation; [the] goodness in God's dealings in [the] first covenant; {his} dispensation towards the angels. But God had a great design from all eternity of manifesting the infinite riches of his mercy and free grace, and showing his goodness, and done in a far greater degree than ever he had done in {all} creation, or any antecedent dispensation or work of his.

And the infinite wisdom of God in contriving this work appears very much, in that 'tis so contrived as on every account, to enhance and magnify the greatness, and riches, and freeness of divine grace; as is intimated in Eph. Ephesians 1:7–8, "In whom we have redemption through his blood, the forgiveness of sins, according to the riches of his grace; wherein he hath abounded toward us in all wisdom and prudence." And it being thus God's declared design in this work, to show that his grace was infinite and without bounds; so it is agreeable to this design, to show that he looks on no good that tends [not] to answer his people's desires, and is fitted to their capacities, and would contribute to be too great for his grace and mercy.

God's declared design is to communicate of his own fullness, and to bring men to a conformity to himself in happiness, in such a manner as to show that his goodness or willingness to communicate of his fullness, has no bounds. And men's littleness and meanness, this can be no hindrance to God's willingness to bestow any good that can contribute to {his design}; because this is one way in which God has intended to fulfill his

-- 777 --

design of magnifying the riches of his, viz. in bestowing exceeding great good in those that were but worms of the dust.

And this we may look upon as one reason why God chose the race of mankind to make the subjects of this glorious work rather than the fallen angels, that his grace might be the more conspicuous in being exercised towards a nature so inferior.

And however great the guilt of God's people has been, and how much soever they have offended and provoked God, and deserved his displeasure; that shall be no hindrance to God's bestowing any good that they desire, how excellent soever. For that also is one way in which God designed to magnify the exceeding riches of his grace, even in bestowing it on those that were so unworthy. Romans 5:20, "Where sin abounded, grace did much more abound."

Men's exceeding guilt is so far from being an hindrance, that God makes use of it as his opportunity the more gloriously to fulfill his design of magnifying the riches of his grace.

To this end, God was pleased so to order things in his providence, that man was permitted to fall and become a guilty wretch; and his elect are first enemies before they are the subjects of mercy, that mercy might be the more exalted. And for this end, God is sometimes pleased to suffer some of his elect to be some of the greatest of sinners. So Christ came to save the chief of sinners that infinite grace might be glorified. And if this is what God makes one means or occasion of fulfilling his design of magnifying his grace, then we may be assured that it will be no hindrance to his design.

God's design in the works of redemption is to show, and to give the most bright and demonstrative evidences to angels and men, that his grace is infinite, by showing that nothing is too great for it; that 'Tis not too great a thing for his grace to show mercy to those that were very mean and inferior; {that 'Tis} not too great {a thing for those that were} most sinful and vile; and that no degree of happiness that is fitted to the capacities and desires of such, is too great {for his grace and mercy}.

Second. It will appear that there [is] no happiness that God is unwilling to bestow on {his people} as too good, if it be considered that [he] has thought nothing too much as the means of procuring their happiness.

If we would judge what God is willing to do, or what he can find it in his heart to do, for his people, let us consider what he actually hath done. He hath given his only begotten Son to die for them. The consideration of this great and wonderful act and gift of God, is argument enough. That single argument is in this case equivalent to a thorough demonstration;

-- 778 --

in Romans 8:32, "He that spared not his own Son, but delivered him up for us all, how shall he not with him also freely give us all things?" God gave his Son, a person that was God, to die and be made a curse for us.

So that nothing has been too good and too great as the means of procuring our happiness. For what thing greater, or what better, could have been given than Christ? And in this also appears that nothing was too much for Christ to suffer for his people. For what could he have endured or undergone worse than death? what death worse than the death of the cross? {what} more ignominious? {what} more painful? But because death in the soul is worse than death in the body, Christ endured {to suffer for his people}.

Now, scarcely there can be no degree of happiness that God's people can desire or enjoy, that can be more wonderful than to answer the wonderfulness of this that God has done, as the means of procuring their happiness. When we read or hear of the exceeding height of glory the saints shall be advanced to in heaven, it is ready to appear incredible to us, that such creatures as we should ever be advanced to such a pitch of glory. But the consideration of this, that Christ has suffered, renders everything credible that any can suppose concerning it. Certainly, 'Tis not more wonderful that the saints should enjoy, that the infinitely honorable and glorious Son of God should suffer. As mean and unworthy as we are, it can't be more wonderful that we should enjoy happiness, than that the second person in the Trinity should suffer torment and death under the wrath of God.

And if we read over all those things that Christ suffered, they are as wonderful as any degree of happiness that ever any did or could desire or enjoy.

We by all our littleness and unworthiness are not sunk more below the highest degree of happiness, than Christ by his divine glory and dignity is exalted above such suffering.

Those things Christ has actually suffered for us, it is already done and finished. Surely, therefore, God that han't begrutched this, does begrutch nothing in the measure of happiness itself; for we may fairly argue from the means to the end, that there will be some correspondence between them.

Third. We may argue it from this, that Christ is their Mediator and head, and God begrutches nothing to him. If they were beheld as they are in themselves, anything would be esteemed too good for them; they would be looked upon as unworthy of any good at all. But God don't behold 'em so, but he beholds 'em as in Christ. And they don't look to God, or come

-- 779 --

to him, for blessedness in their own names, but in the name of Christ. Christ stands for 'em as representing them in his own worthiness and merits, appearing in that worthiness for them; so that they are in God's account, in some respect, as Christ. For Christ is pleased to place himself in their stead, to put himself to be for them, and as it were assume themselves to him to be as himself, even to a communion of names; so that the church is called Christ (1 Corinthians 12:12). So that God, in his acceptance of his Son, accepts them; in his being received, they are received; in his being delighted in, they are delighted in.

And the case being thus, we may be sure that [God] begrutches nothing as too good for his own Son.

Christ declares that the Father loves him always, and God declares that this is his beloved Son in whom he is well pleased; for that end, that his people may be encouraged that they shall be accepted in Christ's acceptance.

And besides, Christ is not only the legal head of the saints. As their head, he is their representative, and their head of government, but also their vital head, their head of influence and communication, and that both of holiness and happiness. He is as it were their head of enjoyment, so that they enjoy all things in him, and as communicating and partaking with him; as when many streams issue from [a] fountain, they all are united to the fountain, and partake of its fullness, every canal has its mouth inserted and immersed into that full fountain. And as the members of the body all partake of the life, and health, and welfare of the head by the nerves, those from every limb of the body are terminated in the head; so the saints, being in Christ as members of his body, or branches on a vine, all partake according to the measure of their capacity of his fullness. John 1:16, "And of his fullness have all we received, and grace for grace." So that as we may be sure God begrutches nothing to the head or fountain, so nothing is too much for the members according to their capacity {of his fullness}. For they are happy by partaking of Christ's glory and joy. John 17:13, "That my joy may be fulfilled in themselves."

Fourth. And lastly, we may argue from what is revealed of that happiness itself that the saints are or shall be exalted to. The privileges they are admitted [to] are of the highest kind conceivable. They are received into the nearest relation to Christ, and that which above all others betokens endearment, even that of being his spiritual bride. They are united to him in the closest union. They are represented as being the members of Christ's body, "of his flesh, and of his bone" (Ephesians 5:30); yea, as brought into an union that is more after that of being one flesh. 1 Corinthians 6:16–17,

-- 780 --

"For two, saith he, shall be one flesh. But he that is joined [unto the Lord is one spirit]." They are received to the relation of children to God the Father, or of his family, "and of the household of God" (Ephesians 2:19); and have right to all the privileges of the children of God.

They are "heirs of God, and joint-heirs with Christ" (Romans 8:17).

The good that is promised them is of the highest kind. 'Tis a mansion, an inheritance in heaven, the highest and the most glorious part of the creation, {the most glorious} palace {in heaven} that God hath built. And then the happiness that is promised them is the full enjoyment of God, without restraint, in the boldness and nearness of excess, in the cold draughts they take. Canticles 5:1, "Eat, O friend; yea, drink; yea, be drunken." If the greatest good that God gives them even in himself, what can God give more than himself? He gives himself with all his attributes, power {glory, dominion, and majesty}. And he gives himself in the highest possible enjoyment {to his people}, as much as they can desire, or are capable of. {And he gives himself to} fully satisfy {their happiness}. For giving himself, he gives all things. 1 Corinthians 3:21–22, "All things are yours; whether Paul, or Apollos, or Cephas, or the world, or life, or death, or things present, or things to come; all are yours."

[He gives us the] highest honor, "[and hath made us] kings and priests" (Revelation 1:6). [We shall] reign with Christ in the same kingdom. [We shall] sit down in his throne (Revelation 3:21), [and] judge the world with him (1 Corinthians 6, at the beginning).

The happiness they shall reign [in] is eternal, [the] length of days forever.

III. And lastly, to give the reason why it is so. And no other reason can be given of it than that God's goodness is without bounds. It can't be resolved into anything in us, {nor into} anything in any of our fellow creatures. We are to seek for the ground of this only in the heart of God.

'Tis as we have heard, because 'Tis not with God as 'Tis with men, who are naturally selfish and so pernicious in their benevolence or goodness. God being an infinite fountain of good is as it were infinitely communicative, or infinitely overflowing, and therefore the communication of how great abundance soever. "God is love" (1 John 4:16). His essence being love, he is as it were an infinite ocean of love without shores and bottom, yea, and without a surface. Noah's deluge overflowed the highest mountains; but the deluge of God's mercy is above the heavens. Psalms 108:4, "Thy goodness4 is great above the heavens." And wherever there is that

-- 781 --

[that] seems to be any obstacle, any meanness, [or] any unworthiness; all is overtopped, overwhelmed, and swallowed up in this infinite deluge.

Those that God is pleased to make the objects of his love, let them be who they will, or what they will—never so mean, never so great sinners—they are the objects of a love that is infinitely full and sufficient.

And therefore nothing that they need, nothing that they ask of God, nothing that their desires can extend themselves to, nothing that their capacity can contain, no good that can be enjoyed by them, is so great, so excellent that God begrutches it to them.5

APPLICATION.

Use I maybe [of] Instr.

First. Hence we may learn that none need to question their good estate, because it seems to them that being a child of God, and an heir of heaven, is too great a thing for them. God's people sometimes make this a ground of doubting of their good estate. When they think how great a thing it is to be indeed converted, and delivered from eternal death, and received as the objects of God's everlasting favor, and to be forever safe and forever happy; it seems to them it is too great a thing for them, and that it would be presumption in them to think any such thing of themselves, who are such little, despicable, and unworthy creatures.

It may be they think with themselves that they are weak, ignorant creatures, and otherwise persons of but little consequence in the world; and if they should have such a mercy bestowed upon them, could do but little to serve God or glorify him for it. They are persons of but small abilities, and are low in the world, and are but little taken notice of among men; and it seems incredible that they should be so taken notice of by God.

And especially it looks too good and too great for them, considering how sinful they are. They have been very great sinners, and have sinned away [a] great part of their lives, and are very sinful creatures still, [and] find a world of corruption in their hearts, and are often offending and fall exceeding short in everything. And it looks too much for them to imagine any such thing as that God would set his heart on them, and would make them the subjects of such a privilege. And sometimes, when God's people that have before entertained hope of their good estate, hear of the glories of heaven, and what privileges the saints will be admitted to there;

-- 782 --

how they will be with the angels, and will be made like unto them, and will shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father; and will dwell with Christ, and reign with him in his kingdom, and be made partakers of his glory; and shall see God, and have the full and perfect enjoyment of him; and shall sit and judge the world with Christ at the last day; and shall drink of the rivers of pleasures at God's right hand; and this to all eternity—it is ready to make 'em call their hope into question. It looks too great for them to expect. And they are ready to say with themselves, "Surely, in order to my having a title to such blessings as those, I had need to be better than ever yet I have been. Certainly, it needs to be something very great indeed that shall be what shall entitle persons to such glory; and I fear I have never had that which is sufficient. I have abundance of sin and corruption in my heart, and have but very little of that which looks like any goodness. It seems to me I must have a great deal more, and do more, in order to my having a portion in such great things."

But by what has been said under this doctrine, we may learn that such objections which persons make against their entertaining a hope of their good estate, if there be nothing else, have no sufficient foundation. Such objections in the saints against themselves arise from wrong notions they have of God, or from a want of a sufficient sense of the infiniteness and freeness of divine grace, and what a sufficiency there is in the heart of God for the bestowment of such things, without either greatness or goodness in them to recommend 'em thereto. It arises from the remains of their unbelief, and of a legal spirit, and an aptness to look to themselves; as though they were to expect some foundation for a title to the unspeakable blessings of another world in themselves, and because they han't learnt to look only to Christ, and the free grace of God in him, so much as they ought to do. They don't sufficiently consider, that when God does bestow those blessings on any, it is not from any regard to their greatness, let 'em be great or small. 'Tis not from regard to their worthiness to be taken notice of, whether they [be] considerable or despicable.

'Tis not out of any regard to their goodness, whether they are more sinful or less. The good of those blessings is never to be sought for in men, but in something infinitely above men; and that was long before man was, even the infinite fountain of grace that is in the heart of God, and in his eternal electing love, and Christ's eternal dying love; for he loved his people with a dying love from eternity.

'Tis true those things are too good and too great for them to deserve, but not too great for the grace of God to bestow on those that are so undeserving. Their meanness and unworthiness can be no impediment to

-- 783 --

him whose declared design it is to magnify his free grace, by doing great things for the mean and unworthy. A sufficient acquaintance with the gospel, where this design of God is revealed, would remove all such objections.

Therefore God's people that have otherwise good evidence of their having been [in] holiness in Jesus Christ, and those that have their dependance on the free grace of God in him, and that [have] chosen God, and Christ, and holiness as their best portion, need not exercise themselves with fears that they are not God's children and heirs of eternal glory, because those things look too good for 'em. But [they] may boldly rejoice in hope of those great, and unspeakable, and everlasting blessings; and need not doubt but that God will grant 'em all their requests, and fulfill and satisfy their desires, even to length of days forever and ever; and that they shall have those things that eye has not seen, nor ear heard, which God hath prepared for his people. And [they] may boldly bless and magnify God's name for it, that has been so infinitely gracious to them, as being self-moved, and out of the infinite fullness of the grace of his own heart.

Second. If it be as we have heard, then here is matter of great encouragement for God's people in their prayers to look to God for whatever blessings they need, and for as great happiness as they desire, and for all that is requisite in order to their complete and eternal felicity. For God won't begrutch them anything that they ask as being too good for them. God stands ready to fulfill all their petitions in anything that they ask that is for their good. 1 John 5:15, "We know that we have the petitions that we desired of him." God is ready to bestow the blessings on his people that they need or desire, that he bids them ask. He is not unwilling we should ask, because he is [not] backward to bestow; but he delights in bestowing, and therefore waits for our asking, and counsels us to it; not to move him to bestow the blessing, but that we may be prepared to receive it; and says to us, as Matthew 7:7, "Ask, and ye shall receive; seek, and ye shall find; knock, and it shall be granted unto you." God is as ready, and more ready, to bestow than we are to ask, and more ready to open than we are to knock. And Christ lets us know that God is more ready to bestow good things upon us that we need, than earthly parents are to bestow good gifts to their children. [The] Matthew 7:11, "If ye, being evil, know how to bestow good gifts unto your children, how much more shall your Father which is in heaven bestow good things to them that ask him?"

Men's desires are naturally very large. 'Tis no small matter will satisfy the desires of the soul of man: 'Tis not all the enjoyments of a finite world

-- 784 --

will do it. A man must have something of a larger extent than heaven or earth in order to it. And godly men's desires of happiness are no less large than others. Godliness regulates men's desires of happiness, and directs them to right objects; but it don't diminish and confine them, or reduce them to straiter limits.

God's people's desires of happiness are exceeding extensive; but as extensive as they are, there is great encouragement in this doctrine to go to God with them. They may expose as large desires as they will, God is ready to hear them. God stands ready to give 'em their hearts' desire. As 'Tis said [the] Psalms 21:2 of the context, "Thou hast given him his heart's desire; thou hast not withholden from him the request of his lips." Let 'em open their mouth in their desires and prayers as wide as they will, God stands ready to fill them; yea, he would have them open their mouths wide. Psalms 81:10, "I am the Lord thy God, which brought thee out of the land of Egypt: open thy mouth wide, and I will fill it."

When God's people think of such mercies as the being treated as the children of God, enjoying communion with God, and having fellowship with the Father and with his Son Jesus Christ; and having the presence and protection of God under all circumstances, and deliverance from all calamity, and defense against all enemies; and being at last received to enjoy God forever—these are great things indeed.

And when we think what poor, polluted, wretched creatures we are, it looks like a great boldness to go and ask such things of God.

But the doctrine shows that this is no greater boldness than6 God allows us of his free grace in Christ. We may go and ask those things and all things that we desire, however great and wonderful. Ephesians 3:12, "In whom we have boldness and access with confidence by the faith of him." Hebrews 10:19, "Having therefore, brethren, boldness to enter into the holiest by the blood of Jesus."

When God stirs up in his people the lively exercises of God, and gives a great sense of the excellency and glory of spiritual things, this stirs up large desires in them. The saints never have such large and earnest desires as in such a frame.

Then especially it is that they want to get near to God. Then they long after the full enjoyment of God. Then they want to know more of God, and to know as much of God, and have as much of him in their hearts, and enjoy as much of God as their natures are capable. In such frames, the saints are further from being contented with but little of God, or resting

-- 785 --

in low spiritual attainments than ever. Their lively sense of the excellency of divine things quickens and sharpens the spiritual appetite; and having a taste of little, makes 'em desire much. And that inclines [them] to grace, and [to] pour out their desires before God, and to ask of him those great blessings. And the doctrine affords great encouragement for 'em so to do. God's people may hence be encouraged, when they have the liveliest sense of the glory and sweetness of divine objects, and find the greatest desires and longings after them; then to go and spread all their desires before God in their full extent, not to be afraid, or straitened, or confined at all, but let their petitions be as large as their desires.

God is not the more backward to hear the prayer of his people, because the blessings they ask are very excellent, but the more forward. Such is God's goodness, that 'Tis with more delight that God bestows great blessings than small ones. The Holy Spirit is the greatest blessing that ever {God bestows}, but God is more ready to bestow this than any other blessing; as appears by Luke 11:11–13, "If a son shall ask bread of any of you that is a father, will he give him a stone? or if he ask a fish, will he for a fish give him a serpent? or if he shall ask an egg, will he offer him a scorpion? If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?"

Therefore let the godly take encouragement from hence in their prayers to come boldly to the throne of grace, and to come frequently. It may well be your delight to you to come to a God that is so ready at all times to hear and to grant you whatever you desire that tends to your happiness.

Let those that are in affliction and disconsolate circumstances on the account of difficulties, either of body or mind, be encouraged to go to God, and let their requests be made known to him.

Obj. If any are ready to make it against what has been said, that they have often prayed to God, and God don't seem to hear them, their prayers ben't answered. They have prayed for outward mercies they need, but God [does not bestow them]. They have prayed that God would lift up upon them the light of his countenance, but they remain still in darkness. They have often prayed that God give 'em the comfort of his Spirit, but they still remain in disconsolate circumstances, as far [as] ever from comfort. They have prayed often, but have received no answer.

Ans. As to outward enjoyments, 'Tis no argument against that which you have heard that they ben't bestowed. For it may be God sees that those particular things you have asked, are not best for you; and then your not having them bestowed, is no argument that God begrutches you anything

-- 786 --

as being too good for you. For God don't withhold it as good, nor as anything that would be good at all to you, but evil. And as to that spiritual light and comfort you have prayed for, I answer

1. That you don't know that God never will bestow it. 'Tis no argument that God is unwilling to bestow this mercy on you as too good for you, because he don't bestow it in your time. He may bestow it in his own time and it may be most for his glory, and also best for you. For ought you know, the dispensation you are now under, though very sorrowful for the present, may in the end be for your humbling, and be more abundantly fitted for comfort, and fitted for greater comfort; so that spiritual comfort may be both sweeter and more profitable. And though it may be you may be ready to say that it seems to you, God has no design ever to bestow on you the mercy you seek, because he has not yet done it; you have sought it so long, and have hoped that the mercy would come, but yet no signs {have been given}, no appearances. {You have been} so often disappointed; {have} failed of hopes {so often}; have been told before that you must wait {for God to bestow it}; have waited till discouraged {of his bestowing it}. This is no argument that God never will hear {your prayers}, because he has not heard you. {This is} no argument that God has no time, because his time is not come yet; and that you have often been disappointed. And {this is} no argument that it won't come, and come in the best time. [You] han't waited to the end. [You] can't know that it will not be answered, till you have waited to the end. God directs you to "hope to the end" (1 Peter 1:13). [It is a] common thing for God to defer long, {for} time to seem very long to God's people: so the promise to Abraham of a son {Isaac}; [so the] promise of deliverance out of Egypt {of the children of Israel}; [so the] promise of [the] covenant {of redemption}; [so the] promise of building David's house, and making the "righteous branch" to spring forth {in Christ}. Hence those many counsels in Scripture so earnestly pressed {upon us} to wait and hope (Psalms 27:14).

2. It may be though you have often prayed for spiritual comfort, you han't done it in sincerity. That duty which is not done in sincerity, is not done at all in the sight of God; for [God] looks not at words or sounds, but at the heart. Men oftentimes pray to God in their words, when there is no such thing as prayer in the heart. Hosea 7:14, "They have not cried to me with their heart." When Christ says, "Ask, and ye shall receive, that your joy may be full,"7 'Tis praying with the heart and in sincerity, he has respect

-- 787 --

to. He has not respect to a mere pretense of prayer without any real prayer. Everything that men have a mind to have, and go and seem to cry to God for in words, they don't pray for with any real prayer in the sight of God. 'Tis only a show and pretense of prayer, and that because their prayer is not the voice of the heart, but only of the mouth; and so is as it were the prayer only of the body, and not the soul.

'Tis no real prayer in the sight of God, because it is not made with a humble sense of their unworthiness of what is prayed for, and a submissive sense of its being something that is in God's free disposal. If a prayer ben't made with such a frame as this, 'Tis no real prayer. The mouth seems to pray, but the heart don't pray. The heart challenges and demands. There is [a] difference between a challenge and a prayer. It may be when you have seemed to pray, and though you have prayed often and very earnestly, yet it has been far from being done with a meek, humble sense of the holy sovereignty of God; and how you depended on God, and deserved not the mercy sought; and how that God, notwithstanding anything of you, even might justly deny the benefit. Christ so came like a humble, meek suppliant to the throne of grace with a quiet, calm, humble, waiting, hoping disposition. It may be, on the contrary, your petitions were put up with an inwardly unquiet, turbulent, discontented, unsubmissive sort of spirit. A prayer that is put up after this manner, is no real prayer. Such persons don't act the part of beggars that supplicate and pray, but of creditors that demand their dues.

Again, it may be when you have seemed to pray, your prayer has not8 been the voice of faith. All real and true prayer is the voice of faith.

He that don't pray in faith, don't look to God in his prayer; he looks somewhere else. He directs his words to God, but at the same time looks, it may be, to himself, or to the creature, or looks to nothing at all. He prays with that unbelief and discouragement that he has no dependance on God, his mercy, or any other of his perfections for the bestowment of the blessings he asks. And so the heart directs no prayer to God, whatever the mouth utters. There is no other way that the heart can look to God, but only looking by faith, by faith seeking the blessing of God, and by faith depending on God for the mercy sought.

He that prays, or rather seems to pray, without any faith or dependance in the providence or mercy of God [for] the bestowment of the things asked; he don't really look to God's power, or mercy, or any of his perfections, and so don't look to God. And God never promised that he would

-- 788 --

hear and answer those that don't look to him for what they need. 'Tis a common thing for persons to deceive themselves with mere appearances. They have desires, 'Tis true, upon some account or other, and they go and, with seeming earnestness, time after time express those desires. But it may be their hearts don't beg, but demand; and their hearts don't look to God for the blessing they ask, nor have a jot of dependance on any of God's perfections for it. And this they call prayer, and wonder they ben't heard. God may, and sometimes does, answer such requests made after such a manner; but he has nowhere promised that he will do [so]. God may hear such petitions, and oftentimes does, as he hears the young ravens when they cry, out of pity to them and regard to the voice of nature, which desires its own happiness; but not that their cry is any more real prayer to him in his sight than the cry of the ravens that know nothing of him.

3. Though God don't give you that degree of spiritual comfort in this world that you desire, yet he can make your trouble and darkness an occasion of abundant, everlasting joy and comfort in heaven. There are two ways that God brings light out of darkness to his people. One is by making their fears and spiritual troubles in the issue a means of a great deal of light and comfort to 'em in this world; and another is by making of them a means of a great increase of joy and felicity in heaven. And if God don't bring light [out] of darkness in the former of these ways, he may do it in the latter. The happiness of the saints in heaven will be according to the degree of holiness in this world. But persons' spiritual hope and comfort in this world is not always in proportion to their holiness.

Though eminent grace has a tendency to eminent comfort, yet it has been so sometimes that some eminent saints have met with a great deal of darkness, and God has been pleased much to hide his face from them. And God may, and often does, make the doubts and darkness of his saints an occasion of their increase in holiness; as he makes it a means of increasing their humility, which many divines style the nurse of graces.9 And as it is an occasion of their being the most humble Christians, so it is an occasion of their being more highly exalted in glory. For the Scriptures teach that "he that humbles himself shall be exalted" (Matthew 23:12). And that 'Tis the humblest Christian, and he that is most like a little child, "that is greatest in the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:4).

God sees the natural temper and the circumstances of some persons to be such, that he sees it best for them to meet with a great deal of spiritual

-- 789 --

trouble and difficulty. God knows how to bring light out of this darkness, and God commonly doth it in this world. But sometimes the day don't dawn till death, and then the day dawns, and the sun rises never to set more. When they get to heaven, they see how it was for their good to be so many years in the wilderness. And though in the time of it they did not know what Christ did unto them in hiding his face from them, yet now they know. And so those words of Christ to Peter are applicable in this case. John 13:7, "What I do [thou knowest not now; but thou shalt know hereafter]."

And if it does but prove so, that they have the more light, and comfort, and glory to all eternity by means, or on occasion, of their darkness and spiritual afflictions in this world; then all objections from hence against what has been sought are removed. For what is time to eternity? What is the spiritual trouble of the saints for a moment in comparison with that glorious light of the heavenly world to everlasting ages?

Third. If it be {as we have heard}, then hence we may learn what great cause the saints have to rejoice in an hope of the glory of God. What we have heard may convince [us] how exceeding great that glory will be. If God begrutches them nothing {they desire}, then we may conclude that they will have length of days {forever and ever}; that their desires shall be fully satisfied; that they shall be brought to pleasures exquisitely sweet indeed; {that they shall be brought} now far beyond all that eye has seen {and the ear heard}, far beyond all that ever was experienced on earth, {and} that it shall be divine pleasure; [that there shall be] honors and glory inconceivable; {that there shall be} riches; {that there shall be} excellent things, things precious beyond all conception.

Seeing that God's grace is so boundless, and the happiness of the saints in glory is designed on purpose for the glory of the boundlessness of that grace, surely there will be something very high and very excellent indeed. And the saints have no reason to doubt of this. For however great their defects are, there is nothing wanting in the heart of God; {there is} nothing wanting in the Mediator.

Therefore the hope of the saints may well be called, as it is [in] Titus 2:13, a "blessed hope." And they have reason to rejoice in this hope with joy unspeakable and full of glory.

[Use] II [may be] of Exh.

First. To natural men. Earnestly to seek that they may be some of God's people. This shows how happy a state they are [in]. Consider three things.

1. If you continue wicked, hereafter you shall have none of your desires gratified. Though the godly shall have all their desires, you [shall have] nothing.

-- 790 --

[You] will have as great desires, or earnest an appetite to happiness, all crossed. {You will have} whatever hopes and expectations are {in you}, all frustrated. Proverbs 10:28, "The expectation of the wicked shall perish." Job 11:20, "But the eyes of the wicked shall fail, and they shall not escape, and their hope shall be as the giving up of the ghost." When you come to die, you shall greatly desire {the kingdom of heaven}. At the day of judgment, when you shall see {God}, you will greatly desire {the kingdom of heaven}. [The] foolish virgins earnestly desired to be admitted {to the kingdom of heaven}. The goats at the left hand, they desire {the kingdom of heaven}. When God's servants shall eat, ye shall be hungry; you will desire to be a partaker with them. [But] it shall be with you as it was with the unbelieving lord in Samaria. 2 Kings 7:2, "You shall see it with your eyes, [but shall not eat thereof]."

2. As God won't mercy1 them that are godly, so neither will he pity the ungodly. Hereafter when you shall be roaring out with inexpressible dolors, and wailing under your extensive torment, God will have no compassions or relentings. Ezekiel 5:11, "Mine eye shall not spare, neither will I have any pity." As nothing that the godly desire will be thought too good for them, so nothing that you desire will be thought too bad for you. Your misery shall be as great and dreadful as your sins, nor will God abate the least mite.

As God won't withhold the eternal happiness of heaven from the saints, because it is so exceeding excellent, but will bestow nevertheless freely; so neither will he forbear to inflict the torments of hell on the ungodly, because they are so dreadful, so intolerable, so much beyond what they can bear.

3. What has been said under this doctrine may be matter of great encouragement to you to seek the privileges of God's people, as it shows that if you repent and come to Christ, [notwithstanding] your great unworthiness, God won't account the blessings of his children too good for you. However great a sinner you have been, and how vile soever you are, the way is open to your partaking of those great and unspeakable blessings, if your heart be but open, All the bleakness and difficulty is within yourself; there is none on Christ's part nor on God's part. 1 Samuel 12:20, "And Samuel said unto the people, Fear not: you have done all this wickedness: yet turn not aside from following the Lord, but serve the Lord with all your heart." Matthew 22:4, "Come, for all things are now ready." Proverbs 9.2

-- 791 --

Second. The exhortation may be to the godly, If it be that {you seek to be God's people}, then don't think that anything that lies in your power is too much to do for God. Shall God be so wonderfully gracious and open-hearted to you as to think nothing—no blessedness, no pleasure, however exquisite; no honor or glory, however high—too good for you, and nothing too much to be done [or] to be given? And shall Christ think nothing too much to be suffered for you to procure this blessedness, which is such as is infinitely beyond all that you can do throughout eternity to requite it? And will you think (nothing)—any portion, any self-denial, any labor, any suffering—too much for God?

How little is it that God requires of you, and how little is the utmost that you can do. When you have done all you can for God, what is it to what God hath done, and stands ready to do, for you? How infinitely does it fall short. And shall God, who is infinitely great, be so free towards you, a worm; and will you be backwards and straithanded towards him? Shall your creator be thus infinitely liberal to you, and will you, his creature, be apt to begrutch the pains and self-denial that is required of you towards him?

Shall God think nothing too good for you that are unworthy of anything, for any to enjoy through eternity? And will you be apt to think a little pain and suffering, a little inconvenience for a few days, too much for him, who is infinitely glorious and worthy of infinitely more than you can render to him? You deserve no good at all of God, but deserve infinite evil. And shall God be so liberal in bestowing that which is so free? And will you notwithstanding be grudging and straithanded in owning or paying your debts to God, especially when you are not required to do this without a promise of being rewarded for it with eternal and unspeakable rewards? But you never can reward God for anything he has done for you.

Surely, those things may well humble Christians to the dust, and cover 'em with shame for their backwardness to their duty; their lothness to take pains, and be at a little difficulty or expense for God, and so apt to excuse themselves from anything that is a little cross to a carnal inclination; in their being so ready to think the things that are required are too much. And that after all the calls and counsels that are given them, they do so little for God, and are so backward to do those things that are to his glory, and so forward to sin against him.
