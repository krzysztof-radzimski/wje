PEACEABLE AND FAITHFUL AMID DIVISION AND STRIFE

Two months have passed and little has changed in Northampton. "Our mouths," Edwards reports in May 1737, are "filled with backbitings" and contention, the "old iniquity of this town." After thirty years of such divisiveness, he gives up on some of them—"I have now no hope, but they will quarrel still"—especially those responsible for the fractious debate over the new meetinghouse the past spring. And so he reserves his improvements of the doctrine, fully half the sermon, only to those who "han't had a hand in" it, "han't had their spirit heated." He rejoices with them that they have been "peaceable and faithful in Israel," as his text counsels, and that they have brought neither disgrace to "our Israel" nor dishonor to God. He twice denies that he makes "too much of things," asserting at one point that "the contentions that we had amongst us of late, though some may call them small, are ten times so much to be lamented, as any contention that has been in the town, and bring ten times the guilt upon the town." He worries that others throughout the land and beyond the seas have learned of Northampton's declension, that the town will no longer be a "place of wonders," a by-word of faith. "No town in America [is] so like a city set on an hill [by God], and to whom he has, in so great a degree, betrusted the honor of religion."

Edwards acknowledges that religion now "lies bleeding amongst us," wounded by discord and selfishness, but he rejects as unfounded that his preaching on such matters has been prompted by others, that he has "reproved any public disorders or iniquities" in the time of his ministry, "I never, as I remember, have yet, nor do I now, deliver anything from any instigation of any person whatsoever." He had flattered himself that the town would respond to its notice from abroad, that "we were not so bad" as he had thought, but he found himself disappointed in it, no more so than in its feckless reaction to the providence of the falling galleries, "I don't know but I have trusted too much in men."

In the forenoon, he had unfolded a tale of murder and deceit in the House of David. Absalom, the rebellious son of the king, lies dead; as does Sheba, the son of Bichri, who followed him; and Amasa, the king's commander,

-- 657 --

who "stood in the way" of Joab, a vengeful and proud officer, "fierce and violent-spirited." The stuff of tragedy, Edwards had pursued it, and the "factious, tumultuous practices" at its center, all through the Doctrine in order to drive home the practical, sordid effects of division and strife, scattering about the figure of the "wise woman" of Abel and the sayings of the "wise man" of Proverbs as so many correctives. Now, in the afternoon of his discontent, he confronts yet another failure of the spirit. "I am not able to set more powerful arguments to persuade 'em than I have done to prevent and avoid contention. I have sought out acceptable words, and those which I thought most likely to take effect: I have used my utmost skill: and I have seen the ineffectualness of all." In the end he advises the faithful to restrain or avoid their unnamed, contrary neighbors—"I don't determine who they be; God knows who they are"—and to pity and pray for them. Nothing, it seems, has changed. Yet such outspokenness, and the whiff of enmity about it, may be an early sign of the deepening rift that in time will engulf him and his church

* * *

Unlike its broad concerns, the manuscript of twenty-four duodecimo leaves betrays relatively few signs of distress, the dashes of omission and the instances of redundancies as much a matter of haste as anguish. The five deleted passages (LL. 2r., 3r., 16r., 19r., and 21v.) are about average for a manuscript of its length, the ten inserted ones (LL. 5v., 11v., 16v., 17r., 17v., 18r., 20v., 22r., 22v., and 23r.) more than average; parts of four leaves (LL. 10v., 11r., 20v., and 23v.) are blank, the whole of one (L. 24) is; there are no transpositions in its two preaching units. Edwards dates the only delivery of the sermon in the upper right-hand corner of the first leaf, "May 1737."

PEACEABLE AND FAITHFUL AMID DIVISION AND STRIFE
I am one of them that are peaceable and faithful in Israel. 2 Samuel 20:19

These are words of the wise woman in Abel that she spoke to Joab on occasion of his besieging that city. It had been a time of very great contention and confusion in Israel, in which the nation in general were embroiled. They were divided into two parties, one for David, and the other for his rebellious son Absalom. The nation in this quarrel had been put into a dreadful tumult; and many sad things had been done very unbecoming a visible people of God, especially that people that God had chosen for his peculiar people, above all the families in the earth. The main body of the people foolishly hearkened to Absalom, and fell in with him, who craftily wound himself into people's affections, by appearing friend to them that looked upon themselves wronged by the king. He said to 'em, "See, thy matters are right and good; and yet there is no man deputed of the king to hear thee"; and said moreover, "O that I were made judge in the land, that every man which hath any suit or cause might come unto me, and I would do him justice! And when any man came nigh to him, he put forth his hand, and took him, and kissed him." And by this means he gained the main body of the people so far, that they rose up in open opposition to David, though still a part of the people cleaved to David. David himself carried it with an excellent spirit; but Joab that was a chief manager for him, though he was of the right side, carried things in many respects in a very unwarrantable fierce and furious manner. So that the nation in general that were divided into these parties, were all in confusion; and many very ill things had been done on both sides greatly to Israel's reproach.

That party in Israel that was against David listed themselves under Absalom, while he lived, [and] after he was dead, followed Sheba, the son of Bichri. And his party it seems grew less and less; so that being pursued

-- 659 --

by Joab, he betakes himself to a fenced city, Abel of Bethmaachah, where he shuts himself up. But Joab comes with his army, and closely besieges the city, and probably reduces it to great distress. Then comes this wise woman, some of whose words I have chosen for my text, and cries to Joab's army out of the city. "Hear, hear," says she; "say, I pray you, unto Joab, Come near hither, that I may speak with thee"; upon which Joab came near. The woman says to him, "Art thou Joab? And he answered, I am he. Then she said to him," as we have account in the 2 Samuel 20:17–19, "Hear the words of thine handmaid. And he answered, I do hear. Then she spake, saying, They were wont to speak in old time, saying, They shall surely ask counsel at Abel: and so they ended the matter. I am one of them that are peaceable and faithful in Israel: thou seekest to destroy a city and a mother in Israel: why wilt thou swallow up the inheritance of the Lord?"

In the words of the text, we may observe two things.

1. We may observe the character of a certain number in Israel that she speaks of; and that consists in two things.

(1) That they were peaceable in Israel, in the midst of all this strife and tumult. They were quiet and made no disturbance: they were calm in their spirits, and wisely avoiding intermeddling in contention: they did not put their hands into the fire. When the nation was in a rage, they were meek and mild in spirit and practice. There were a number of peaceable-spirited persons that lamented the unhappy breaches and divisions of Israel, and carried it as their burden,1 as this wise woman evidently did. She had a great sense in her spirit, how that the inheritance of the Lord in all this contention suffered, and was almost swallowed up; as in the later part of the verse of the text: "Why wilt thou swallow [up the inheritance of the Lord]?"

(2) They were faithful in Israel. They were faithful to God, and did not, for the sake of their temporal interests, dishonor the name of God, and stir up public contention, and so bring public misery; but they cleaved faithfully to God, while the multitude seemed to have forgot themselves and their allegiance to God. And they were also faithful to their rightful sovereign, King David. When Israel in general, excepting the tribe of Judah, rose up in a wicked rebellion against David, the Lord's anointed, siding with Absalom; she never countenanced such a rebellion, though she was not one of the tribe of Judah, but one of the other tribes.

2. We may observe in the words that she ranks herself among those she

-- 660 --

professes, and claims this character: that she was one of those that were thus peaceable and faithful in Israel. Though the people of the tribe that she was of, and the city that she dwelt in, generally were hot and end engaged on Absalom's, and afterwards on Sheba's, side; yet she never joined with them. She never countenanced their factious, tumultuous practices.

And though she was a good and faithful subject of King David, yet she showed no spirit or fierceness of mind; but while all were in a tumult about, she was quiet. In this time of trouble and contention, she chose to wait on God. Though she was apposite in her judgment to the men of her city, yet she never appeared fierce or violent; otherwise she never would have been trusted by the men of the city. She was one that was sensible of the sad effects of strife, and heartily lamented them, as appears by her words.

Though she was of the same side that Joab was, yet she did not justify, but condemned, his fierceness and violent spirit.2

DOCTRINE.

[When] a spirit of strife has been prevailing amongst a visible people of God, and they have been divided into parties, 'tis that which a person may well [greatly] rejoice [in], if he can say [that] he is one that has been peaceable and faithful among them.3

This is a character that the wise woman in the text justly claims. She was one that was peaceable and faithful in Israel, in the time of those great divisions and strifes that had been in Israel.

She mentions it as a thing she rejoices in, and claims it as a just honor due to her that she be regarded as such an one; [and] mentions to Joab, as what might justly give weight with him, to what she had to say.

I would handle this point, first, by inquiring when a person may justly say this of themselves; second, why, when they can, they may well rejoice in it.

I. I would inquire when a person in time of strife and division amongst a people, can justly say that he is one that is peaceable and faithful.

-- 661 --

First. Negatively, in several things.

1. Not everyone that is on the right side can claim this character. We are speaking now of a case wherein there is, or has been, a division of a people into different parties. When it is thus, sometimes the case is so that neither side can properly [be] said to be the right side. Both may be in the wrong: each may be led by wrong principles, and what each contends for may not be just. Two parties may contend for that which belongs to neither. And when the ground of contention is a mere trifle, a matter of no manner of consequence, not worthy at all to be insisted on, as sometimes it [may be], neither can be said to be of a right side. But sometimes 'tis so in case of strife, that the cause of one party in the main is much more just than the other: their right is chiefly invaded, and they are principally injured. But when the case is thus, and a man is of the right side, or of that party, whose cause in the main is just; yet that is not sufficient to denominate him one that is peaceable and faithful in Israel. Though he may be right in his judgment, and the party he opposes wrong; and though he be of that party that is chiefly injured, and the party that he opposed much [the] most injurious; he may, notwithstanding this, be exceeding far from the character of the wise {woman of Abel}. He may be a very contentious person, and carry himself very contentiously, and be the blamable cause of a great deal of that strife that is carried on.

Joab was one that was of the right side in the time of the great contention in Israel, when the nation was divided into two parties between David and Absalom. He was of David's side, which was the right side. They were on the right of the cause; yet he showed a very fierce and violent spirit in the management of this quarrel. The wise woman of Abel don't justify him, but greatly condemns him, though she was of the same side that he was; for she was a faithful subject of King David. Yet she condemns his fierce and violent way of proceeding, as having a tendency to undo God's inheritance. Says she, "Why wilt thou swallow up the inheritance of the Lord?"

Though he was on the right side in this quarrel, in that he was of David's side, the rightful sovereign of the Lord and the Lord's anointed; yet he was not influenced by good principles, nor did he act from right ends, in what he did. He minded nothing but his own interest and, to get his will, acted from a proud, revengeful spirit; and this drove [him] to very unwarrantable and spiteful actions. So he killed Absalom, which, as the case was, he had no business to do. And so to satisfy his own envy and revenge, he murdered Amasa in the time of this strife; because he could not bear that Amasa should be above him, and have that place which he had formerly

-- 662 --

had. So in cases of strife among a people, 'tis often so, that though men are of the right side, yet in the management of things, they are like Joab. They justify themselves by their having the right of the cause; but indeed they act mainly from private views, to gratify their own envy, and the spirit they have against some particular persons. They manage things in a very unsuitable, fierce, and unchristian manner. These therefore are not some of those that are peaceable and {faithful in Israel}.

2. Not everyone that cries out of, and condemns, contention in others, is one that at such times is peaceable and faithful in Israel. A person at such times may say a great deal about the evil of strife and contention, and what a pernicious tendency it has; and may see wherein others are to blame in stirring up contention; and may cry out of the measures they take, and may inveigh against them for taking such measures; [and] may, when they get together amongst those of the same party with themselves, insist much in their talk on this. They may with indignation tell over how those of t'other side act, or stir up contention; and not only with indignation, but with contempt. They may tell how they act, and then break out into a laugh, and make themselves sport with it; and may insist much on the base spirit that others show. I wonder at 'em, wonder at their folly. And when they see those of the opposite party, [they] may reprove 'em vehemently, and charge 'em with stirring up strife with a great deal of spirit, [and] vehement and fierce language.

Such as those are not of the number of those that are peaceable and faithful in Israel. Though they oppose others for their contention, yet their manner of opposing it is itself contentious: it only increases contentions: they oppose contention in a contentious manner. They that in this manner do oppose contention, are like them that blow against the fire: their pretense is that they would blow it out; but indeed they do nothing but blow it up, and make it burn exceedingly the more fiercely. The way to put out fire is not to oppose fire to fire, but to throw on water. The fierce way some men have of opposing contention in others, is like a man's setting one fire against another. The effect will be nothing but to make the flames the most exceeding furious. To this purpose is that in Proverbs 26:21, "As coals are to burning coals, and wood to fire, so is a contentious man to kindle strife." But they that are peaceable in Israel, rather oppose contention with peaceableness and meekness of spirit. A man may show as much spirit, in crying out of others' ill spirit, and inveighing against it, as any other way.

Did the wise woman of Abel in the case of the two parties in Israel, oppose contention after this fierce manner? No; she took a contrary method. She lived in the midst of them, that were of the contrary side to

-- 663 --

her, in the city of Abel. But if she had taken such vehement measures, 'tis not probable {she would be} tolerated amongst 'em, much less had such influence {upon them}.

How did she oppose contention in Joab? In a wise, meek way. Though he had approved4 himself a very murderer in this quarrel, {the woman of Abel} won upon Joab's heart, [and] melted him.

Joab himself, on the contrary, took a contrary course. He opposed contention in others. He condemned the factious spirit that others showed, particularly the faction of Sheba (2 Samuel 20:21 of the context); but did all in a fierce, furious manner.

3. Not every one of them that seem peaceable, after they have got their wills, or after they see there is no hopes of it, [is so]. Many persons will strive and contend, as long as they have a gaining their will in prospect that afterwards will be peaceable. Many, after they have got their wills, will be very quiet that before are very fierce and violent; and others, after they are conquered, they see there is no hopes of getting their wills.

So it was with Joab, though he was so fierce {and violent}. After [he] killed Absalom, Amasa, and all that stood in his way, {Joab} sees himself established in his place of captain of the host. {He seems} very peaceable and quiet, though exceeding fierce and violent, {though exceeding} malicious [and] murderous {of spirit}. So on t'other side, the disaffected Israelites strive, many of 'em, till they see no hopes. {They knew} strife under Absalom and, when he was dead, then under Sheba; and when he was dead, too, and quite conquered, then as we read in the context, they returned "every man to his tent" (2 Samuel 20:22). But they were not of that number that the wise woman was, one that was peaceable and faithful.

Second. Positively. The character of one that is peaceable and faithful in Israel, in the time of public divisions and parties amongst a visible people, contains in it the following things:

1. He is one that is more influenced by a regard to the interest of religion and the best interest of God's people than any private interest concerned in the quarrel. In fact, his reputation may be concerned: he may be one that has been greatly abused by those of the opposite party. But those things don't sit with that weight on his spirit that {the interest of religion does}. He had rather lose in his estate, rather unjustly be put under disadvantages, and suffer much in his worldly interests, in his just rights. [He] values the honor of God and honor of religion more than his own honor and reputation.

-- 664 --

[He had] rather suffer abuse {from the opposite party}, rather his enemies should get their wills. {He} prefers the interest of religion more than abeing delivered from unjust adversaries.

From this especially the character of faithfulness belongs to him. He is faithful to God, and faithful to the cause of Christ. He adheres to Christ, the true David, the rightful king of the church, though he forsakes his own interest. As the wise woman {of Abel} was denominated faithful, in that she in a time of public rebellion, and when all about her forsook their allegiance to David, that rightful sovereign, she cleaved still to him, never went after Absalom nor Sheba. When others gadded about, she stood fast to the Lord's anointed. So Christ's faithful disciples are more influenced by regard to his interest than to their own private interest. They are of that spirit that they hate their private interest in comparison of the interest of Christ's kingdom. Luke 14:26, "He that cometh to me, and hateth not his father, and mother, and wife, and children, and brethren, and sisters, yea, and his own life also, he cannot be my disciple."

Such prefer Jerusalem above their chief joy, as the Psalmist earnestly professes of himself: "If I do not remember thee, let my tongue cleave to the roof of my mouth, if I prefer not Jerusalem above my chief joy" (Psalms 137:6). The Apostle much blames the Corinthians that they did not show more of this spirit, when occasions of contention arose amongst them. 1 Corinthians 6:7, "Why do ye not rather take wrong? why do ye not rather suffer yourselves to be defrauded?"

The wise woman was of this spirit. The thing that lay with greatest weight upon her spirit, was mostly the interest of the inheritance of the Lord. This was what she was concerned for, lest that should be swallowed up; as appears by what she says to Joab.

Joab was of a contrary spirit. {He was concerned for} his own ends to serve them. {And} that made him kill Absalom {and Sheba}; that made him kill Amasa, {for he} thought that Amasa would stand in his way.

2. They don't act from any edge, or engagedness of spirit, against any of their neighbors. {They} don't do anything from any old grudge. 'Tis not because they remember what such a man did at such a time—how he then did him an ill turn; how he abused him; how he treated him basely; or how he did things at such a time that were cross to him—that he acts anything. He is not acted from a spirit of revenge. He don't say with himself, "Now I'll do to him, as he hath done to me. Now I will be even with him. Now I'll let him know that he shall get nothing by such things as them." Such things are sweet to contentious men: nothing [is] sweeter than revenge to 'em. But this is not the spirit of them that are peaceable and {faithful in Israel}.

-- 665 --

He don't act what he acts from a spirit of envy, because it hurts him that such and such men should prosper, so that they should have so much honor; because it hurts their spirit, that they should have such power and influence. This is not the thing that pushes 'em on. The peaceable and faithful {in Israel} won't gratify such a spirit. He don't do things from heat of spirit, or any bitterness rising up against others, or from any appetite that longs to cause others to disappoint and frustrate them. 'Tis not because they long to get their wills of such that oppose 'em that they may see it, and so be grieved to see that they have got the victory. They attend these rules: Philippians 2:3, "Let nothing be done through strife or vainglory but in lowliness of mind"; and Galatians 5:26, "Be not desirous of vainglory, provoking one another, envying one another." His practice is such as is not condemned by that in James 3:13–15, "Who is a wise man and endued with knowledge? let him show out of a good conversation his works with meekness of wisdom. But if ye have bitter envying and strife in your heart, glory not, and lie not against the truth. This wisdom descendeth not from above, but is earthly, sensual, devilish."

3. He that is peaceable and faithful in Israel, in time of distress and division into parties, avoids backbiting, reviling, and evil speaking. At such times there is commonly a great deal of that, when men's spirits are engaged. When they get together, there will be a great deal said against such and such to set forth how bad they are, to run them down, to make one another have a worse opinion of 'em there. They will tell of this and that, and then make their reflections, and have their dissents; and will observe, how foolish and silly this action was, and what a base spirit that showed, and how intolerable this was. And very commonly there will be a great deal of ridicule. There scarcely ever is a quarrel subsisting among a people, {but there} will be much laughing, one at another, behind their backs.

When those of the same party get together, the time is much filled up with this.

But he that is peaceable {and faithful in Israel, avoids it}.

4. He is one that don't, at such a time, allow himself in judging and evil surmisings, with respect to others. {There is} commonly a great deal of this, judging one another's hearts, [or] what others have done that they don't know that they have done.

[They are] very free in charging others with things only on surmise. Such an one did such a thing: it was he that did [it]: he was the author of such a story: he was all the cause of such a difficulty. Or [they are very free in] judging others with respect to the principles or ends that they acted from that they don't know. They do but guess. One will say any such an

-- 666 --

one did so, only for such an end, that was all. He pretended to do it for such a good end; but that was not his end that was the thing he aimed at. And they will be sure to pitch upon an end wicked enough. Those things they do without restraint, they give themselves a vast liberty this way, though they condemn it greatly in those of the opposite party. If they hear any of them have taken such a liberty with respect to them, they are vexed and look upon it very ill.

But he that is {peaceable and faithful in Israel}, he wisely holds his peace. He don't take upon him to judge others: he bows to their own master: he attends these rules of Christianity. Matthew 7:1, "Judge not"; 1 Corinthians 4:5, "Therefore judge nothing before the time, until the Lord come, who will both bring to light the hidden things of darkness, and make manifest [the counsels of the hearts]"; Romans 14:4, "Who art thou that judgeth another man's servant to his own masters?" He avoids the condemnation of that scripture, 1 Timothy 6:4–5, "Whereof cometh envy, strife, railings, evil surmisings, perverse disputings of men of corrupt minds."

5. He that is peaceable and faithful {in Israel}, don't act in the affair in hand in a fierce or violent manner. As his spirit within him is not edged, so his outward behavior is not violent, but calm and gentle. When he talks with others that are of an opposite judgment, 'tis not with a violent, clamorous air. His speech is "with grace, seasoned with salt," as the Apostle says, "that he may know how to answer every man" (Colossians 4:6). His talk is not with that vehemence to be like wind, to blow up a flame, and foment division. But there appears in him those fruits of the spirit mentioned. Galatians 5:22–23, "The fruit of the spirit is love, joy, peace, longsuffering, gentleness, goodness, faith, meekness, temperance." He shows that wisdom that is from above that is peaceable, gentle, {and meek}. And he exercises that "charity that suffereth long, and is kind; that envyeth not; that vaunteth not itself; that is not puffed up; that doth not behave itself unseemly." He puts away "all bitterness, and wrath, and anger, and clamor, and evil speaking, with all malice," agreeable to Ephesians 4:31.

6. He won't, at such a time, act anything against any of his neighbors in a secret and underhand manner, secretly plotting against anyone; how to disappoint, [or] cross {him}; bring him down, {or} overthrow his designs; whispering one with another secretly; flattering as Absalom, and taking such like counsels as he; taking indirect and underhand methods to possess the minds of others, against such and such a man; secretly endeavoring to create jealousies of others, and to fill the minds of others with prejudice against such an one that, it may be, before they thought well enough of; neither doing those things, nor lending a listening ear

-- 667 --

to them, that do 'em; but rather distrusting of 'em, and discountenancing them.

This way of whispering, and plotting, and secretly managing for one against another, is greatly condemned in the Word of God. Proverbs 16:28, "A whisperer separateth chief friends"; Proverbs 6:16, etc., "These six things doth the Lord hate." One of the things mentioned is "him that soweth discord among brethren." 2 Corinthians 12:20, "Lest there be debates, envyings, wraths, strifes, backbitings, whisperings."5

[II.] Reasons [why persons may well rejoice, when they are peaceable and faithful in time of strife and division].

First. When it is so, it shows an excellent spirit. What an excellent spirit did the wise woman of Abel show in her behavior; who, when all about her were in an heat and feud, remained calm and sedate in her temper and behavior.

A meek and peaceable spirit is an excellent spirit, and is abundantly so spoken of in the Word of God. We are told that 'tis in the sight of God of great price. 1 Peter 3:4, "The ornament of a meek and quiet spirit, which is in the sight of God of great price."

For persons to remain peaceable and faithful in time of {strife and division}, shows wisdom: all such show themselves to be wise persons. James 3:13, "Who is a wise man and endued with knowledge? let him show out of a good conversation his works with meekness of wisdom." Solomon, the wise man who knew what belonged to wisdom, is often speaking of the wisdom of such a spirit. Proverbs 13:10, "Only by pride cometh contention: but with the well advised is wisdom"; and so Proverbs 29:8, 6 "Scornful men bring a city into a snare: but wise men turn away wrath." "Scornful men bring a city in to a snare": that is, it is such as bring a town or city into contention. It is not the wise men that do it; they "turn away wrath."

A prudent, discreet person is not of such a spirit. Proverbs 19:11, "The discretion of a man deferreth his anger." They that in times of strife are peaceable and faithful, savor of that wisdom that is from above. James 3:17, "The wisdom that is from above is first pure, then peaceable, gentle, and easy to be entreated, full of mercy and good fruits, without partiality, and without hypocrisy."

On the contrary, they that are out to enter into confrontation, show a

-- 668 --

foolish spirit. Proverbs 14:29, "He that is slow to anger is of great understanding: but he that is hasty of spirit exalteth folly"; Ecclesiastes 7:8–9, "The patient in spirit is better than the proud in spirit, for anger rests in the bosom of fools"; and Proverbs 18:6, "A fool's lips enter into contention." When two parties are contending, they are commonly fools that have the main hand in it, and carry it on. Proverbs 20:3, "Every fool will be meddling." The men that such times are commonly the most fierce and vehement in their way of talking, and are plotting, and secretly managing things, are foolish men. Proverbs 14:16–18, "A wise man feareth, and departeth from evil: but the fool rageth, and is confident. He that is soon angry dealeth foolishly: and a man of wicked devices is hated. The simple inherit folly."

Hence 'tis that the woman that speaks in the text, stands in the sacred records with the character of a wise woman. The Holy Ghost gives this character for the wisdom, that she showed in her being faithful and peaceable in the time of {strife and division}.

Such a peaceableness and faithfulness, as has been described, shows a true greatness of spirit. Proverbs 16:32, "He that is slow to anger is better than the mighty; and he that ruleth his spirit than he that taketh a city." Those men that are violent that are great conquerors who subdue cities and nations, are accounted great-souled men; but the wise man tells us that he that rules in spirit is bigger than he. This is a very honorable spirit. Proverbs 20:3, "It is an honor for a man to cease from strife." They are little-souled men that are apt to enter into contention, and to stir up strife. It is a sign of littleness of soul easily upon every occasion, to be put into heat and ruffle. A peaceable spirit is a divine spirit, a spirit suitable for the children of God; and therefore they are honored with that name in Scripture. Matthew 5:9, "Blessed are the peacemakers: for they shall be called the children of God." To be of such a spirit is to be like Jesus Christ; for he was meek and lowly of heart. "When he was reviled, he reviled not again." He was like a lamb before those that injured and reviled him.

Second. Because such persons don't provoke God as others do. Persons in times of strife do greatly provoke, especially a visible people, a people that are favored with distinguishing privileges. The people of Israel dreadfully provoked God in the time of that strife that there was in Israel. There were great provocations on both sides: Absalom dreadfully provoked God, and brought himself to a fearful end; and Sheba likewise {brought himself to a fearful end}; and the people that were with them: and Joab, on the other side, contracted that guilt in the time of this contention that at last brought him to a fearful end.

The wise man teaches us that contentious men do contract such guilt.

-- 669 --

Proverbs 29:22, "An angry man stirreth up strife, and a furious man aboundeth in transgression."

Third. Such persons have much the most peace and quietness in their own minds, both in the time of it and afterwards; while others are put into a ruffle and heat, inward peace is disturbed. Such persons don't quietly enjoy themselves. If there are the prevailings of inward malice and envy that are uneasy principles, they are to the soul as a fever to the body. It is both uneasy in the time, and also destructive; [it] consumes the health and strength. Proverbs 14:30, "A sound heart is the life of the flesh: but envy is rottenness to the bones."

Fourth. Such persons as are peaceable and faithful {in time of strife and division}, are like to be distinguished from others by a divine blessing. Blessing is pronounced on such persons as one of such a spirit. There is scarcely any spirit or temper that has more blessings promised to it than a meek spirit. Such persons are much more likely to have the presence of God with them, than they that intermeddle in strife. The presence of God is promised to such persons. 2 Corinthians 13:11, "Live in peace; and the God of love and peace shall be with you." Such persons are much more like to have the comforts of God's Spirit than others. And while others are in heats and feuds, they are often in the enjoyment of that "peace that passeth all understanding." It is said, Psalms 147:6, that "the Lord lifteth up the meek." He delights to lift up and honor such, and favor them above others with comfort and joy. So it is said, Psalms 22:26, "The meek shall eat and be satisfied." They shall eat and be satisfied, and be abundantly comforted; while the souls of others that are contentious, feed on the wind, and are in a poor starved condition. For such persons are in the way of no good. I appeal to the experience of all that are now my hearers whether at such times, as when their spirits have been heated with a spirit of opposition, and desire of crossing others, or the like, whether their souls han't been destitute; and instead of flourishing in grace, and comfort, and the light of God's countenance, all han't gone down [from] him and languished. Such persons ben't in the way for grace, nor comfort, nor any other good thing; but only to what is hurtful and prejudicial. Proverbs 17:20, "He that hath a froward heart findeth no good: and he that hath a perverse tongue falleth into mischief." On the contrary, they that maintain a meek and peaceable spirit, are in the way of light and joy. Isaiah 29:19, "The meek also shall increase their joy in the Lord."

Yea, they that seek peace are in the way of all manner of blessings. 1 Peter 3:10–11, "For he that will love life, and see good days, let him refrain his tongue from evil, and his lips from speaking guile. Let him eschew evil, and do good; let him seek peace, and ensue it."

-- 670 --

Fifth. Such a spirit is often attended with blessed effects to others. Such, whose spirit and practice is peaceable and faithful in the time {of strife and division}, are under abundantly the greatest advantages to do good. They can have the greatest influence on others to do service. Persons will be more inclined to hearken to them. An angry man won't hearken to one that is like himself; but sometimes they will hearken to such a wise person, as she whose character we have in the text. What blessed effects was there of her faithfulness and peaceableness in the time {of strife and division}. What power and weight had her words with that fierce and violent-spirited man Joab. She soon melted him as it were. And what weight had her words also with the men of her own city that were of an opposite party, and doubtless very much engaged; in that Sheba in his greatest strait chose to trust himself there with them, rather than anywhere else; which he would not have done had not he looked on 'em as firmly attached to his interest. Yet she went through all the city in her wisdom, and they hearkened to her. And the wisdom of this one peaceable, faithful woman was made the instrument of putting a final end to those dreadful broils and contentions, that had so long subsisted through the whole nation.

APPLICATION.

In the improvement of the doctrine, I would only apply myself to one sort of persons, viz. those that have [been] peaceable and faithful amongst us in the time of the strifes, and divisions, and parties that have lately been amongst us. I doubt not but there are7 such here present.

'Tis most notorious that there has been much of a spirit of strife seen in this town of late. Contention and a party spirit is the old iniquity of this town. It has been remarkably a contentious town, I suppose, for this thirty year. People have not known how to manage scarce any public business without siding and dividing themselves into parties. Though it be a great disgrace to a town to have any such true of 'em, yet 'tis too notorious to be denied of this town. When the Spirit of God of late was so remarkably {amongst us}, this spirit seemed to cease. But as God has withdrawn, this hateful serpent has again put forth his head; and of late, time after time, that old party spirit has appeared again, and particularly this spring. Some persons may be ready to say that they are ready to think that I make too much of things, and think worse of them than they be.

However, though I don't suppose that the town in general have actually

-- 671 --

been engaged in a malicious, envious, or hot spirit; yet so much is notorious that some hot-spirited, contentious persons—I don't determine who they be; God knows who they are—have industriously stirred up strife; so that such great numbers have been foolishly heated in their spirit, which they have many ways manifested; and so that there has been a manifest, open division into two parties, with a great deal of heat in many of them, one against another.

And these things are undeniable—he that would hide it, must hide the wind—that many men have shown an edge and grudge one towards another, and have had their spirit engaged to cross one another. And however they may have restrained themselves in open and public town meetings, yet there has been a great deal of backbiting. Persons have given themselves a liberty to run out one against another. Much conversation has been spent in it, yea, and laughing one at another; that is, when those of the same party have got together, and have been set down and talking about such and such persons of the other party, they, after setting out how they have done, they have broke out into laughter. They possibly might think in the time of it of no harm; but if two parties do so one against another, that is contention, if anything be contention, and a very bad degree of it, too. And persons have given themselves a great liberty in judging and surmising things one against another only from their guesses, things that they did not certainly know. And some have been fierce and vehement, and shown spirit in their way of talking of things one about another.

And there has been a great deal of secret contriving and caballing one against [another]. These things are too evident to be denied.

But I don't suppose that all have been guilty of these things in our late divisions. I am sensible there has been a number all along that han't been guilty, at least in no considerable degree: they han't had a hand in contention. There are some, without doubt, that have carried themselves like those that are peaceable and faithful in Israel. They han't had their spirit heated. They have acted from no manner of ill will to any man in these affairs that have been matter of strife. They have kept a calm, cool spirit. And they have refrained from these backbitings, and other violent measures, that others have taken: and they have heartily lamented the contentions of others. It has been really grievous to 'em, to see such strife amongst [us], after such great things God has done for us; and after all the obligations God has laid; and when we are so, as a city set on an hill; and after so much warning. I make no question, but there are some such; and those I would now apply myself to in the application of this doctrine.

First. Rejoice in it, and praise God for it, that you have been preserved

-- 672 --

from such unchristian heats and contentious practices, as others have been guilty of. This you have great reason to rejoice in. If you can say it, you may say with humble joy, as the wise woman did, "I am one of them that are peaceable and faithful in Israel." You have great reason to praise God for it. This is distinguishing mercy to you that God han't left you to such a spirit and such practices, as he has others. That you may see what cause you have to praise God for this, consider particularly,

1. God has kept you from having any immediate hand in this dishonor and disgrace to the town that our late divisions will probably be an occasion of. God has of late put great honor upon this town: he has most remarkably honored us by the great things he has done for us: the great work that he has lately wrought in this part of the country, began here, and was principally seated here. This has given us honor abroad in other places in the eyes of good men that have heard what God has wrought; for they have entertained very honorable thoughts of this town, on the account of [the] peculiar honor God has shown us. They have been ready to say, "What people are so great, that hath God so nigh to them?"

We have had great honor put upon [us] on the account by wise, pious, and learned men beyond the seas, who have taken great notice of what has been done; and they account us an happy people, and look upon us as a sort of chosen people above all others; and have very high thoughts of the blessings God has bestowed upon us, as appears by what they say in their letters.8 But they are, without doubt, expecting to hear of a walk and conversation very distinguishing from other people, as well they may. But the divisions that we have had, in all probability will turn greatly to their disappointment, and our dishonor; and so much the more abundantly, for the very high and great profession we have made. Those things that are amiss that would not be taken notice of in other people, will be taken notice of in us with surprise. There have been many all along that have reproached us for the profession we have made, and have called [us] a parcel of hypocrites and enthusiasts, and [a] whimsical, proud, deluded people. But good men to and fro have stood up for us, and have opposed those that reproached us; and have pleaded that it must be a work of God, and that we are certainly a wise and holy Christian people; and have had hard work to withstand the reproaches of others, and, it may be, have borne reproach themselves for standing in our defense. But when such things as these come to be heard of us, it will weaken their hand, and will cover 'em

-- 673 --

[with] both shame and confusion, and in great measure stop their mouths. It will [be] an argument into the hands of our adversaries that our friends won't know how to answer. And good men themselves will be surprised and stumbled, and won't know what to think; especially when they consider that 'tis so soon after such a wonderful {work of God}, which is heard of and wondered all over the country, and taken great notice of by good people. And thus we shall be disgraced and dishonored. And 'tis well if we don't begin to stink in the nostrils of many; for nothing appears more nauseous amongst men than a very great and high profession joined with wicked unchristian practice; as this, no doubt of it, will be the dishonor that will be the issue of {this town}, as 'tis said of a person. Ecclesiastes 10:1, "As dead flies cause the ointment of the apothecary to send forth a stinking savor: so doth a little folly him that is in reputation for wisdom and honor." But you that have been peaceable and {faithful in Israel}, though you greatly lament this dishonor we do ourselves, yet may have this to comfort you in this grief, that you have [no] reason to confess that by your sins you have helped to prompt God to leave. You had no immediate hand in it: those contentions were not owing to you, and so you han't had a direct hand in this dishonor to the town.

2. God has kept you, [who] have been peaceable and faithful in our Israel, from having any immediate hand to the great wound that has been given by those contentions to the honor of religion. God has so ordered in his providence that the honor of vital, spiritual religion is put very much into our hands, so that we may kill it, or keep it alive.

'Tis a time wherein such love of religion as we profess, is very much out of credit. And there has been a great fame of the wonderful appearance of it here far and wide; and 'tis known 'tis of that sort that Arminians, and others of the more fashionable sort of principles, do oppose and reproach. And the fame has been so great that the eyes of all sorts have been drawn upon us—enemies to vital religion to find faults, friends to vital religion to find wherewith to confute the enemies of it.

Friends have hitherto rejoiced and looked upon it, that they had a great advantage put into their hands. Not only in this country, but in England, great notice has been taken, and is more and more. [I] have lately received a new particular account from a letter that has been sent since, which [they] have wrote over, manifesting their great joy, and their design to spread the narrative of it in the world, as much as in 'em lies.9 And the servants

-- 674 --

of God beyond the seas, [who] are now rejoicing and praising God for us, and expecting still to hear glad tidings of us, probably have no thought entering into their heart that we in the meantime {have been contentious}. When their mouths have been filled with praises, our months have been filled with backbitings.

No town in America [is] so like a city set on an hill [by God], and to whom he has, in so great a degree, betrusted the honor of religion. {God has} committed the honor of his own great name by putting honor upon us, and by [the] blessings bestowed on us.1

It has also been very much taken notice of by pious and learned persons in other parts of the land: how wonderfully preserved {from contention}, [how] greatly affected with it {we are}. [They] speak how God makes Northampton [a] place of wonders. {And it is} manifest by what they say of themselves, how they expect we should be affected by it. But to hear, after all, that we {have been contentious}, what a wound will this be to the honor of this sort of religion that we profess: no doubt but it will be an exceeding wound. It will add an ill savor to the report that has been {made of us}. And it is well if it don't make good men that have confidently boasted of us, and gloried in us, ashamed of us. The Apostle warned the Corinthians against [it]. 2 Corinthians 9:2–4, "For I know the forwardness of your mind, for which I boast of you to them of Macedonia, that Achaia was ready a year ago; and your zeal hath provoked very many. Yet have I sent the brethren, lest our boasting of you should be in vain in this behalf; that, as I said, ye may be ready: lest haply if they of Macedonia come with me, and find you unprepared, we (that we say not, ye) should be ashamed in this same confident boasting." I have no expectation of any other, but that the credit of the work that has been wrought amongst us, will sink very much {to our shame}. I have heard already that persons abroad have heard the news with surprise.2

Let those that have been peaceable and faithful in our Israel therefore rejoice that they have been kept from having any immediate hand in such a great wound to religion. Rejoice and praise God that you have had no hand in that dishonor to religion; that a wise, considerate person, and that

-- 675 --

had a right sense of things, would not have any hand in [it], not for all our inner commons, nor all the rest of the land in the whole town.

3. Bless God that you have had no immediate hand in that wound that has been given by our divisions to religion itself amongst us. 'Tis what everyone sees and acknowledges, that religion of late has exceedingly declined, and that we are grown very dull. And there is no doubt but one principal cause, [that] has been our contentions and strifes, one after another, that we have had in the town. There is scarce anything that gives such mortal stabs to religion among a people as contention. Where contention is alive, there religion will be dead; and there will be nothing flourishing that is good, but it tends to make everything to revive that is evil. James 3:16, "Where there is envying and strife, there is confusion and every evil work." Religion lies bleeding amongst us. Christ, who has lately been so wonderfully gracious to us, bestowing salvation as the fruit of his precious blood, which he shed for us [is] as it were bleeding amongst [us]; as if he had [not] suffered enough already. His wounds have been as it were opened afresh by the selfishness and sinister ends, and high spirits, and envy, and anger of contentious persons. 'Tis a sorrowful sight. O, bless God, you that have been peaceable {and faithful in Israel}, that you had no immediate hand in it.

Fourth. Bless God that you han't contracted such guilt, and given such words to your own souls, as other persons have done. There has been contention amongst us, none will deny. And therefore some have had a hand in it; yea, some have been the fomenters of it. They have given great wounds to the honor of the town, which they should regard more than precious ointment, and grand wounds to the honor of religion, and to religion itself. But they have given to none of them greater wounds than to their own souls. They have contracted great guilt in the sight of God. If we consider things aright, you would not have contracted the guilt, that such persons have contracted, for thousands of pounds.

Some probably may be ready to say that I make too much of things. Though there has been some contention, yet 'tis not worth the while to make so much of it, and preach so much about it; but it must be for want of confidence. If anything, it must be for want of sense of the unspeakable obligations we are under.

I have that sense of the things that God has done for us, and the circumstances of the town now with regard to the credit of religion, and the many warnings and obligations we have had, that I do think that the contentions that we had amongst us of late, though some may call them small, are ten times so much to be lamented, as any contention that has been in

-- 676 --

the town, and bring ten times the guilt upon the town. We have laid an offense and stumbling block by such things before the world. It would be well for some persons, if they did more consider what Christ says. Matthew 18:7, "Woe to the world because of offenses! It must needs be that offenses come; but woe to that man by whom the offense cometh!" Christ there denounces woe to the man by whom an offense comes to but one single Christian. "If any offend one of these little ones which believe in me, it were better for him that a millstone were hanged about his neck, and that he were drowned in the depth of the sea." But those that have been the contentious persons amongst us, it is to feared, have laid a stumbling block before good people far and near. What guilt then must they needs have brought on their own souls, especially considering what abundant warnings they have lately had.

And it's well if some of 'em han't come with their hearts full of the leaven of a cloyed, ill spirit to the Lord's Supper: either secretly gratified with having got their wills, pleased to think how they have got their wills of others, and crossed them; or grieved and vexed that they could not get their wills of others, and so grudging one at another. This spirit remained entire in 'em, and remains still, though it may [be] now they don't say so much as before. Such have eat and drank condemnation to himself.

Let those that have been kept {from dishonor}, and have been peaceable, bless God that they have been kept free from thus dreadfully wounding their souls, and bringing such guilt upon them.

'Tis probable that some may say that I have been instigated by others thus to preach at this time. I have observed, to my surprise, that I have scarce ever since I have been in the town in my preaching, reproved any public disorders or iniquities.3 But I have heard that this has been surmised, that I have been {instigated by others}, though it has every time been altogether without ground. Such surmises seem to proceed from the same jealous and evil surmising spirit that prevails amongst us, and the same spirit that the Jews showed, when they said to Jeremiah, "Baruch the son of Neriah setteth thee on" (Jeremiah 43:3). I never, as I remember, have yet, nor do I now, deliver anything from any instigation of any person whatsoever. But I proceed,

Second. To give some words of advice to those that are peaceable amongst us, how to conduct themselves in time of contention in the town for time to come.

-- 677 --

As to those persons that are of [a] contentious spirit, and that have had hand in our former and later contentions, 'tis to be feared they will be contentious still. There is no prospect from anything that appears in men, or any circumstances that appear at present, but that sort will be still from time to time contentious in the town, for all anything that is said to persuade to the contrary. When men's outward interest in their pride comes to be touched, I have no reason to think from anything that at present appears, but they will contend and create strife and division in the town.

I confess that I have had great hopes in times past that the people of the town would be persuaded, and that the consideration of the great things that God had done for the town would have such an impression upon 'em as would be an effectual restraint; but soon saw that it had not so great an effect as I expected. Then I flattered myself that when the town came to know what great notice was taken {of it} abroad by others, that that would have effect. And when I set home4 the consideration of these things, I must confess I thought we were not so bad, but that it would have a lasting effect upon the town in general. But still I found myself disappointed. People proved apt still, in every little difference in judgment, to {be contentious}.

Then came this surprising providence, and so wonderful a preservation {from strife}, that was so exceeding remarkable and affecting. And so many families were immediately concerned in that providence, that I thought surely providence had put such an advantage into my hands; that what I said in public, as applying that providence and warning 'em from it, would be effectual, not imagining that so soon after it {I found myself disappointed}. I don't know but I have trusted too much in men, and put too much consideration in the goodness and piety of the town, but what has been might be enough to prevent it for the future. I have seen all external means, and all strength of men, fail as to any effectual preventing this. It is very like there may be some men in this town, that there has scarcely been a public strife in the town this twenty years, or never since they came to act in public affairs, but their spirits have been [on] edge, and their mouths open in evil speaking, in fierce and clamorous talk, and backbitings, and evil surmisings; though it may be they condemn quarrelling, and cry out of others' quarreling. As to such persons, I have now no hope, but they will quarrel still, unless God takes their hearts in hand, and makes 'em much better men than ever yet they have been. The wise man tells us how little hope there is of such persons. Proverbs 29:20, "Seest

-- 678 --

thou a man that is hasty in his words? there is more hope of a fool than of him." I have no reason to expect that the town will be in circumstances wherein it will have more powerful obligations, and external inducements, to avoid contention than it is now, and has been of late; especially this spring, since so wonderful a providence {has put advantage into my hands}, and such warnings on that occasion given from the pulpit. I am not able to set more powerful arguments to persuade 'em than I have done to prevent and avoid contention. I have sought out acceptable words, and those which I thought most likely to take effect: I have used my utmost skill: and I have seen the ineffectualness of all. There are many men in the world that, when they are opposed and crossed, will contend, let what will become of the honor and interests of religion, yea, though heaven itself should go to rack. Nero, the Roman emperor, wished that all the people of Rome had one neck, that he might cut it off at one blow.5 So there is a sort of men, that if the honor of religion has as it were but one neck, and their contending would cut it off at one blow, yet they would contend, rather than not get their wills. And 'tis well if there be none amongst us too much akin to 'em, if not some of 'em. The honor of religion in this land never had more one neck than now,6 and that betrusted with us, our practice shows how careful we are of it.

Therefore I would direct myself to them that I can advise and counsel with more pleasure and more hope, viz. those that are peaceable and faithful in our Israel. I would pray you to attend to a few words of advice how to conduct yourselves in case of contention for the future, in three things.

1. In such cases, endeavor to restrain contentious persons, while others are stirring up strife. Such persons as are peaceable had need to be diligent to restrain and prevent [them]. When others are kindling fires in the town, be you diligent to quench them. There is as much need of being earnest and diligent in such cases as to quench an house, when it has got on fire. And take heed that you don't do [it] in a fierce way, or by opposing fire to fire, but in a meek and mild way. If others are violent, you had so much the more need to be meek and gentle. "A soft answer turns away wrath," and "a soft tongue breaks the bone" (Proverbs 25:15); as we have an instance in the wise woman.

If you follow this advice, you won't only be peaceable but peacemakers, and will be entitled to the blessing.

-- 679 --

2. Avoid the company of contentious persons. If they won't be restrained, avoid 'em. Don't sit in those companies where they are carrying on {about others}. Don't listen to such talk. Show a discountenance of it. And if any come to you whispering you in the ear, and endeavoring to possess you with an ill opinion of such and such a man, don't hearken to him. Or if others laugh at their neighbor's faults, don't laugh with them. Don't associate yourself in times of contention with contentious persons. If you do, it will be very difficult for you to avoid being infected, and partaking of their spirit, and falling in with their talk, and also being provoked in an unsuitable manner to oppose 'em. Proverbs 22:24–25, "Make no friendship with an angry man; and with a furious man thou shalt not go: lest thou learn his ways, and get a snare to thy soul."

Third. Pray for such persons as will be contentious. They stand in great need of your prayers, for they are in a sad way: they need pity and prayers. Their prayers for themselves, if they do pray, ben't like to do 'em much good; for God don't love to hear the prayers of contentious persons. Therefore we are directed when we pray, to lift up pure hands without wrath (1 Timothy 2:8).

If you are truly of that character, your prayers will be much more likely to be heard; for God hears the prayers of the meek and humble. While others therefore are contending, be you praying and earnestly seeking to God for them, and for the ark of God that is like in such quarrels to be carried captive.7 And pray that the inheritance of the Lord mayn't be swallowed up.
