BLESSED STRUGGLE

IT may seem ironic that Edwards dismisses as "too well known" several places in Scripture about striving after God's blessing, when the Text of his sermon, and a quarter of its pages, is an explication of perhaps the most familiar (and resolute) struggle of them all, Jacob's night-long wrestling with the angel of God. But the Genesis narrative better serves his dramatic purposes—as, in a minor way, do the trials of the woman of Canaan and of the blind Bartimaeus—than, for instance, the parable of the frantic search for a lost piece of silver in Luke he leaves adding. Besides, Edwards considers Jacob's struggle "typical of something spiritual," a type of "fervent, and earnest, and persevering" prayer, as well as a "literal wrestling," adding another, and more enigmatic, dimension to the encounter. So he sets the figures and scenes of the context arching toward add: Jacob's fear of his brother's armed revenge for the stolen birthright; his loneliness in prayer to preserve him and his family from Esau's "hostile design"; his surprise at the appearance just then of a man out of the dark; his delight in his conversation and company; his plea to continue with him, wrestling through the night and the pain of a thigh out of joint; his suspicion about the change (and exchange) of names; the blessing then, and then the shock of recognition: "I have seen God face to face."

Well, not exactly, as Edwards tells it. What Jacob saw was "the angel of God's presence," since "God the Father is never called an angel" in Scripture, and he perceived him in language Edwards commonly reserves for the Son of God, in attributives like amiable, condescending, friendly, and sweet, and in abstractions like excellency, familiarity, holiness, and meekness. The "extraordinary person" Jacob met with, talked with, and wrestled with was Jesus, literally, and that authorizes Edwards to treat his perseverance typologically. Thus the experience of Christian seekers after salvation may often parallel Jacob's "fourfold trial" at Peniel: God opposes and resists their prayers, extends the length of his denial, disenables and all but conquers them, and finally succumbs to their inflexible resolve. Such trials are "most fit and suitable," Edwards explains, because they enhance the value of the thing sought and the way of seeking it, blessing and perseverance;

-- 419 --

without them the spirit of "thorough earnestness" could not be tested nor its fruits known. Accordingly, God tries their resolution and steadfastness by, among other means, a deaf ear and stumbling blocks, deferrals and diversions, frowns of providence and temptations of Satan, and "withdrawing, before a formal departing," bringing them to darkness and "extremity" of soul, unusually painful reminders of more recent afflictions. Not only do the trials often end in blessing, they also prove the weakness and dependence of seekers after it and their need for humiliation before knowing it, for "the issue of all those things, is to bring persons to God's feet."

Throughout the Doctrine, Edwards returns to his source again and again to ratify the struggle ("Thus it was with Jacob," "So it was with Jacob"), but he hedges it about with uncertainty and mitigation ("God seems to turn a deaf ear," "seems to manifest himself very angry," "seems sometimes to be a-going"), so that the whole enterprise hovers between what the trials are and what they seem to be, between hope and utter despair. By the Application, he can say of them that they are "only trials" and "no sign at all" that God would deny his blessing, and he can speak of the "exceeding folly" of backsliders for not persevering during the late "remarkable pouring out of God's Spirit" in town. Now he exhorts them to "hold on" and "hold out," though God "hides his face," his Spirit "going off." The greater the trial, Edwards is certain, the greater the likelihood of reward. Moreover, God "directed" his people to seek his blessing, and that "is ordinarily meant to accompany with success." Following upon that, and before he ends in five numbered fragments, Edwards can assure his people that they have "no cause to be discouraged."

* * *

Those numbered fragments, reproducing in part the opening lines of the first five heads of the third proposition of the Doctrine, occur on the last of sixteen duodecimal leaves and are characteristic of the manuscript. As many as sixty signs of omission, i.e. extended dashes, and other (unintended) lapses haunt its pages, its final leaves airy and vaguely abandoned (LL. 12r.–15v. and LL. 16r.–16v.). Edwards enters the date, August 1735, in the upper right-hand corner of the first leaf, and repeats the citation interlinearly on the ninth, just above the sixth head and below a paragraph break, to begin the second preaching unit. Two false (deleted) starts launch the Doctrine (L. 4r.), and two sets of transposed passages

-- 420 --

later reorder part of it (LL. 7r.–7v. and L. 10r.); a modest number of strikeouts and additions creates no more than modest difficulties. For all its cogency to the spiritual crisis in Northampton and its neighboring towns, there is no evidence in the manuscript that Edwards preached from it again.

BLESSED STRUGGLE
And Jacob was left alone; and there wrestled a man with him until the breaking of the day. And when he saw that he prevailed not against him, he touched the hollow of his thigh; and the hollow of Jacob's thigh was out of joint, as he wrestled with him. And he said, Let me go, for the day breaker. Genesis 32:24–26

WE have here, and in the context, a remarkable account of Jacob's wrestling with God, as he appeared to him in the form of a man; by which we are to understand a literal wrestling, as [a] striving to overcome in that bodily exercise, though typical of something spiritual.

We read in the foregoing part of the chapter that Jacob ordered all his family, and his cattle and substance, and took them, and sent them over the brook, and stayed behind himself alone; as it is said in the Genesis 32:24, "Jacob was left alone."

He purposely stayed behind that he might be alone for prayer; and the occasion of it seems to be the fear that he had of Esau his brother, for he knew the enmity that Esau formerly had against him, for his getting away his birthright, afterwords the blessing.1 And Jacob was now informed that Esau was coming forth against him, four hundred men with him; by which he doubtless concluded that Esau was coming not as a friend, but with a hostile design, intending to revenge himself upon him; and we are told in the Genesis 32:7 that "Jacob was greatly afraid and distressed." And this seems to be what Jacob stayed behind alone for, viz. to seek to God by prayer concerning this thing, that he would preserve him and his family from the hand of Esau his brother; and, we are told there, wrestled a man with him, till the breaking of the day. This person that appeared as a man was the Son of God, as is evident, because it was God; as when Jacob had prevailed with him, he tells him that as a prince he had power with God

-- 422 --

(Genesis 32:28); and Jacob himself says on this occasion, "I have seen God face face" (Genesis 32:30). And yet, though he was God, he is called an angel. Hosea 12:3–4, "By his strength he had power with God: yea, he had power over the angel, and prevailed." By this it was evident that it was Jesus Christ, who is the angel of the covenant, and is called the angel of God's presence. God the Father is never called an angel.

It may seem something strange, how Jacob came to wrestle with him. If he had only wrestled in earnest prayer, that would not have been so strange; but he did not only do that, but there was a literal wrestling. But if we diligently consider this story, we may conceive more easily how it might come to pass. While Jacob was there alone seeking God, there came one to him in the form of a man; and Jacob did not know that it was God till the last. But yet it [is] evident he was sensible that it was some extraordinary person, probably by his appearance, though it was night. [He] probably took him to be a messenger from God, sent to him on this occasion of his extraordinary danger by Esau. However, he doubtless perceived by his appearance, gesture, and conversation, that he was some person of very extraordinary excellency, wisdom, and holiness.

And it may be conjectured that Jacob might come to wrestle with him thus. He had, before the wrestling, been conversing with Jacob; and he appeared so excellent and amiable a person, and conversed with him in so sweet, condescending, and friendly a manner, that Jacob was greatly delighted in his company, and could not bear to part with him. And therefore when he got up and made as though he would go away, he might entreat him to stay longer; but when he still seemed resolved to depart, Jacob entreated him, if he would go, to give him his blessing. Jacob laid hold of him to stay him before [he went]. But when he still seemed resolved, hence ensued a wrestle or struggle between Jacob and the angel; the angel as it were striving to get away, but Jacob to retain him there. 'Tis evident that this was the strife, by what the angel says. Says he, "Let me go"; and Jacob says, "I will not let thee go." Jacob might probably be emboldened to this freedom by the extraordinary meekness he observed in him, and by the friendliness and familiarity of his conversation with him. The angel, in wrestling, touched the hollow of Jacob's thigh, and put it out of joint, which much dis enabled Jacob in the struggle; but yet he was so resolute that he struggled or wrestled still, and would not let him go. When the day broke, the person, having not prevailed by wrestling, urges Jacob to let him go. Jacob replies, "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me." He was resolved, if he would depart, and he could have no more of his company, yet he would, if possible, obtain his blessing before he went. The

-- 423 --

extraordinary excellency, wisdom, and holiness that appeared in him, made him value and desire the blessing of such a person.

But when, upon this, the person asked Jacob what his name was, and told him that his name should no longer be Jacob, but Israel, because as a prince he had power with God and with men; then Jacob began to suspect that it was God, and prays him to tell him his name. And when he perceives that it was God, he expressed himself as one astonished to think that he had had such familiar conversation with God. He called the name of the place Peniel; for says he, "I have seen God face to face, and my life is preserved."

Jacob's thus wrestling with God, was doubtless ordered in providence, and recorded in the Scripture history, to be a type and representation of prayer; of that fervent, and earnest, and persevering prayer, in which we ought to seek the blessing of God. And Jacob's resolution and constancy, in seeking the presence and blessing of the angel of God, is remarkable; and it especially appears by the manner of his acquainting himself, under the trials he had of it.

And there may be observed in the words of the text, a fourfold trial of Jacob's perseverance in seeking the blessing that God exercised Jacob with.

1. His opposing Jacob in wrestling. In this wrestle, there was a struggle between Jacob and the angel, wherein they opposed each other.

What Jacob sought by his wrestling, was that the angel might stay with him longer, and might bless him. The angel seemed to resist and oppose him in this; otherwise there could have been no struggle between them.

Now, this was a trial of Jacob's perseverance in seeking what he sought of him. That the angel should thus oppose him, looked discouraging on Jacob's design. But this was only for the trial of Jacob's resolution and perseverance in his seeking the blessing, not that he was unwilling to bestow the blessing on Jacob; for he came for that very end, to bless him. So God, when he has a design of bestowing the blessing on persons in answer to prayer, he stirs 'em up to be earnest in prayer, and oftentimes seemingly opposes them, and resists them, instead of hearkening to them, for the trial of their constancy and performance in prayer.

2. A further trial was the length of time [in] which he seemed to deny resist Jacob. It is said that there wrestled a man with him, till the dawning of the day. It would not have been so expressed probably, had it not been [a] great part of the night that was spent in wrestling.

It seems a wonder that Jacob was not weary before, and that it did not discourage him, when he saw the angel seemed to continue to resist and oppose

-- 424 --

him, in what he sought so long. It was a wonder, Jacob did not give over thinking it would be in vain to seek it any more; but he continued constant.

3. Another trial was his putting Jacob's thigh out of joint. It is said that when he saw that he prevailed not against him, "he touched the hollow of his thigh; and the hollow of Jacob's thigh was out of joint." This was after long resisting and struggling before; and the angel hereby seemed as if he was resolved to conquer, and not to comply with what Jacob sought of him. Jacob hereby was greatly dis enabled from wrestling; but yet he was so resolute that he went on still. He would not give out, like the woman of Canaan, who would not leave off importuning Christ; though he had given her one repulse after another, and at last told her, "It was not meet to take the children's bread and cast it to dogs" [Matthew 15:26].

4. And lastly, another trial of Jacob's performance, was his speaking to him to let him go, because the day broke; as much as to say, "Let me go, for it is high time I was gone; you have detained me here a great while, even till the day is broke." But even then Jacob declares it to be his final resolution, under all those discouragements and trials: "I will not let thee go, except thou bless me."

DOCTRINE.

'Tis frequently God's manner, when persons are seeking the blessing of him, to exercise them with trials of their resolution and steadfastness in seeking, before he bestows it upon them.

To open and illustrate this doctrine, I would, first, observe that 'Ti God's will, that in order to persons obtaining his blessing, they should earnestly and perseveringly seek it; and second, I would show what is intended by a trial of their resolution and {perseverance in seeking the blessing}; and third, what trials of their resolution {and steadfastness} God is wont oftentimes to exercise those that seek his blessing, before he bestows it; and fourth, give the reasons, why God is wont to deal thus with those that seek his blessing.

I. I would observe 'Ti God's will, that in order to persons obtaining his blessing, that they should earnestly and perseveringly seek it. God requires earnest and resolute seeking; as is most plain by many places of Scripture that speak of striving to enter at the strait gate; being violent [and] taking by force the kingdom of heaven; seeking as for silver diligently to find it; pressing into the kingdom of heaven; which are places

-- 425 --

that have been too often mentioned to you, and too well known by you, to need to be particularly mentioned at this time.

'Tis God's will that persons, in order to the obtaining of the blessing, should be thorough in seeking it; not only with respect to earnestness and diligence, but also with respect to perseverance. We are directed often not only to seek, but wait: to wait at wisdom's gates (Proverbs 8:34); to "follow on to know the Lord" (Hosea 6:3); "to pray always, and not to faint" (Luke 18:1); that is, to persevere in praying, as the parables that illustrate and enforce that precept of Christ, do show. The importunate widow, she obtained what she sought by wearying the judge with her constant importunity; she would not give out, and did as it were tire out the judge. So we are commanded to "pray without ceasing" (1 Thessalonians 5:17), to continue instant in prayer, to watch thereunto with all perseverance (Ephesians 6:18).

'Tis God's will that persons should thus seek his blessing, because 'Ti in itself most fit and suitable that it should be thus sought. Such a way of seeking is most agreeable to the value and importance of the thing sought: to seek such a good with thorough earnestness, resolution, and perseverance, is but treating of it suitable to its nature.

And by such a way of seeking the blessing, persons are prepared highly to prize it, and to praise God for it.

And such a way of seeking the blessing, is the way for persons to know their own weakness and dependence. Hereby they will be most likely to find their insufficiency to reach the good they seek of themselves; and how dependent they are on the power, and arbitrary pleasure, of God for the vestment; and so are best prepared to acknowledge God on it, when bestowed.

II. I would briefly explain what is intended by a trial of a person's resolution and perseverance in seeking the blessing. Hereby is meant any difficulty or opposition laid in [a] person's way, in overcoming of which a resolved and persevering spirit in seeking the blessing, would be exercised and manifested. Where persons meet with no opposition in their seeking the blessing, or nothing that is in any respect difficult, there is no trial whether such an one be thorough in seeking; because thoroughness in seeking consists in seeking with such resolution [and] steadfastness, as to overcome the difficulties and opposition that is in the way. And therefore he that meets with no opposition, is never tried whether he will be through: there is no opportunity for it to appear whether he has a spirit to be thorough or not. Here,

First. Such difficulties and oppositions are trials of persons' resolution and steadfastness in seeking, as they give occasion for the exercise of it,

-- 426 --

without difficulty or opposition in the way. There is no occasion or opportunity for the proper exercise of a spirit of thorough resolution and steadfastness, because the proper exercise of it consists in withstanding opposition, and overcoming difficulties.

And I have already shown that 'Ti the will of God commonly to bestow the blessing in consequence of thoroughness and steadfastness in seeking. And 'Ti his will not only that there should be first a steadfast disposition to seek, lying hid in the breast, but that it should be in [a] proper act. 'Tis his will to bestow his blessing in consequence of the exercise of steadfastness and resolution in seeking. And therefore 'tis no wonder that 'tis his will first to give proper occasion for the exercise of such steadfastness, before [he] bestows the blessing.

Second. Such difficulties and opposition are trials, as they make it appear, whether persons have a spirit of thorough resolution and steadfastness in seeking or no. Such a spirit appears by the proper fruit and effect of it. The proper fruit of thoroughness and steadfastness in this business, is holding out immovable through difficulties and opposition.

Such trials are not to make it appear to God whether persons are thoroughly disposed to steadfastness or no—for he knows all things: he searches the heart, and knows what is in men, without any trial—but that it may be manifest to their own sense and conscience; whereby they are the more prepared and disposed to prize the mercy, to acknowledge God in it, and bless God for it; and sometimes that it may be manifest to others, to the glory of God.

III. I proceed to show in several instances, what trials of resolution and steadfastness in seeking God, is wont oftentimes to exercise persons with, before he bestows the blessing upon them.

First. God oftentimes for a time, seems to turn a deaf ear to their cries; not that he really turns a deaf ear, but he seems to them so to do. There is for sometime no answer; neither is there any manifestation of God's hearing them. Though God may indeed hear, and intend to answer, yet for the present, he gives no testimony of it, but altogether hides himself. Though they cry to him for mercy, yet for the present there is no answer, no token for good. No glimpse of light appears. There is no favorable aspect of things; but it still looks exceeding dark.

So that sometimes they are ready to say, "God has no regard nor pity to me. I have been seeking and crying to God, but God don't seem to take any notice of me. God seems to shut up himself from me. I don't receive the least crumb." So Job 30:20, "I cry to thee, and thou dost not hear me: I stand up, and thou regardest me not"; and Psalms 22:2, "O my God, I cry in

-- 427 --

the daytime, and thou hearest not; and in the night season, and am not silent"; Lamentations 3:8, "When I cry and shout, he shutteth out my prayer." Persons, when seeking God, often find that true in Isaiah 45:15, "Verily thou art a God that hidest thyself, O God of Israel, the Savior"; and many complain as Job did, Job 23:8–9, "Behold, I go forward, but he is not there; and backward, but I cannot perceive him: on the left hand, where he doth work, but I cannot behold him: he hideth himself on the right hand, that I cannot see him." Thus Christ dealt with the woman of Canaan. At first he did not seem to hear her request, when she cried after Christ, "Have mercy on me, O Lord, thou son of David"; at first we are told that "he answered her not a word" (Matthew 15:22–23). So did Christ deal with blind Bartimaeus, when he cried out, "Jesus, thou son of David, have mercy on me." Jesus at first made him no answer, and others rebuked him, and bid him hold his peace (Mark 10:46–48). So when Jacob first sought to God, when he wrestled with him, God did not seem to be willing to answer his request, but seemed to oppose him in it; as has been already observed. When God thus seems for a while to turn a deaf ear to persons' cries, it is oftentimes only for a trial of their resolution and steadfastness in seeking; as it was in those instances that have been mentioned.

Second. 'Tis a trial of their thoroughness in seeking, when those things are laid in the way that are difficult and hard to be gone through. When persons are seeking his blessing, God commonly exercises them with such a trial. He lays those things in their way that are very hard to be done, or to be borne; whereby there is a trial how great a value they have for the blessing they seek, and how earnestly desirous they are of Christ; how strong their resolution is for it, and how steadfast they will be in seeking; whether their resolution and steadfastness is such, as that it can grapple with such difficulties, and stand the shock of them. Persons must not expect to obtain the blessing, without first being tried with those things. Hence the gate is said to be strait, and the way narrow, and so is very difficult to be entered. And hence 'tis represented in Scripture, as though the kingdom of heaven must be taken with violence, as a city is taken in war. When God [in] warfare takes2 a city out of the hands of their enemies, they expect no other than to meet with opposition and violent resistance, which will be a trial of their vigor and resolution. Those trials are of two sorts.

1. Those things that [are] very cross and contrary to the corrupt dispositions or inclinations. Persons' thoroughness and steadfastness in seeking, must be tried with those things.

-- 428 --

They must have those things laid in their way that are exceeding cross to their inclination. They must have occasion greatly to deny their inordinate appetites: "But if thy right hand offend thee, cut it off." They often will have those things laid in their way that are very cross and contrary to their pride. They must abuse themselves, and do what the pride of their hearts makes dreadful resistance to. As for instance, sometimes they have faults and injuries to confess to their neighbor, which is very disagreeable work to them; and sometimes especially so, because it may be those that they have to confess to, are those they have had a long quarrel with, and a particular prejudice against; and it will therefore [be] dreadfully cross to them to humble themselves to them. Sometimes they have restitution to make; and among other things, there are oftentimes [some] laid in [a] person's way that are very cross to their pride. They must also go through that labor and diligence that is very cross to their slothfulness. And every lust must be denied. And often God gives persons occasion for a more than ordinary self-denial in this respect, while they are seeking his blessing, by special temptations that they meet with them.

2. Sometimes they are tried by sufferings that are laid in their way; things that are not only cross to corruption, but hard to human nature. There are sufferings in their reputation, in the good will of {their neighbors}, in their estates, if not in their bodies. Thus Jacob in wrestling for the blessing, besides other trials of his steadfastness, he was tried with suffering: the hollow of his thigh was put out of joint, so that he could not wrestle without pain.

Third. They are sometimes tried by God's long deferring to bestow the blessing on them. God don't only seem to turn a deaf ear to {their cries} for a time, but for a long time; so that they are many times under great temptation to discouragement, and have occasion to cry out, "How long, Lord?" and are under great temptation to discouragement. Sometimes they have sought and cried, till they have been weary, and have waited, and looked, and nothing comes. Others have obtained mercy: God has heard their prayers, but themselves are overlooked, and distinguished by the darkness that they are left under. They are ready to fear they are marked out for destruction, that God will never hear their prayers, and are often tempted to give up the case. God doth thus, oftentimes only for the trial of persons' constancy and perseverance in seeking.

Fourth. God oftentimes exercises them with frowns of providence, whether they are seeking his blessing. He not only seems not to regard them, but, on the contrary, they meet with dreadful repulses from him. He exercises 'em sometimes with sore afflictions, and seems to manifest

-- 429 --

himself very angry. Instead of smiling on them, and giving them tokens of his favor to encourage them, they meet with frowns and tokens of his displeasure, which seems to make their case very dark and discouraging.

Thus it was with Jacob, when he wrestled with God for the blessing. God did not only seem to resist and oppose him, but at length put one of his limbs out of joint; which proved a lasting affliction and calamity to him, that he never got over as long as he lived. This seemed to look as if God was far from giving of him the blessing, and had a contrary aspect; as though God was resolved against it, and intended to afflict him, instead of blessing of him. But this was only for a trial of his constancy.

Fifth. God sometimes for a trial of their constancy and resolution in seeking, gives Satan liberty to exercise them with his temptation; so that besides other difficulties they meet with, they have great opposition from that grand enemy of their soul. God gives Satan a liberty to tempt them, so that they have them to overcome for a trial of their resolution in this business. God frequently gives Satan liberty at such times, to make violent onsets in men's souls to stir up corruption. He sometimes strongly tempts them to atheism; tempts them to call in question the reality of spiritual blessings, and to think the Scriptures not the word and truth in religion, and question the reality of a future state {of their souls}, and the very being of a God; exercises them with blasphemous injections,3 horrid thoughts that put their minds into a dreadful tumult; tempts [them] to neglect ordinances and all means of grace; tells 'em it will be worse for them {if they do not}; sometimes tempts them to despair.

It often is so that persons, while seeking God's blessing, have terrible conflicts with Satan; as when Joshua the high priest was seeking to God for his blessing, in the state of the Jews returned from captivity: "Satan stood at his right hand to resist him" (Zechariah 3:1).

God suffers Satan then to oppose [them] for the trial of their resolution and steadfastness in seeking.4

Sixth. Sometimes persons' resolution {and steadfastness}, is tried by God's taking away some advantages that they formerly had. They han't so great advantages, as when they first began to seek the blessing. They had more means and helps, more to stir them up, than they had now; so that they are upon many accounts, more exercised to grow senseless and negligent than they were.

So it was with Jacob. He was under much greater disadvantage for

-- 430 --

wrestling at the latter end than he was at the beginning; for at first he had the free use of all his strength, and of every limb, but at the latter end, he was deprived of the use of one of his limbs.

Seventh. Sometimes persons' constancy and steadfastness in seeking, is tried by what they meet with, to divert them from that business God brings those changes; and those things happen in providence that tend to divert their minds, and take of their thoughts, and turn them to other things, and cause them to relax their earnestness and the engagedness of their minds. And there is need of the utmost care and watchfulness to prevent it. Sometimes [it is] a crowd of worldly business: something happens in their own worldly affairs that tends to take of their minds. Sometimes they meet with something that is very taking and pleasing; sometimes with that that is very troublesome that tends to take of their minds. This is another trial whether they will be thorough in seeking, that God is sometimes pleased to exercise persons with.

Sometimes something extraordinary of a public nature happens that tends much to divert the mind. It takes up the discourse and talk of persons, and are ready to crowd in, and take up the thoughts, and fill the mind. Those things God also brings for a trial.

Eighth. God often tries persons' resolution and steadfastness by seeming in some measure to withdraw. Amongst other trials, God often uses this with persons. He seems sometimes to be a-going, as though his Spirit was withdrawing and leaving persons. There are frequently such seeming withdrawing, before a formal departing, to try persons to see whether or no they will easily part with God; to see whether or no [they will] be thorough then, and when they see an appearance of God's withdrawing, they will bestir themselves or no, whether they will hold God fast, and earnestly run after him, and cry after [him] in an earnest manner; to see whether they are resolved not to let him go, as Jacob, whether they will part with him, and with the strivings of his Spirit, without great difficulty.

Thus it was with Jacob, after he had been a long time wrestling. God made as though he would go and leave him. "Let me go," says he, "for the day [is] breaking." This gave Jacob occasion to express his resolution.

Ninth. And lastly, oftentimes persons' steadfastness {and resolution}, is tried by its seeming to grow darker and darker, the longer they seek. Sometimes after persons have been seeking long, [their] case seems to them darker and more discouraging than ever, further off than ever. {Sometimes their} case [seems] far more difficult than when first begun, {and seems} attended with less hope.

So it was with Jacob, {in the trial of his perseverance in seeking the blessing

-- 431 --

that God exercised him with}. So it was with the woman of Canaan, {who would not leave off importuning Christ, though he had given her one repulse after another}. But this was not because God had no design {of bestowing the blessing on them in answer to prayer}, but only for the trial {of their constancy and performance in prayer}.

And sometimes persons at last are brought to extremity. {Sometimes} it looks as dark as midnight. {Sometimes it} seems as if they should never obtain {Sometimes they are} brought to the very borders of despair. Oftentimes the greatest trials are the last. And if persons' steadfastness in seeking it, such as to stand the shock of them, they are commonly just before the bestowment of the blessing, just before light breaks forth, and the day of comfort dawns in the soul; as Jacob's greatest trial, when it seemed most likely that God would depart, was just before the breaking of the day, when he received the blessing; [as was] the woman of Canaan's greatest trial.

IV. [In this] place [I would give] two reasons {why God is wont to deal thus with those that seek his blessing}.5

First. Which has already been hinted. If God did not exercise persons with trials of their steadfastness in seeking, there could be no such thing as a steadfast seeking. [It] might be a disposition, but not an actual steadfastness. Man could not be actually steadfast in seeking; [for there is] no labor without difficulty, no valiant fighting, where [there is] little opposition. There is no steadfastness of endeavor in going constantly downstream, because no opposition stream carries that way.

And if it be inquired why God won't bestow {his blessing} without any steadfast or earnest seeking, it might be as well inquired why he won't bestow [it] without any seeking at all. The same reason there is why God should require any seeking at all, that he should require men to be thorough in seeking.

Men by seeking {the blessing} don't get the blessing by their own strength. [There is] no natural connection between {their seeking and their getting the blessing}; nor do they purchase the blessing, or in any wise make themselves more worthy of it.

But yet God has wisely determined to bestow the blessing in this way; for if men might obtain the blessing without seeking, and it were all one whether men sought or no, everyone would entirely give himself up {seeking it}. {There would be} no restraint to any corruption; {there would be} no use of prudence; {there would be} no endeavor, no exercise of any faculties in the case.

-- 432 --

[Men would] have nothing to stir 'em up to use the gospel, or ordinances, or sabbaths, or any means {of grace}. And then those means had as good never have been appointed {by God}, and then men had as good live in heathenish darkness as {the day of comfort}.

So God would not treat men according to their own natures as rational creatures; for if thus, there would be no use of a man's reason and natural powers in the case. And then God requires men to seek, and constantly to seek, because hereby [they will be] prepared to prize the blessing {by him}, [to] acknowledge it received of him, {and to} praise him for it.

Second. Those trials that God exercises {those that seek his blessing}, tend to their humiliation {and despair}. [The] wisdom of God much appears in thus ordering and dispensing upon this account; [for it] tends to bring 'em to a sight of themselves, {to bring them to} show what they be, {to bring them to} know [their] own hearts, {to bring them to} despair in themselves, {and to bring them to} empty them of themselves.

Thus, for instance, when God seems to turn a deaf ear, [and makes] no answer {to their cries for mercy}, it tends to bring persons to reflect on themselves, and consider how they have offended [and] provoked him, thus to refuse to answer; how just [to] take notice of what is in [their] hearts, how poor their prayers, how little to be depended on. And again, when things laid in the way [make it] difficult and hard to be gone through, {it tends to bring persons to} find [their] own weakness, find {their own} strength of corruption, [and] how much [it is] against this constant self-denial. When persons come to have occasion violently to resist corruption, then they will find the strength {of resolution}. But by their having those things that are most contrary to corruption, they will find {God's blessing}.

By God's deferring long to bestow {his blessing}, they learn by more and more experience, how far 'tis out of their reach, how impossible they should {obtain it}; and are led more and more to consider their dependence on God for it, and to consider their unworthiness of it. Hosea 5:15, "I will go and return to my place."

And so when God exercises them with frowns of providence to teach them that, instead of deserving the mercy they seek, [they] deserve nothing but frowns of displeasure, {they learn} that their prayers and cries deserve no gracious regard at all.

So when God gives liberty to Satan to exercise {them with his temptation}, [it] shows 'em, how weak they are, how exposed in themselves; what naked creatures, what infants in strength {they are}; how [their] enemies [are] too strong for 'em, [and in] what need of God's help. [It] tends to bring 'em down to the dust.

-- 433 --

{So when God} takes away advantages, [it shows] how dependent [they are], not on man's, but immediately on God's [help].

So when persons are exercised with disadvantages, [it] shows 'em more of the need of God's help.

So when God tires, by seeming in some measure to withdraw, [it] shows how dependent they are on him, how soon they fail.

So God causes it sometimes to grow darker and darker. Sometimes it must be as dark as midnight, before persons will despair in themselves.

And oftentimes the issue of all those things, is to bring persons to God's feet.

APPLICATION.

[Use] I. Hence what cause have they to lament that [they] have failed, and been discouraged under trials they have met with, in their seeking, because God hid his face from 'em?

Not without great difficulty {is there strivings with his Spirit}.

[The] more discouraged thought it in vain; thought those things a sign that God never intended {his blessing}; [thought it] to no purpose {to seek it}; and so [have] gone out, left off in discouragement.

Hence we learn that things were no signs at all that you should not have obtained, if you had gone on. What if God did hide his face {from you}? What if you did meet with discouragements? What if God frowned? What if the time was long? What if you were passed over?

Those were only trials whether you would be steadfast {in seeking}. [It was] no sign at all that [God] never [intended his blessing].

You have therefore done very foolishly. Now you have lost all {means of grace}.

You that lately have been guilty of backsliding—as I fear there are some such here, that have lately been under awakenings in the time of the remarkable pouring out of God's Spirit that has been amongst us—what exceeding folly have you been guilty of {and been discouraged under trials}.

Use II may be to exhort and encourage those that are seeking salvation, to hold on with steadfastness and perseverance therein, under all trials that you meet, though God hides his face.

Many are now in this town exercised with trials. {Many} others have been taken, multitudes {of them}. And you left {off} seeking, [for it] seemed to be in vain.

[You] han't the advantages you had a little while ago. And then 'tis a great trail that the concern seems to be abating. Such a time, even a time

-- 434 --

of the going off of the Spirit of God from a place, is a time of great trial to those that have been seeking.

Multitudes are wont to fail in such trials.

Consider that those are but trials of your steadfastness.

Obj. I have greater trials than others.

Ans. 1. Your steadfastness will be the greater, if you persevere. So much the more will you come up to use rules of striving, to enter in at the strait gate {and the narrow way}, seeking wisdom as silver, {diligently to find it}.

2. So much the greater your trials are, so much the more likely will it be that you will have success, if you steadfastly hold on.

No seeker in the world [is] in so hopeful a way.

'Tis the best sign of a thorough seeker; that is, the more opposition you meet with, the more difficulty with your own heart, the more likely you will obtain it.

The more God hides his face, the darker things seem to appear to you, the more likely {you will obtain it}; because you will so much the more answer the character of one that is violent for the kingdom of heaven. Those that meet with peculiar and extraordinary difficulty {and opposition} scarcely ever fail, if they hold out. Jacob was the more likely to obtain the blessing a great deal for persevering through such. The woman of Canaan was the more likely [to obtain the blessing] abundantly for [persevering through such]. So that the very thing that you make an objection of, and are ready to discourage yourself with, may be turned into an advantage, if rightly improved. For hereby is there occasion and opportunity given for such seeking, as God has directed to, and that he is ordinarily meant to accompany with success.

Hence, therefore, you may see that you have no cause to be discouraged with that which many are ready to be discouraged with, viz. that they find so much difficulty.6

First. [God oftentimes seems to] turn a deaf ear [to their cries].

Second. ['Tis a trial of their thoroughness in seeking, when those things are laid in the way that are] difficult [and] hard to be gone through.

Third. [They are sometimes tried by God's] deferring long [to bestow the blessing on them].

Fourth. [God oftentimes exercises them with] frowns of providence, [whether they are seeking his blessing].

Fifth. [God sometimes gives] liberty to Satan, [to exercise them with his temptation].
