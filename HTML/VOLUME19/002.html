NOTE TO THE READER

Representing the period of the Northampton Awakening—the occasion of Edwards' publication of A Faithful Narrative (1737) and traditionally the beginning of the "Great Awakening" in New England—this fourth volume of selected sermons shows Edwards applying a mature homiletical technique in his most ardent effort to lead his congregation beyond the limits of ordinary religion into that converting experience which his mentor Solomon Stoddard had argued would change its subjects once and for all. Whether or not the experience ultimately proved satisfying for pastor or congregation, the literary legacy of sermons from this period is of great importance, for in the long trajectory of Edwards' preaching career the period of 1734–1738 represents the apogee, combining technical mastery with great optimism concerning what could be accomplished through pastoral preaching. Edwards published his own homiletical memorial in Discourses on Various Important Subjects (1738), the only collection of pastoral sermons he made, including a preface which constitutes his most sustained discussion of practical homiletics. Thus, although some of his greatest individual sermons were preached in other periods, this period is more fully represented than any other (including the entire Discourses) save that of New York in the series of sermon volumes.

The reader of this volume may wish to consult the "General Introduction to the Sermons: Jonathan Edwards' Art of Prophesying" in The Works of Jonathan Edwards, 10, Sermons and Discourses, 1720–1723 (1992) for an overview of the sermons and an examination of Edwards' practices as a homiletical author. Volume 10 also contains a preface to the New York period which examines Edwards' early expressions of his personal religion in their biographical context; similarly, the second sermon volume, The Works of Jonathan Edwards, 14, Sermons and Discourses, 1723–1729 (1997), contains a preface to its period, stressing Edwards' intellectual flowering during the years of his tutorship in Yale College and his apprentice pastorate in Northampton under his grandfather Solomon Stoddard. The third sermon volume, The Works of Jonathan Edwards, 17, Sermons and Discourses, 1730–1733 (1999) introduces the period in which Edwards established his pastoral leadership in a busy provincial center, a talented and

-- xii --

promising pastor in a prominent church, though still a seemingly conventional man dealing with the inevitable complexities of church and society. The present volume's preface continues this narrative into the time of extraordinary religion in Northampton, the first fruits of years of thought and effort in the pulpit. As in the case of each of the other sermon volumes containing selected sermons, an appendix, "Dated Sermons," lists all extant sermons composed during the period covered by the volume, individually numbered and identified by Scripture text, and further identified by the doctrinal statement if unpublished, and by title if published in the Edition or previously. Throughout this volume, unpublished sermons are referred to by text and number as found in the appendix.

Preparation of the Text

The text of Jonathan Edwards is reproduced in this Edition as he wrote it in manuscript, or, if he published it himself, as it was printed in the first edition. In order to present this text to modern readers as practically readable, several technical adjustments have been made. Those which can be addressed categorically are as follows:

1. All spelling is regularized and conformed to that of Webster's Third New International Dictionary, a step that does not involve much more than removing the "u" from "colour" or "k" from "publick" since Edwards was a good speller, used relatively modern spelling, and generally avoided "y" contractions. His orthographic contractions and abbreviations, such as ampersands, "call'd," and "thems." are spelled out, though pronounced contractions, such as "han't" and "ben't," are retained.

2. There is no regular punctuation in most of Edwards' manuscripts and where it does exist, as in the earliest sermons, it tends to be highly erratic. Editors take into account Edwards' example in punctuation and related matters, but all punctuation is necessarily that of the editor, including paragraph divisions (especially in some notebooks such as the "Miscellanies") and the emphasizing devices of italics and capitalization. In reference to capitalization, it should be noted that pronouns referring to the deity are lower case except in passages where Edwards confusingly mixes "he's" referring to God and man: here capitalization of pronouns referring to the deity sorts out the references for the reader.

3. Numbered heads designate important structures of argument in Edwards' sermons, notebooks, and treatises. Numbering, including spelled-out

-- xiii --

numbers, has been regularized and corrected where necessary. Particularly in the manuscript sermon texts, numbering has been clarified by the use of systematic schemes of heads and subheads in accordance with eighteenth-century homiletical form, a practice similar to modern analytical outline form. Thus the series of subordinated head number forms, 1, (1), I, a, (a), in the textual exegesis, and the series, I, First, 1, (1), I, a, (a), in Doctrine and Application divisions, make it possible to determine sermon head relationships at a glance.

4. Textual intervention to regularize Edwards' citation of Scripture includes the correction of erroneous citation, the regularizing of citation form (including the standardization of book abbreviations), and the completion of quotations which Edwards' textual markings indicate should be completed (as in preaching).

5. Omissions and lacunae in the manuscript text are filled by insertions in square brackets ([ ]); repeated phrases sometimes represented by Edwards with a long dash are inserted in curly brackets ({}). In all cases of uncertain readings, annotation gives notice of the problem. Markings in the text designate whole word units even when only a few letters are at issue.

6. Minor slips of the pen or obvious typographical errors are corrected without annotation. Likewise, Edwards' corrections, deletions, and internal shifts of material are observed but not noted unless of substantive interest.

7. Quotations made by the editor from the Bible (AV) and other secondary sources are printed verbatim ac literatim. Edwards' quotations from such sources are often rather free but are not corrected and are not annotated as such unless significant omissions or distortions are involved.

Nearly all of the sermon texts in this volume were composed at the time of their delivery in Northampton. Any significant later modifications and additions are indicated in textual footnotes. The five sermons from the period that are included in Discourses on Various Important Subjects, printed in 1738, have been edited from the first edition prepared and emended by Edwards himself, and his preface to that collection has been reproduced in an appendix to this volume. Differences between these five texts as Edwards published them and the original manuscripts are described in headnotes and footnotes.

PREFACE TO THE PERIOD

AT no time in a preaching career that spanned more than thirty-five years did Jonathan Edwards achieve such sustained mastery of the sermon form as during the five years from 1734 to 1738, the scope of this volume. Near the beginning of the period he kindled his first revival, and as it spread to neighboring towns and villages, so did his renown, not only throughout the Connecticut River Valley, but to New York, Long Island, and the Jerseys, and across the Atlantic. His full account of it, A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God in the Conversion of Many Hundred Souls in Northampton, was first published in 1737 in London and Edinburgh and within a year had been reprinted there, issued in Boston in three versions, and translated into German.1 Near the end of the period he put together Discourses on Various Important Subjects, the only gathering of sermons he saw through the press; in his preface he recalls what led to the five it comprises.2 The first four discourses—Justification by Faith Alone, Pressing into

-- 4 --

the Kingdom of God, Ruth's Resolution, and The Justice of God in the Damnation of Sinners—were delivered at the time of "the late wonderful work of God's power and grace" and were published "on the earnest desire of those to whom they were preached" the last, The Excellency of Christ, was delivered somewhat later and was added as "proper to succeed those things that were to show the necessity of salvation."3 By the time these sermons were fixed upon, the Spirit of God had long since departed. The great work itself had been remarkably short—five months at best—from early winter 1734 to late spring 1735, and now those who yearned to read them did so "to renew the same effect in them that was wrought in the hearing" years before. It was 1738, and Edwards had been about the business of the awakening for more than half his Northampton pastorate.

Between January 1734 and December 1738, he preached probably four hundred sermons and lectures. Of the less than half that survive, some extend the reach of the previous dozen years of his ministry in matters of God's sovereignty, Christ's excellency, and man's depravity; others engage speculative theological issues, such as the nature of justice, the person of Christ, and the degrees of glory; still others meet the quotidians of a pastoral life: private meetings and quarterly lectures, November thanksgivings and prayers for rain. But given the controlling event of these years, there are many more sermons that deal with conversion and declension. Throughout December 1734, with the faint stirrings of the awakening in his congregation, Edwards preached salvation: that God is sovereign in it, that it ends in eternal life, that it is so glorious as to be celebrated forever, and that time for it is running out. In June 1736, a year after the revival effectively ended, he preached return: that "Christ is the Christian's all," that only the faithful will be admitted to see his beauty, that only their hearts burn with divine things as those of the unrepentant do not. In June two years later, he warned, "When God comes to execute deserved punishment on ungodly men, he will not pity them."4 More broadly, he lamented their communal fall from grace, a city on a hill in ruins.

The impetus for this restless pursuit may very well lie in the unsettled question of his pastoral authority, the need to make the parish his as it was

-- 5 --

his grandfather's, the need to end the rancor bequeathed him—that "old iniquity of this town." Although this may account for his heightened rhetoric in several of the thirty-two sermons here, the figural intricacies and tonal nuances in them are as much a product of his maturity as a writer as his response to a burdened past or a fevered present. Early in the period, he recasts the lecture on justification as a treatise in order to explore a longer, more complex form of discourse, not discontent; late in it, he dwells upon the parable of the ten virgins and the fruits of charity in experiment, not anxiety. If his sermons in New York and Connecticut hint at promise and those early in Massachusetts often at fulfillment, then the work of the five years touching the Northampton revival discloses not only bright success but the type and end of his homiletic talent and the direction it would eventually take him.

Apart from a brief survey of Edwards' early, often turbulent, years before this critical period, the events chronicled here derive chiefly from his published accounts of them, Faithful Narrativeand the preface to Five Discourses. From the first comes a usable history: preparation and the setting in of the Spirit, declension and the departing of it; from the second come his arguments for justification, excellency, and the plain style, and, parenthetically, his key phrases as section titles for this preface. But his dated sermons keep the calendar as well, more faithfully perhaps than his recollection, and they are better gauges of the rise and fall of the revival, of his hope and despair. And so this discussion follows in order the course of his preaching over these five years, save for two clusters of sermons, one on Christology early in the sequence and the other on the new meetinghouse late in it. The Memory of the Past sketches the evolution of Edwards as a preacher (1722–1733); The Frame in Which They Heard Them, The Old Protestant Doctrine, and Practical Discourses consider him as revivalist and controversalist (1734–1735); An Evangelical Subject, Others That Were Delivered, and To Lament Our Declensions examine his interpretations of providence (1735–1738); In Endless Controversy and Dispute focuses on his quarrels with his parish, especially over the new meetinghouse; and The Elegance of Language turns to matters of his style and editorial revisions of his sermon texts.

The Memory of the Past

In the late summer of 1722, when he was not quite nineteen, Edwards left graduate studies at Yale to serve as supply preacher to a separatist Presbyterian congregation in New York; in the early spring of 1723 he accepted

-- 6 --

a call to Bolton, a newly incorporated town in Connecticut, about fifteen miles southeast of his home in East Windsor. Once returned to Connecticut, he finished his master's thesis, resumed his scientific and philosophical speculations, and preached at Bolton. By late fall 1723 he agreed to a formal settlement and salary there, but the following spring he was elected tutor at Yale.5 Then, less than a year into this new calling, Northampton "Voted Unanimously to get Some meet Person to Assist" their aging pastor Solomon Stoddard, for more than half a century minister of the largest congregation west of Boston, and found one in his grandson. By February 15, 1727, Edwards was ordained; on July 28, he married Sarah Pierpont of New Haven. Two years later, almost to the day, his grandfather died.6

Over the course of these journeyman years, Edwards evolved as a preacher and maker of sermons, crafting the triadic Puritan form of Text, Doctrine, and Application into an instrument not only of persuasion but, increasingly, of art.7 In New York, his private and public writings document an intense religious experience and intense discourses to contain it, sermons at once personal and communal. Of the forty or so sermons and lectures he probably delivered during his eight months there, the earliest of the surviving twenty are typical of a young minister starting out, with numerous, often undeveloped, arguments and exuberant, if repetitive, lines. Other sermons are more restrained, governed by reason and precision, but there are tropes and ironies among them as well, the pleasures of a more seasoned rhetoric.8 At Bolton he repreached several New

-- 7 --

York sermons, reworked others, delivered two written at East Windsor between his active pastorates, and drafted seven for his new congregation—a seamless performance. At Northampton a far heavier pulpit load, unmet by the work of New York, Bolton, and New Haven, forced change in his habits of composition, especially after his grandfather's death, when the demand almost doubled.9 His sermon manuscripts now take on all the writerly quirks found in the later ones: a hurried march of abbreviations, cropped words, closed vowels, dashes and fragments, transpositions and interlineations. His concerns, however, remain much as before—the reasonableness of Christianity, for instance, and the glory and excellency in Christ, though his reach now extends to hellfire and types.1 Only later, particularly as the congregation's sole pastor, does he venture sermons of even greater complexity and interrelatedness as he engages other theological issues, among them the morphology of conversion.

Both his father and grandfather followed the preparationist model, that is, that there are distinct steps to salvation rather than a sudden onset of it.2 Indeed, a pattern of conversion had been part of Puritan New England from the start, if not with the separatists of Plymouth then certainly by 1640 in nonseparating Massachusetts Bay, and although the exact number of steps varied from minister to minister, the overall order did not. First came attendance upon the ministry of the Word and next the understanding of the law, or the nature of good and evil. Later, this general understanding narrowed to a particular awareness of personal sinfulness, what the Puritans called conviction of sin or humiliation, a condition marked by fear and faith and polarities of doubt. Profound despair set in, and hard upon it, in endless counterpoint, a cry for forgiveness. Assurance came then that God

-- 8 --

would be merciful, and so came unmerited, saving grace. None of this was certain, of course—"to be sure one must be unsure"3—and much of it was visible: the humility, the calling, the doubts, the walk with God.

But Edwards' doubts ran deeper still, well beyond the distractions of New Haven and the "various exercises" he practiced to overcome them. The contemporary account he started in New York inventories a more profound agony of the soul two years after the "inward, sweet sense" of things that he told his father of.4 Public conflicts beset him as well. Although the small, disaffected English congregation he ministered to in New York was cordial and close-knit, the equally small Bolton church apparently was not. Such was their dissension that by the time of his formal settlement there he thought it necessary to preach on reconciliation and restraint; later at Northampton he would spend a sabbath in May 1737 imploring peace, all in vain. Even the Yale of his youth had been wracked by discontent and rivalry.5

Climate of the land or weather of the soul, Edwards had to deal with yet another, perhaps more daunting problem, the pastoral legacy of his father and grandfather. Throughout the long years of their ministry, Timothy Edwards and Solomon Stoddard often roused the unrepentant to conviction and conversion. At his father's parish, there had been "four or five seasons" of revival, more than in any other town "on this western side of New England, excepting Northampton."6 At Northampton itself, his grandfather gathered in "five harvests, as he called them," in 1679, 1683, 1690, 1712, and 1718, some greater than others, and in each "the bigger part of the young people in town" seemed to be drawn to them. Yet when Edwards settled there, most were "very insensible" to religion and had been for nearly ten years.

-- 9 --

Eight months after his ordination in 1727, a violent earthquake shook the New England coast, jolting Boston, Deerfield, and other towns in Massachusetts to revival; in Northampton there was "a considerable ingathering of souls" but "nothing of any general awakening." After Stoddard's death in 1729 an "extraordinary dullness" set in, which Edwards sought to reverse,7 but many of the young continued "indecent in their carriage at meeting" and their elders remained offensive to each other "in all public affairs." In time—and "by degrees"—young people ended their tavern-going and night-walking and became more seemly in church, more religious in practice. In time the Spirit of God settled upon Northampton, and, like his father and grandfather before him, Edwards reaped a harvest of souls.8 It was, he would later record in the preface to Five Discourses, a "remarkable season."

The years leading up to it were no less remarkable, for in addition to his pastoral rounds and private writings, Edwards lectured in Boston on divine sovereignty, published a sermon on supernatural light, and began a treatise on justification, all the while keeping to his weekly labors of the desk. He preached on foolish disbelief and the pure in heart sometime between the spring and summer of 1730, on preparation and rebirth between the fall and winter, and on the glory of Christ about then. He revised the last of these sermons for a Boston public lecture on July 8, 1731, and published a further revision within the year as God Glorified in the Work of Redemption, By the Greatness of Man's Dependence upon Him, in the Whole of it. A small octavo volume of twenty-five pages, it was apparently enough to convince Thomas Prince and William Cooper that the "noble" subject he pitched upon with such "strength and clearness" was surety that the "evangelical principles" of Solomon Stoddard would "shine" in his grandson and that, despite his twenty-seven years, he was a "workman" equal to his gospel brethren.9

-- 10 --

In June 1733 Edwards returned to Boston to preach on Canticles and the transcendently lovely Christ; in Northampton two months later he preached on Matthew and the transforming light of God. Published the following year as A Divine and Supernatural Light, Immediately Imparted to the Soul by the Spirit of God, Shown to be a Both Scriptural, and Rational Doctrine, the running title on its pages announces "the reality of the spiritual light," and it is this that drives his argument.1 Neither the conviction that natural men have of their sinfulness, nor the impressions made upon the imagination, nor the suggestion of "any new truths" not found in Scripture, nor, yet again, "every affecting view" of religious matters, the spiritual light is "a real sense and apprehension of the divine excellency of things revealed in the word of God," a sixth sense. It is a way of knowing akin to John Locke's empirical model in An Essay Concerning Human Understanding, a "new simple idea" unmediated by thought or logic; or to the transforming light of Isaac Newton's Opticks made metaphor, the reconciliation of God and man in the physics of the natural world; or the sensus suavitatis of John Calvin's Institutes of the Christian Religion, a sense of sweetness in the promise of divine assurance, a taste of salvation. It is perhaps all these and a little beyond, the confluence of Edwards' philosophical, scientific, and theological thought, the new language of old belief.

The Frame in Which They Heard Them

"At the latter end of the year 1733, there appeared a very unusual flexibleness, and yielding to advice in our young people," and, shortly after, word of "a remarkable religious concern" in a village three miles from town.2 In March, Edwards had met privately with them and spoken on Job 20:11 of dying with the sins of youth. Now in December he takes up a text he had preached upon before—and would again the following month—Ecclesiastes 9:10, "Whatever thy hand findeth to do, do it with thy might," exhorting both young and old alike to seek salvation without delay.3 In

-- 11 --

February 1734 he closes Heeding the Word, and Losing It with an appeal to parents and, more important as it turns out, to (some) of their children: "If young men would form themselves into societies, and meet together, and spend the sabbath evenings and evenings after lectures in religious exercises, it would be a much more suitable and profitable way of spending their time, and that they would have a great deal more comfort in reflecting on afterwards." In March, Edwards asked what more God could have done than he had to bring them to salvation, whether Christ had not dealt faithfully with them, whether they were willing to be his.4 And then in April, in the little village of Pascommuck, the deaths of a young man "in the bloom of youth" and a young married woman "considerably exercised" about salvation had a "solemnizing" effect on many others. That in degenerate times the godly are "not so lively, but are more dull and dead," as Edwards remarks in a fast-day sermon at the time, was more than metaphor.5

Youth and the Pleasures of Piety, a sermon preached in May in Northampton and a half dozen times elsewhere, counters dullness of the young with a more personal remedy than religious societies. Borrowing from their love of finery and appearance, Edwards tenders them "ornaments of angels" and the "lovely image of God" for their enjoyment of earthly love and friendship, the "purer flame" of divine love in the "espoused" Christ; for the company they keep, the heavenly conversation of Christ—"company enough," particularly when they are alone; and for their sensual appetites, the Lord's Supper: "angel's food." But what in May was "greatest madness," the possible rejection by the young of the pleasant ways of virtue, became in June "infinite madness" in The Unreasonableness of Indetermination in Religion. Like the Israelites of the text, 1 Kings 18:21, they are undecided and silent about divinity, whether Jehovah or Mammon; like the Jews of the gospel account, they are wary and uncertain about a redeemer, whether Christ or a "mere impostor"; like Christians from childhood on, they are unsure and hesitant about the things of religion, whether fact or fable.

In July such indeterminacy was met with The Dreadful Silence of the Lord. God suffers stubborn souls and waits for them to forsake sin, exercising countless means to convince them; yet they continue in their secret lusts, their malice toward neighbors, and their "willful and allowed neglect" of Christ. And so God will leave them to their sins, Edwards predicts, withdraw

-- 12 --

his mercy, and isolate them, as he had the Jews in the text—a "parallel" case—and the heathens of Paul's account in Romans, who were not "a tenth part" as obstinate.

Within the month he wrote of the contrast between False Light and True, the "counterfeit" light of Satan that mimics not only the saving light of God but the natural light of man, particularly the workings of the imagination and the words of inspiration, the twin chimeras of the mind. A light more like "a comet or blazing star" than the abiding, if at times clouded, sun, it perverts the reality of spiritual discovery—the "sweet, and glorious, and delightful" sense of the heart—and breeds hypocrisy. In his thanksgiving sermon that November, Edwards celebrates the pleasures of the saints in heaven praising God, and later, at a lecture-day night meeting, condemns the pleasures of sinful mirth as "worthless, vanishing, and ruinous." At a private meeting given over to the doctrine that "early piety is especially acceptable to God," he proposes once again that young people "divide themselves into several companies" to spend evenings after lecture in "social religion." They agreed to do so and continued in them, he reports in Faithful Narrative, and their example was imitated by their elders. "This was followed with the death of an elderly person, which was attended with many unusual circumstances, by which many were much moved and affected." And then there began "a great noise" in Hampshire County.6

On November 18, 1734, William Rand of Sunderland, a small town not a dozen miles upriver of Northampton, had been overheard preaching "new notions as to the doctrines of justification," but he soon "retracted his errors" and returned to principles "approved by his church and neighboring ministers."7 However vague those notions remained, Edwards knew their effect, and what to call it. Arminianism held that Christ died for all humanity, not just the elect, and emphasized freedom of the will, rejecting both supralapsarian and infralapsarian interpretations of divine predestination. Condemned as heresy within a decade of its original expression in 1610, it flourished in England and survived in America in a distinct formulation. The noise Edwards heard was less an assault on Calvinist justification and predestination—that would come later—than a challenge to authority in matters scriptural and clerical as well as social

-- 13 --

and political, a cry that "God be fair."8 Though Cotton Mather counted only one Arminian in all of New England in 1726, Edwards numbered many more in 1734 and, using the real threat of prelatical Arminianism to depict the attenuated American sort, worked to silence them. And when in 1735 Rand preached what is more nearly antinomianism, Edwards still heard Arminianism, and would again when he heard Rand's friend Robert Breck.9

The Hampshire Association of Ministers, suspecting "youthful immorality, and anti-scriptural tenets," tried to block the ordination of Breck, newly called to the First Parish of Springfield, Massachusetts.1 Troubled by such interference in so local a matter, Springfield asserted its Congregational independence and voted a formal offer to him. When an ordination council of Boston and Hampshire ministers met to hear Breck, the Justices of the Peace arrested him, charged him with heresy, and returned him to Connecticut, the site of his errors. Later the Massachusetts General Assembly censured the civil authorities for their presumption, Breck was ordained in January 1736, and the "lamentable Springfield contention" ended—or so it seemed. But, at the behest of his uncle, Edwards wrote a defense of the Association's actions, full of "speculative niceties, and subtile distinctions," the mode of orthodoxy that, he later alleged, the "new-fashioned divinity" found so cumbersome.2

The Old Protestant Doctrine

A spirited polemicist, Edwards was as well a gifted biblicist, often centering a text in a complex scriptural narrative or improving a doctrine with clusters of citations. In one instance, he exhibits uncanny agility. In the course of God Amongst His People, the November thanksgiving sermon for 1735, he transcribes in whole or in part, in particular or in paraphrase, roughly seven scriptural texts per manuscript page, nearly 150 in all, a rich mosaic of figures and tropes.3 But in a two-part lecture on justification the previous November, he had faced the more daunting prospect of reconciling two texts opposed to each other: the touchstone of his discourse, Romans 4:5, "But to him that worketh not, but believeth on him that justifieth the ungodly, his faith is counted for righteousness," and a text uncomfortably at odds with it, James 2:24, "Ye see then how that by works a man is justified, and not by faith only." Before setting out on Justification by Faith Alone, Edwards had explored the proposition in the Miscellanies several times, and had already preached upon it in 1729, but in none of the manuscripts before the lecture does the James text arise. As it is, it occurs far earlier in manuscript than in the later (and longer) version, where it appears near the end as the sixth and last objection to the doctrine. By that time Edwards could dispose of it with some very nice distinctions.4

The doctrine sets out the terms of the argument plainly in the contrast between what a man does and what he believes, between his works and his faith. Covenant, or federal, theology held that God had made an agreement

-- 15 --

with Adam offering him (and, through him, his posterity) eternal happiness, God's freely given promise for the active, not simply the passive obedience to his law: a covenant of works. In his disobedience, his original sin, Adam had forfeited the covenant of works and earned himself (and us) eternal damnation. But God, by his sovereign will and in a free act of love for man, offered Abraham (and his seed) a new covenant, one determined by faith, and gave Christ, his only begotten Son, to satisfy the old covenant and to justify, that is, to make or declare, man righteous: a covenant of grace. Scriptural proof of justification abounds, especially in the teachings of Paul. "There is no one doctrine," Edwards avers, "that he insists so much upon, and is so particular in, and that he handles with so much distinctness, explaining, and giving reasons, and answering objections." All of it makes clear that works share no part of justification, not ceremonial law or moral law, not obedience or virtue or righteousness. To share in justification "diminishes" God's grace, compromises Christ's sacrifice, and makes man "his own savior." Justification demands acts of "positive" and "perfect" obedience, a first act of faith and perseverance in it, "an evangelical, childlike, believing obedience."

Within weeks of preaching on justification, Edwards cautions against letting "the precious days and years of youth slip without improvement." Young people so forced his thoughts that even his comments on their elders, those who have "lost the best" of their time, were predicated on them: "What have you done with the whole time of your youth, you that are past your youth?" So he exhorts them in The Preciousness of Time to "wake out of sleep, thoroughly to rouse up," and work toward eternity. "And then it was, in the latter part of December [1734], that the Spirit of God began extraordinarily to set in, and wonderfully to work amongst us; and there were, very suddenly, one after another, five or six persons who were to all appearance savingly converted, and some of them wrought upon in a very remarkable manner."

Months before, he had preached on the conversions of the terrified jailer after the earthquake at Philippi, of the "notedly wicked" woman, the "noted sinner" Zaccheus, and the "notoriously wicked" thief, celebrating God's infinite mercy, much as Paul and Silas had, "in these days of declension."5 Now, a young woman, "one of the greatest company-keepers

-- 16 --

in the whole town," told a "surprised" Edwards that "God had given her a new heart, truly broken and sanctified." Once begun, the awakening was the "greatest occasion" of revival of "anything that ever came to pass in town," the news of it like "a flash of lightning, upon the hearts of young people," his treatise on justification vindicated by it. The world, then, "was a thing only by the bye. The only thing in their view was to get the kingdom of God, and everyone appeared pressing into it."6

Practical Discourses

Of the other sermons delivered from January through May 1735 only eleven survive, so that the three that follow the published version of Justification by Faith Alone may very well be as "practical" as Edwards says they are; that they are "very plain" may be something else again.7 In the first paragraph of Pressing into the Kingdom of God, a four-unit sermon delivered in February, he compares the coming of John the Baptist, "Christ's forerunner," to the dawn of a new day. "First the daystar rises; next follows the light of the sun itself, but dimly reflected, in the dawning of the day; but this light increases, and shines more and more, and the stars that served for light during the foregoing night, gradually go out, and their light ceases, as being now needless, till at length the sun rises, and enlightens the world by his own direct light, which increases as he ascends higher above the horizon, till the daystar itself is gradually put out, and disappears." But he soon abandons such conceits, and later semantics, for the "perishing necessity" of getting into the kingdom of God, and then the sermon bristles and storms: "'[T]is NOW, at this day; NOW is the accepted time, even while it is called today!" to "LET ALL GO" for salvation and "look not behind you!" To old sinners he bids fair warning not to "stand it out" this time, for God is "about finishing" with them; to young sinners, "now under their first special convictions," he tenders fair prospects for their redemption: "Make thorough work of it the first time!" And they did, in surprising numbers.8

-- 17 --

The third and shortest of the Five Discourses offers another example of Edwards' biblicism. Delivered during a spring "full of the presence of God" of love and joyful singing,9 Ruth's Resolution reconciles not contradictory texts, as does Justification by Faith Alone, but complementary figures. Scripture, Edwards explains, "often takes notice of little things" that "remotely relate" to Christ, so that as David and Solomon are of Ruth's seed he is "of her posterity," a distant son. More to his purpose, though, Ruth "seems to be a type of the Gentile church, and also of every sincere convert," since she forsakes all out of love for God and proves inflexible under trial and virtuous in dedication; even the ambiguous resolution of his title serves the type of his text. Still, it is the paradox of her act, echoed in leave and cleave, that is particularly useful to him, for Ruth typifies not only the Christian convert but the conversion process itself: the sinner departs "the tents of wickedness, to dwell in the land of uprightness." And so Edwards implores those left behind not to part from others "of your sort"—the young, who earlier followed their companions in sin to "now follow them to Christ," and the very young "to seek and cry" to God, as some were about to, remarkably, Phebe Bartlett, aged four.1

The mutuality of salvation in April becomes by May an "exact mutual answerableness" of punishment in The Justice of God in the Damnation of Sinners, and the young people he spoke of then now appear damned by parental neglect, given "bread to eat" but starved of "the bread of life."2 Inasmuch as obedience is proportionate to authority, the greater the authority,

-- 18 --

the greater the obedience; further, a deserved punishment is a just punishment, for that is the "very meaning of the words." Man violated "infinite obligations" to God, a sin "infinitely heinous," and so deserves infinite, that is, just, punishment, even if the sin appears insignificant. Using the figure of his text, "That every mouth may be stopped," Edwards exhorts his parishioners to "inquire at the mouth of conscience" the manifold causes of God's displeasure with them, and then, after cataloging the dismal record—"feasting your lusts" at public worship, for instance, and "quenching God's Spirit" during awakening—declares that none ought to "open his mouth" to protest God's justice. Meted out measure for measure, God's just condemnation of man balances man's treatment of God, of Christ, of others, and of himself; still, Edwards cautions against despair. "How are they ready to draw towards despair," he observes, "as if there were no hope or help for such wicked creatures as they!" Edwards would learn the measure of that all too soon. On May 30 he began "a particular account of the present extraordinary circumstances" in Northampton for the Rev. Dr. Benjamin Colman of the Brattle Street Church, Boston; he finished it the next day. On the following morning, a Sunday, his Uncle Hawley "laid violent hands upon himself," cutting his throat, a victim, Edwards wrote in a postscript two days later, of "deep melancholy" and "despairing thoughts" about the condition of his soul.3

"In the latter part of May," Edwards would later report, "it began to be very sensible that the Spirit of God was gradually withdrawing from us, and Satan seemed to be more let loose, and raged in a dreadful manner," Hawley's suicide being the "first instance" of it. "The news of this extraordinarily affected the minds of people here," and soon "multitudes in this and other towns seemed to have it strongly suggested to 'em, and pressed upon 'em, to do as this person had done.…'Cut your own throat, now is a good opportunity: now, now!' "4 Edwards delivered the funeral sermon on another text from Romans, "For when we were yet without strength, in due time Christ died for the ungodly," and again, as in The Justice of God in the Damnation of Sinners, he begins with justification by faith alone and argues inability. But gone from Our Weakness, Christ's Strength

are the thundering imprecations of late May, the lengthy record of man's transgressions, and the metaphors of debasement: instead of stopped mouths, impotence. Aside from the grim prospect of inability, the only allusion to Hawley's suicide comes in the last paragraph, when Edwards tries to explain God's displeasure with Northampton for boasting his favor and glory to neighboring towns. "God has of late awfully rebuked us, and as it were spit in our face, in that such a thing should happen amongst [us] in one of our principal men, and the instances of this nature being repeated once more. An Evangelical Subject

That fast-day sermon is the first of several on Christological themes following the death of Hawley. Designed perhaps to quicken the work of redemption against the contagion of despair, these discourses center on the living Christ, his pledge of salvation, his love, excellency, and willing sacrifice, his timelessness.5 Twice in August Edwards preaches on the person of Christ. In Blessed Struggle, Jacob's night-long wrestling with an angel is a labor at once "typical of something spiritual," a type of "fervent, and earnest, and persevering" prayer, as well as a "literal wrestling." It is "the angel of God's presence" whom Jacob strives with, portrayed in language Edwards commonly reserved for the Son of God. The "extraordinary person" Jacob met with, talked with, and wrestled with was Christ, literally, and that authorizes Edwards to treat Jacob's perseverance typologically as the experience of Christian seekers after salvation. Although the companion sermon, The Sweet Harmony of Christ, retains the diction of Blessed Struggle, it increases manyfold the occurrence of one word, sweet, and adds in even greater number another wholly absent from the first, love. Described as "everlasting," "transcendent," "supreme," and "dying" in a single paragraph, love governs the discourse, much as the dozen or so citations from Canticles sanction it and the twin notions of mutuality and harmony promise it. Indeed, "the nature and genius of Christianity" lies neither in professions, tenets, observances, or outward shows nor in orthodoxy, superstition, or "will-worship" but in the "internal, spiritual harmony between Christ and the soul," the mutual love of one for the other.

-- 20 --

Earlier, The Free and Voluntary Suffering and Death of Christ, preached in April, had counted the cost of such love.6 Jesus was a willing agent in his own death, laying down his life for sinners "with great delight," Edwards argues, a perfectly free and voluntary act pledged before the world began, part of his priesthood as God-man. As God, his death and resurrection were prefigured in images and shadows in the Old Testament; as man, he suffered an agonizing death for sinners to glorify God, a tribute of the Son to the justice of the Father and testimony of his love for the elect. Because Christ was both passive and active in his death, it was both satisfactory and righteous, the one a redemption from sin, the other a redemption to God. And because his last act was obedience to a positive precept, he merited heaven for himself and purchased it for sinners. The month before Edwards had spoken of Christ as "a person of transcendent worthiness in the sight of God" and a month after of both his excellency and "dreadful presence" in June at Northampton he promised that the faithful shall see "their King in his beauty," at Boston that "Christ is the Christian's all." Now in August he prepares for the Lord's Supper.7

In The Excellency of Christ, Edwards celebrates not so much the feast as the affection Christ shares with his invited guests. Edwards' first extant sacramental sermon, The Spiritual Blessings of the Gospel Represented by a Feast, preached sometime in late 1728, extols the "pure dainties" at the table and "every kind of thing that tends to make us happy" there; in a sermon a year later, he contends that they "need not and ought not to set any bounds to their spiritual and gracious appetites." Still, he warns of dissembling, and, in Glorying in the Savior, of the "seeming union" and "seeming subjection" to Christ of hypocrites, whose "tongues and outsides," but not their hearts, are his. In Self-Examination and the Lord's Supper two years later, Edwards compares unworthy partakers to those who come to a "prince's table" in "garments all over defiled with ordure"; they eat "as a wild beast eats his prey," with a "murderous bloodthirstiness," much as "the murderers of Christ might be metaphorically said to eat the flesh and drink the blood of Christ."8

Little of that, certainly none of the vehemence, touches The Excellency

-- 21 --

of Christ, the longest of the sacramental sermons: the words friend or friendship occur thirty times over as many manuscript leaves. "As the glory of Christ appears in the qualifications of his human nature," Edwards makes plain, "it appears to us in excellencies that are of our own kind, and are exercised in our own way and manner, and so, in some respects, are peculiarly fitted to invite our acquaintance, and draw our affection." In the published version he goes further, nearer the divine glory: "Christ has brought it to pass, that those that the Father has given him, should be brought into the household of God; that he, and his Father, and his people, should be as it were one society, one family; that the church should be as it were admitted into the society of the blessed Trinity."9

Unlike The Free and Voluntary Suffering and Death of Christ, delivered in April 1736, another sermon preached two years later considers Christ's divine rather than his human nature, since the historical Jesus, though free of sin, suffered and died and therefore was not "absolutely" unchanged. Immutable in his office as Mediator and Savior, Christ delights in it, and did so even on the cross, for he aims at the same two ends, the glorification of God and the happiness of the saints, and acts by the same two rules, the covenant of redemption and the covenant of grace. Though his wrathful judgment of the ungodly is "infinitely" dreadful at the outset, it increases "a thousand times more so" as time and sin go on. "You hung over the pit of hell then, and so you do now, but with this difference," Edwards warns, "that you have ever since been kindling and enraging the flames of the fiery gulf that you hang over, so that they are vastly fiercer

-- 22 --

than they were then. And the moth of time has been nibbling at that slender thread ever since, and has much nearer gnawed it off than it had then." That hanging figure, starkly drawn at Enfield, Connecticut, three years later, realized the Great Awakening then but became in time its notorious symbol.1 In 1738 it simply, if bitterly, commemorates the passing of Northampton's revival three years before.

Others That Were Delivered

Aside from the funeral oration, neither of the other two extant sermons in June 1735 refers to Hawley,2 and in the only one in July, Continuing God's Presence, Edwards all but avoids the subject as "a thing of a very awful nature fallen out in the town." Even the end of the revival was muted in forbearance. As he had before the onset of the awakening, Edwards prescribed religious societies of the young to remedy declension, though now he suggested that "both sexes and all ages do meet together" as well as apart, citing Acts 16:13 to justify, if somewhat tentatively, societies of women, especially young unmarried women. Nothing changed. In a lecture on August 6, he holds that "All that natural men do in religion is altogether forced" within the month he tempers hope with caution, suggesting that although God often exercises his people with trials to test their resolve, it is an "awful thing" to stand apart from saving grace.3 In the last week of August, Gov. Jonathan Belcher and a committee of the General Court came to Deerfield, sixteen miles north of town, to treat with the Indians, an event, Edwards noted, that "diverted people's minds, and turned their conversation more to other affairs." Within days, Edwards had left Northampton "on a long journey for [his] health." Only one sermon survives from the next eight weeks, an October discourse that promises God will execute deserved punishment upon some sinners rather than others.4

-- 23 --

"It may be said to this town this day," he ventures in his November thanksgiving sermon, God Amongst His People,"'Cry out and shout, O Northampon, for great is the Holy One of Israel in the midst of thee.'"5 That he had earlier judged the city of Zion to be one of the "principal types" of the church and its king, David, "the most remarkable type" of Christ, enabled him to historicize his text from Isaiah, as he would at some length another verse from it several years later, and to extend it locally. He recalls the events "in this town, within this twelvemonth past," recounting how God appeared "evidently and wonderfully" how in a short time a "very great number" of all sorts converted, even "little children" how more was done in a week, at times in a particular day, than was done in a year; how young people in private societies cast off "vain and lewd" songs for anthems of praise; and how at the same time that Northampton knew God's presence, awakenings swept Windsor, York, and eastward, "without being influenced at all one by another." All is owing to God's sovereign and arbitrary will, not to "any particular conjuncture of circumstances in a town," not to piety, training, or prayers. "No, it is not for anything in us," Edwards reminds his listeners, intimating at one point that their greater guilt may have warranted their "greater privilege." To those in a Christless condition, he offers the hope of grace yet abounding; to those under the saving effects of grace, he summons the duty of praise. Of the "miserable, distressed souls" of March and early June, there is scarcely mention.

Over the next several months Edwards multiplied hope. In December it was Jonah's hope to look again upon God's temple, in January the hope of answered prayers, of God's forgiveness, of Christ's calling, of the closeness of his kingdom. On the death of his grandmother Esther Stoddard in February 1736, Edwards assured the saints of Northampton that when they leave this world for another, "their works do follow them"; at Springfield, he assured those "sensible of the need" of salvation that they have it "in their power" to gain it. In March he returned to the Jacob of Blessed Struggle and his dream of angels ascending a ladder to heaven, "which God has set on the earth for men to ascend to happiness even into heaven," an expression, Edwards makes clear in another sermon that month, of The Everlasting Love of God.6 As he explains it, the love of God to his saints had its being from eternity, "before the world was," and would continue to eternity "immutably the same." Self-moved and from his sovereign good pleasure,

-- 24 --

God's love depends on nothing temporal, nothing in the saints, a love unaffected by circumstance or condition. All his acts are acts of love to them, both before time and in it, for he created the world out of love for them, especially the "highest heaven." That the elect are assured of this rests in the unchangeable nature of God, in his truth and in his will, in his timelessness and in his Son, since Christ is the "same in substance" with the Father. And so the saints may delight in the "eternal transaction" that accorded them redemption: in God's "absolutely" free love, an act out of time and nature, they may find great comfort and "unspeakable joy."

Throughout the spring and early summer of 1736 Edwards shuttled between the promise of such joy and the reality of the unmoved, between hearts afire with "divine things" and hearts "harder than the very stones," between the knowledge that the Spirit of God effects "a thorough reformation" and the evidence that "the devil is a liar," between God the Portion of the Christian and All That Natural Men Do Is Wrong.7 In the last of these, Edwards founds his argument against justification by works not on semantics, as he had in his treatise on faith, but on questions of right and wrong, matters more of relation than definition. Since natural man is under sin, any act arising from him is "disagreeable" to God's law or contrary to it, and so nothing he does is, or can be, right. Despite that, God may "snatch" him out of sin and "translate" him to heaven, surely glory and wonder enough to convince him not to depend on his own works. Indeed, as another sermon has it, "those that trust in themselves and seek satisfaction and rest for their souls in their own strength do commonly weary themselves with many vain attempts." And, in a sermon on the James 2, the nettlesome text he confronted in Justification by Faith Alone, Edwards suggests that "a man's life and walk is a better ground" of his godliness than "any account that he gives about it in words." Still, words raised to God in psalms and hymns and those beseeching him for showers in a dry season are not only acceptable but necessary. By July the drought turned metaphysical.8

To Lament Our Declensions

Thirteen months had passed since "that very awful disaster, the death of Mr. Hawley," as Edwards phrased it now in the summer of 1736, thirteen months of spiritual malaise for Northampton, and for him a keen

-- 25 --

sense of loss at once private and public. By early November he would complete his second, longer letter to Benjamin Colman on the surprising conversions recalling the gradual withdrawal of the Spirit of God in the later part of May 1735, the suicide of "a gentleman of more than common understanding" shortly afterward, the outbreak of "strange enthusiastic delusions" in neighboring towns, the general declension (and defamation) of religion in the Connecticut River valley, and other distracting affairs: Governor Belcher's visit, Reverend Breck's ordination, the new meetinghouse, the illness that "greatly hindered" him. He would recall as well his discourse on a familiar text in which he told his congregation how its good tidings had spread to England, to the Dissenting ministers John Guyse and Isaac Watts of London, later sponsors of the first edition of Faithful Narrative;9 but he left to A City on a Hill itself his fears lest those "very eminent divines" hear anything "but what tends to confirm their good opinion." That others nearer home had taken "great notice" of what God had wrought he learned at first hand: "I have had special opportunity to know in the journey that I took the last fall" to New York, the Jerseys, and Long Island, "from one end of it to the other." There he met with people inquisitive about the wonderful work in town, ministers who had preached and published on it, others who talked much of it, and, since his return, still others who wrote asking more about it. "O, what pity is it," he cries, "but that they should hear that our fruits are answerable." Such was the gesture of his witness.

The text, made memorable in America by the first governor of Massachusetts Bay Colony, is a verse from Christ's Sermon on the Mount, "Ye are the light of the world. A city that is set on an hill cannot be hid." Near the end of A Model of Christian Charity, a lay sermon preached aboard the Arbella standing off Boston in 1630, John Winthrop pledges the Puritan settlers to a covenant with God and one another and warns of the perils of breaking it: "For we must consider that we shall be as a city upon a hill. The eyes of all people are upon us, so that if we shall deal falsely with our God in this work we have undertaken, and so cause Him to withdraw His present help from us, we shall be made a story and a by-word through the world." Since then, in addition to places in Edwards, the phrase crops up in politics and histories, even novels, its scriptural source usually cited as holy sanction for America's exceptionalism and millennial expectations.1

-- 26 --

Like other Americans of his time, Edwards held that the millennium follows the westering sun, out of Jerusalem to Rome, out of Geneva to New England. "I can't think that anything else can be intended," he remarks on Isaiah 60:9, "but America by 'the isles that are far off.'" That the biblical "new" world was discovered about the time of the Reformation; that it was a wilderness, "dry sand and barren rocks," upon which God would build a paradise; that the "Sun of righteousness has long been going down from East to West"—all these stamp America as the probable site of the millennium and the Massachusetts Bay Colony as its quarter. "And if we may suppose that this glorious work of God shall begin in any part of America, I think, if we consider the circumstances of the settlement of New England, it must needs appear the most likely of all American colonies, to be the place whence this work shall principally take its rise."2

Throughout the next two and a half years, Edwards returned to the issue again and again, even at times to its grand metaphor. In September he surveyed St. Paul's "many arguments" for perseverance, and then reduced them to the stricture of doctrine, that perseverance is "absolutely necessary" to salvation.3 But he found some converts irresolute and charged them with "falling away" from faith and obedience, guilty of insincerity, hypocrisy, and impatience. He exhorted them to "hold out to the end," to be sensible of the commandments of God, the end of life, and "the excellency of Christ." Following Persevering Faith, he preached at Lambstown, Portsmouth, and Springfield; observed days of thanksgiving, fasting, and prayer; offered communion in January and in July lectured on Christ, "the beautiful attire of saints" in August he delivered the first of a two-part quarterly lecture on divine revelation, Light in a Dark World, a Dark Heart.4 "The nations that are destitute of revelation are most of 'em

under gross darkness, and barbarous, and are very brutish in their notions and customs," he writes, "as we see in the many nations of Indians here on this continent." He vilifies Muslims and Catholics and excoriates deists. "What is the reason," he asks, "that the deists, that have been taught the principles of reason and religion, and pretend to great learning, as soon as they reject divine revelation begin to doubt, yea, absolutely to reject, 'em?" The second part of the lecture in November shifts the focus of light from the public record to private memory. He speaks now of the light in the heart that "discovers things as they are," the spiritual light that apprehends the glory and perfection of God and, through his word made manifest in Christ, the promise of redemption, "sweet, and pleasant, and refreshing." Yet though it shines at conversion and continues through life, it is a "small" light, a "dim" harbinger of the "Sun of Righteousness."

During the time between the first and second parts of the quarterly lecture, Edwards argued humiliation and "earnest diligence" and decried bundling and the sottishness of unbelief.5 In a sermon on Romans 8:22, preached in September, he charges mankind with violence upon the natural world, and in Indicting God, a fast-day sermon preached in April, upon its maker. The works of nature are God's glorious gift to man, but man satisfies only his lusts upon it, killing brute creatures by the "thousands and millions" every day, making the earth a "meat shop of sin." Yet the visible world groans under the ceaseless plunder of its riches because "God suffers it to be so," and the whole creation labors "after deliverance," flooding and shaking itself, parching the land, infecting the air. All this desolation, Edwards insists, tends to God's end in creation, namely, to banish sin from the kingdom of Christ.6 In Indicting God he contends that a covenant people in sin "really cast this reflection on God, that they have found some iniquity in him, though they don't speak in words." Because each accuses the other of breaking the covenant between them, "there is no medium in such a case: either there is iniquity in God or in them." In the second preaching unit he centers on America's exceptionalism and Northampton's. "There is probably no people in the world, whose case in the manner of our becoming a distinct covenant people, is so parallel with that of the church of Israel as this people." No less did God favor the town in the "late season" of blessing and mercy, "a city set on an hill." covenants; now they requite God his former blessings and "gentle" rebukes with false charges of iniquity, disgracing both him and themselves. Such is "our declension," Edwards rues, "from what [was] visible in us three years ago." Visible as well since then was another iniquity, a growing dissension in Northampton, not over souls but over pews.

In Endless Controversy and Dispute

Some months earlier, Edwards had addressed the theological notion of Degrees of Glory, explaining that though Christ purchased "perfect happiness for the whole body" and each part receives perfect happiness in itself, some parts receive more than others because they are set by God in a "higher place, and in a greater capacity." But pervading the discourse is a sense that logic is not quite enough to settle the matter, that despite objections and citations and figures, the issue of proportion remains largely unmet, a sign of unease, perhaps, or trial. Given the subscription wars over the new meetinghouse then under construction nearby, it seems unlikely that Edwards could convince his parishioners in February of the uneven distribution of glory or, for that matter, the need for a truce among them. And then on March 13, as he was beginning his sabbath sermon on Acts 13:41, "Behold, ye despisers, and wonder, and perish," the front gallery of the old church fell, the Word of God overcome with "dolorous shrieks and crying" and the thunder of timbers cracking.7 Somehow no one was seriously injured, a "remarkable and almost miraculous preservation," and then, on the Wednesday afternoon of thanksgiving, Edwards used his "very applicable" text of Undeserved Mercy (Ezekiel 20:21–22) to tally both the "unspeakable" mercies and "awful" rebukes visited upon its residents. The recitation grows bitter, however, and uncommonly personal when he considers the new meetinghouse and the "backward spirit" of its antagonists. That he is vulnerable to attack himself because he "bear[s] none of the charge" for construction provokes a spirited defense and admonition, for, as God's messenger, he is called upon to "reprove and warn" his people and to humble himself before him and with them, that they may accept his reproof as God's. By the end, he is left with a summer's memory of the town—" a city set on an hill," he recalls—shattered now by the reality atop Meetinghouse Hill.

Two months had passed and little had changed: "our mouths," he says

-- 29 --

in peaceable and Faithful amid Strife and Division, are still "filled with backbitings" and contention.8 He gave up on some of its people and rejoiced in others; he twice denied that he made "too much of things" and he worried that Northampton would no longer be a "place of wonders," a byword of faith "No town in America [is] so like a city set on an hill [by God], and to whom he has, in so great a degree, betrusted the honor of religion." It now "lies bleeding amongst us," wounded by discord and selfishness, but Edwards rejected as baseless that his preaching on such matters had been Prompted by others. He had flattered himself that the town would respond to its notice from abroad, but now he found himself disappointed in it no more so than in its feckless reaction to the providence of the falling gallery: "I don't know but I have trusted too much in men." And he confessed to another, far more personal failure. "I am not able to set more powerful arguments to persuade 'em than I have done to prevent and avoid contention. I have sought out acceptable words, and those which I thought most likely to take effect: I have used my utmost skill: and I have seen the ineffectualness of all."

Within weeks he preached Striving After Perfection on a particularly apt text from Psalms, "They also do no iniquity: they walk in his ways." Although the godly must live a "fighting, struggling life," a "life full of conflicts," in heaven they will be at peace, their "wearisome, long, continued warfare" ended, and they will "actually be perfectly holy." Before then, they must search a deceitful heart for sin to rid themselves of it. All is internal now, an "inward craving," inward appetite, "inward persuasion," and "inward strife," not the bickering and public contention he deplored earlier in the month. The tone is quiet, almost meditative, the metaphors rural. There is no mention of Northampton, no bitterness, no despair. Rather, the sermon seems a pastoral letter of conciliation, addressed to its author as much as the saints. By winter, the chill of his discontent returned. On December 11, four days after an earthquake, he urged Northampton "to prepare for whatever changes may come to pass in the world." In the morning of the review me, upon completion of the new meetinghouse, he suggested that "the greatest glory of an house for public worship is the praise of Christ in it."9 That afternoon, he censured its seating.

Church members had quarreled over a new meetinghouse at least since a site for it was first proposed at a town meeting early in 1733. After more

-- 30 --

than two years and "considerable debate" about need, they voted to build it, agreed to its dimensions, and appointed a committee of seven to oversee its construction, but it was another nine months and an "agitated" adjournment before they decided where to put it. By late September 1736 they had raised the frame, by early February the question of seating. Originally, the issue was the placement of pews around the meetinghouse, but by November it had matured into the separation of the sexes, and the congregation elected a seating committee of five to help resolve it. Their pastor was not a member. Within a month they had instructed the committee to assign seats "principally" by estate, then by age, and lastly by "usefulness," contrary to the order prevailing earlier in town (and elsewhere in New England) of age, service, and wealth; to allow married couples to sit together; and to reserve the front gallery for men. They appointed a committee of six to "estimate" the work of the committee of five. Before the end of the year and after "some debate," they accepted the report of the seaters, extending their mandate and their title, the "Standing Committee for Seating."1

Two months before the spire went up, Edwards had railed against those who were "either secretly gratified with having got their wills, pleased to think how they have got their wills of others, and crossed them; or grieved and vexed that they could not get their wills of others, and so grudging one at another." Now, in The Many Mansions, he chides those who "prize one seat before another in the house of worship, only because it is the pew or seat that is reckoned first in number." He recalls the deaths within the past two weeks of a young man and his father, their seats empty in a "comely" house. A witness to the young man's dying, he bears his message to the young people assembled to seek a seat in heaven, a mansion of honor in God's house hereafter, not here. "You that are pleased with your seats in this house, because you are seated high; or in a place that is looked upon honorable by those that sit round about, and because many sit behind

you; consider how short a time you will enjoy this pleasure." And then, it seems, he took to his study.

In November, Edwards had preached on Matthew 25:1, the opening verse of the parable of the ten virgins; now in December, he continued with the remaining eleven verses of it, developing a sermon series of nineteen preaching units, probably to the end of March, about the signs of grace and the means of assurance. In April he embarked on another series, this one on 1 Corinthians 13, chiefly on the first ten verses, in twenty-one units on charity, and completed it probably in October.2 He had spoken of charity before, significantly in Degrees of Glory, but at that time he had in mind charity to the poor and the rewards in this life and the next. Now, in a sustained analysis, he probed the sum of Christian love, its fruits, its spirit, practice, and end. Each of the fifteen sermons begins with a summary and prospect, and then, in standard triadic form, opens a text, explains the doctrine, and urges its application, all plainly instructive and logical, heavy with Scripture. Doctrinal concerns of the 1730s filter through, as he connects charity to the divine and supernatural light in the second sermon, to inward peace in the fourth, to imputation and justification in the thirteenth. In the first sermon, almost a year before he would undertake a series of thirty preaching units on the history of redemption, he connects charity to God's grand design in time: "The work of redemption, which the gospel declares unto us, above all things affords motives to love; for that work was the most glorious and wonderful work ever seen or thought of." In May he explicated The Terms of Prayer.3

Only their "wrong notions" of God keep some people from happiness: their unworthiness and want of "good estate" misconstrue the free and infinite grace of God; their proud spirit mistakes the "eternal dying love" of Christ. Yet when God quickens their "spiritual appetite" for the "sweetness

-- 33 --

of divine objects," their desire for happiness grows, and God readily satisfies it. Edwards exhorts the ungodly to repent and the godly to labor, not to "begrutch the pains and self-denial" required by God. Such are the ancient terms of the covenant, he reminds them, and of prayer. God, he notes in a sermon Proverbs 9:12 that month, directs us "to be friendly to ourselves."

Throughout the summer of 1738 he continued his lectures on charity and, as the occasion demanded, preached reproof and redemption. Though no sermons survive from September and October but those on charity, the remainder of the fall followed the pattern of summer. In November he called the young to account for their lack of understanding; in December he reckoned that those "advanced in years" should be "answerably advanced in their attainments in religion" and, in a general appraisal later that month, that "wicked men are inconsistent with themselves." But he also asserted that "the blood of Christ is sufficient for the perfect taking away the guilt of sin" and, in the November thanksgiving sermon, that "God is a being possessed of the most absolutely perfect happiness." "True grace in the heart," he had said in June 1737, "is a powerful thing." Now, at the end of 1738, it is "something divine." For Edwards, it was more than just a matter of words.4

The Elegance of Language

Yet almost from the start, his concern was precisely about words. On the coverleaf of a collection of notes and fragmentary essays he would later call "Natural Philosophy," Edwards recites a beginning writer's advice to himself, aged twenty: "The world will expect more modesty because of my circumstances–in America, young, etc.…Mankind are by nature proud and exceeding envious, and ever jealous of such upstarts, and it exceedingly irritates and affronts 'em to see 'em in print." So he will strive for order, clarity, and artlessness– "Let it not look as if I was much read, or conversant with books or the learned world" –and a plain style "so that the ideas shall be left naked."5 Fifteen years later, deference gone, Edwards still argues plainness. "However unable I am to preach or write politely, if I would," he writes in the preface to Five Discourses, "yet I have this to comfort me under such a defect, that God has showed us he does not

-- 34 --

need such talents in men to carry on his own work, and that he has been pleased to smile upon and bless a very plain, unfashionable way of preaching."6

Early readers found little to pardon, much less to praise, in his words. So pervasive was the nineteenth-century view that his was one of "the most remarkable specimens of bad writing" of his time that an editor of Religious Affections abridged the text to reduce its "monstrous profusion of words," and another rendered the whole of Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God in "other language." Most editors intrude without excuse, and if other works suffer less by them, the effect is still disfiguring.7 Here, for example, is a short narrative paragraph from the first edition of Ruth's Resolution as revised by such an editor (additions appear in angle brackets, deletions as strikethroughs):

Naomi was now returning out of the land of Moab, into the land of Israel, with her two daughters-in-law, Orpah and Ruth; who well ‹will› represent to us, two sorts of professors of religion;‹:› Orpah that sort, that ‹, those who› indeed make a fair profession, and seem to set out well, but dure but ‹continue only› for a while, and then turn back; Ruth that sort, that ‹, those who› are sound and sincere, and therefore are stedfast and persevering in the ‹their› way that they have set out in. Naomi, in the preceding verses, represents to theseher daughters the difficulties of their leaving their own country to go with her.8

Neither precision nor concision result from these fussy emendations, but through them the passage loses much of its integrity. The parallel biblical structure that begins the first sentence—"out of the land" and "into the land"—starts to fall apart without "that they have set out in" at the end of it, and trading "those who" for "that sort, that" all but shatters it; striking "these" in the last sentence and misreading "Well" for "will" in the first needlessly weakens the bond among the women, its very core. The manuscript has none of this.

It was when Edwards readied the sermon for Five Discourses that he expanded it, though he assured his readers that it had "but little added" since "it was first prepared and delivered."

In fact, Ruth's Resolution is almost a fifth longer in print than in manuscript, the sum of assorted changes. The Text division itself runs to some two hundred words more in print, and while many of them serve to confirm scriptural or rational evidence, others refine style. Between writing the sermon and preaching it in Northampton, Edwards made mainly cosmetic changes: "especially" for "chiefly", "the prince of the world" for "Jesus Christ," and "speaks to her" for "turns to her," deleting "in his heart" after "every true Christian." When he delivered the manuscript to Boston, the changes were significant. He reworked the end of the third paragraph to quicken Ruth's sense of loss: "land of her nativity, father and mother, and her own country and all her former possessions" becomes in print "all her natural relations, and her own country, the land of her nativity, and all her former possessions there" and then he adds Psalms 45:10 to close it, "Hearken, O daughter, and consider, and incline thine ear; forget also thine own people, and thy father's house." In the fifth paragraph he deepens her commitment with "steadfastness in her purpose" and "immoveable resolution," and, in the sixth, he heightens the consequences of her resolution with rolling biblical cadences:

and she had indeed a full reward given her, as Boaz wished; for besides immediate spiritual blessings to her own soul, and eternal rewards in

-- 36 --

another world, she was rewarded with plentiful, and prosperous outward circumstances, in the family of Boaz; and God raised up David and Solomon of her seed, and established the crown of Israel, (the people that she chose before her own people) in her posterity, and (which is much more) of her seed he raised up Jesus Christ, in whom all the families of the earth are blessed.

He spends nearly half his two hundred words to close the Text and then, continuing in the sermon, transposes several passages in the Application, deletes others, renumbers minor heads, and elaborates occasional points not found in the manuscript. Thus he provides the text (and argument) on the separation of sheep and goats (Matthew 25:32–33) to the second head in the Application; another text on the marriage of the Lamb (Revelation 19:7–9) and the context for the invitation of Moses to Hobab (Numbers 10:29) to the fourth head; and another reason for resolve to the five in the manuscript, inserting it (and another page) after the fourth head of the Doctrine in the 1738 version. If his language has "a noble Negligence" about it, as an earlier admirer claimed, it obviously did not want for care.9

Of the thirty-two sermons included in this volume, five were published in 1738, discourses Edwards revised for the press and, such was his care, corrected after publication. Five more were issued by the time of his bicentennial, each recast.1 The absence of even basic punctuation in the manuscripts, occasional lapses in citation, and sometimes curious spelling obviously merit intrusion of a kind, but all else seems a misguided effort at clarity. "Words are but wind, and are very cheap," he said in Continuing God's Presence after Hawley's death, probably remembering Job 8:2. Though cheap does not occur there or elsewhere in the Bible, it plainly complements the text, drawing it nearer New England. Hardly elegant, the language is at once scriptural and logical and pastoral, in a word, Edwards.
