DEGREES OF GLORY

Sometimes Edwards begins a sermon with a chronicle of events, often stretching over several texts before coming to the one at hand, but seldom does he cast his proof text aside entirely, as he seems to here. In Degrees of Glory he recounts the plight of the impoverished churches in Judea, Galatia, Macedonia, and Achaia and the efforts at relief, citing texts from Acts, Romans, Galatians, and I Corinthians as ancient examples of "charity to the poor"; yet the phrase turns up again only at the end of the sermon, and mention of the impoverished churches not at all. Although his text from II Corinthians broadly hints at charity, his doctrinal statement avoids it altogether—"We ought to seek high degrees of glory in heaven"—and he settles instead on the comforts of symmetry, the just correlation between work and reward. Still, seven objections and ten answers take up half the Doctrine, eighteen texts crowd the last four (manuscript) pages of the Application, and figures are invoked that degrees of glory "may be yet better understood."

Those words open "Miscellanies," no. 403, one of eight entries on degrees of glory in the series,1 and lead to an elaborate figure of union and proportion:

Now, the whole body, head and members, have communion in Christ's righteousness; they are all partakers of the benefit of it; Christ himself, the head, is rewarded for it, and every member is partaker of the benefit and reward. But it does by no means follow, that every part should equally partake of the benefit, but every part in proportion to its place and capacity: the head partakes far more than the other parts, because 'tis of a far greater capacity, and the more noble members partake of more than the inferior. As it is in a natural body that enjoys perfect health, the head and the heart and the lungs have a greater share of it, they have it more seated in them, than the hands and the feet,

-- 610 --

because they are parts of greater capacity; so in the mystical body of Christ, all the members are partakers of the benefit, of the righteousness of the head, but 'tis according to their different capacity and place they have in the body.

At the lectern, Edwards reduces the figure, trading parts: "Christ's righteousness purchased perfect happiness for the whole body; perfect happiness for the hand and for the foot, for the eye and for the head. But yet the eye or head may receive more happiness than the hand or foot, because by the sovereign pleasure of God set in [a] higher place, and in a greater capacity; for happiness needs more happiness."2 In No. 367, the earliest of the entries, he compares various capacities of the saints for happiness to "so many vessels of different sizes cast into a sea of happiness where every vessel is full," which he now renders, "If many vessels were cast into the sea, everyone would be full; but yet bigger vessels would hold more than little ones." But other figures are not held in common: those of a kind father and a little child, of little stars and the perfect luster of their light, of a "jewel of distinguished brightness" proffered by a prince to his possibly ungrateful subject, of "a prize for us to run for" are in the 1737 sermon, not the miscellanies of earlier years. Of course, Edwards borrows his figures from Scripture, the central one of the head and body, for example, from the 1 Corinthians 12, though lungs appears nowhere in it (or elsewhere in the Bible). As well, he lifts whole passages from the "Miscellanies," a standard practice: from No. 367, the argument that "Christ's purchase did not meddle with" God's determination of the saints' perfect obedience and capacity. But pervading the discourse is a sense that logic is not quite enough to settle the matter, that despite objections and citations and figures the issue of proportion remains largely unmet, signs of unease, perhaps, or trial. Given their contentious spirit, it seems even more unlikely that Edwards could convince his parishioners of degrees of glory or, for that matter, of charity to the poor.3

-- 611 --

Other than several unidentified numbered entries perpendicular to the text in the lower right-hand quadrant of the first leaf, and subsequently deleted with an "X," there is little in the manuscript of sixteen duodecimo leaves to note: another deletion of size (L. 9v.), one transposition (L. 15v.) and a few longish additions (LL. 7v., 9v., 11v., and 15v.). A shorthand sorting symbol shares the topmost line of the first page with the date "Feb. 1736, 7"; the last page is blank. There is no indication that Edwards ever used this sermon again to convince others of degrees of glory.

DEGREES OF GLORY
But this I say, He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; but he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully. 2 Corinthians 9:6

We find in the New Testament mention often made of a certain collection that was made by the churches abroad in the world for the relief of the saints in Judea; the occasion of which was a great dearth that there was in the world, which seems to have fallen heavy especially on the Christians in Judea, either because the scarcity was greater in that country, or because they suffered more through persecution, and by that means felt more of the calamity through the unkindness of their neighbors; who instead of relieving of them, and bestowing anything on them in this time of distress, rather opposed them, and took from them. The first mention we have of it is in the Acts 11, on [the] occasion of relating a prophecy that there was of it beforehand, [the] Acts 11:28–29: "And there stood up one of them named Agabus, and signified by the spirit that there should be great dearth throughout all the world: which came to pass in the days of Claudius Caesar. Then the disciples, every man according to his ability, determined to send relief unto the brethren which dwelt in Judea." And the Apostle mentioned it in his defense before Felix. Acts 24:17, "Now after many years I came to bring alms to mine own nation, and offerings." We read particularly of this collection being made in the churches that were in Galatia, and Macedonia, and Achaia. The two latter are mentioned by the Apostle in his epistle to the Romans. Romans 15:26, "For it hath pleased them of Macedonia and Achaia to make a certain contribution for the poor saints which were at Jerusalem." And there is mention of the church of Galatia in 1 Corinthians 16:1 "Now concerning the collection for the saints, as I have given order to churches of Galatia, even so do ye." And there seems to be a reference to it in the Epistle to the Galatians, chapter two, [verse] ten, "Only they would that we should remember the poor; the same which I also was forward

-- 613 --

to do." The church of Corinth was one of the churches of Achaia, and we find this collection much insisted on in both the epistles to this church. 'Tis particularly insisted, urged, and directed to in the last chapter of the First Epistle, and also throughout two chapters of this Second Epistle, viz. that wherein is our text and the foregoing. Corinth seems to have been a great and wealthy city, and their church one of the principal Christian churches that had been planted by the apostles. The Apostle is therefore much in stirring them up to bountifulness in their contributions. And among many other things he says to excite 'em to it, there is this in the text: "He which soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; but he which soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully."

In which words we may observe,

1. What is the duty4 that the Apostle here endeavors to stir them up to, viz. bountifulness to the poor, and particularly to the poor saints of Judea.

2. What kind of motive or argument is in the words insisted on, viz. the reward that they obtain by it. The thing whence he takes his argument is their own interest, and the principle he would work upon by it is a principle of self-love.

3. We may observe how the relation or connection that there is between the work and the reward, is here represented, viz. 'tis compared to that which there is between sowing and reaping. Giving of their worldly substance in charity, is here compared to the husbandman's sowing seed; as we also find it in other places in the Scriptures. When the husbandman sows his seed in the ground, he seems to throw his grain away. But he don't look upon it as thrown away, because he expects to have it again; and not only to have it again, but to have it with great increase. He casts his wheat and other grain out of his hand for the present, expecting to receive much more for it. And receiving the reward is here compared to reaping, or gathering in what was sown.

4. We may observe the proportion between the work and the reward. "He that soweth sparingly shall reap also sparingly; but he that soweth bountifully shall reap also bountifully." The husbandman, when he sows but a little ground, and scatters but a little seed, he don't expect to receive a crop so large as when he sows more seed, and seeds a larger field.

So it is with respect to our charity and liberality, both in the rewards received in this life and that which is to come, as both are promised to godliness, and especially to that part of it that consists in Christian charity to the poor. But the life to come is especially the time of rewards. The present

-- 614 --

life is the day we have appointed us to labor in; at the end of the day the laborers receive their reward. The present life is the seedtime; but the life to come is the harvest, and life everlasting is what is reaped. Galatians 6:8–9, "For he that soweth to his flesh shall of the flesh reap corruption; but he that soweth to the spirit shall of the spirit reap life everlasting. Be not weary in well doing: for in due time ye shall reap." Many that now do the work which God has given, do it with great affliction, and meet with affliction as long as they live, and do this work under a heavy cross, suffering much for Christ's sake. But "they that sow in tears shall reap in joy. He that goes forth weeping, bearing precious seed, shall doubtless come again with rejoicing" in the life that is to come.

After this life is Christ's harvest, wherein he will reap, and gather in his saints. And then is also his people's harvest, when they shall receive everyone according to their works.

DOCTRINE.

We ought to seek high degrees of glory in heaven.

Indeed every saint shall be advanced to an high degree of glory. That glory that all shall be advanced to is inconceivably great; for "eye has not seen, nor ear heard, neither have entered into the heart of man, the things which God has prepared for them that love him." 'Tis represented to a crown and kingdom of glory: 'tis called glory, honor, and immortality. And the greatness of the future glory of the saints is by Christ set forth by that [in] Matthew 13:43, "That the righteous shall shine forth as the sun in the kingdom of their Father"; which is the most glorious of all things in the visible world. And the glory that all the saints shall enter into is called an "exceeding and eternal weight of glory" (2 Corinthians 4:17).

But yet when we compare the different degrees of glory in heaven one with another, some may be said to be higher and some lower. 'Tis in this sense that I intend that we ought to seek after high degrees of glory, degrees thus comparatively high; and would treat of this subject

I. By showing that there are different degrees of glory [in] heaven.

II. By giving the reasons why we ought {to seek after them}.

[Prop.] I. There are different degrees of glory in heaven. Some saints will there be exalted higher in glory than others. This is a doctrine very

-- 615 --

fully revealed in the Scriptures: 'tis a doctrine that the Scriptures plainly teaches in our text, and in other places.

First. In the 1 Corinthians 15, where the Apostle is speaking of the glory of the saints at the resurrection, he represents the different degrees of it by the various degrees of the glory of heavenly bodies. "There is one glory of the sun, and another glory of the moon, and another glory of the stars: for one star differeth from another star in glory. So also is the resurrection of the dead" (1 Corinthians 15:41–42). The Apostle in the foregoing verse compares the difference there is between the bodies of the saints at the resurrection, and their bodies in this present life; to the difference there is between terrestrial bodies, or things we see here upon the earth, and celestial bodies; as the sun, moon, and stars, which appear abundantly more bright and glorious. There are also celestial bodies and bodies terrestrial; but the glory of the celestial is one and {the terrestrial another}. But now he observes that not only is there a great difference between celestial {bodies} and terrestrial {bodies}, but a great difference in celestial bodies themselves; meaning thereby to instruct us that not only will the resurrection bodies greatly exalt the bodies of saints in their present state, but that there will also be a great difference in glory among the resurrection bodies themselves; that some will greatly differ from others, as one star differs {from another}; as the saints are from time to time compared to stars in Scripture. And 'tis particularly said, they shall shine forth as the stars forever and ever after the resurrection in Daniel 12:2–3. "And many of them that sleep in the dust of the earth shall awake, some to everlasting life, and some to shame and everlasting contempt. And they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament; and they that turn many to righteousness as the stars forever and ever."

Second. This is also a doctrine that we are very plainly and directly taught in the parable, in [the] nineteenth of Luke, of the nobleman travelling into a far country to receive for himself a kingdom, and delivering to his servants each of them a pound; who when he returned, having received his kingdom, reckoned with the servants; and one told his lord that he had with his pound gained ten pounds, and he rewards him by making of him ruler over ten cities; and another gained five pounds, and he is rewarded {by making of him ruler over five cities}. This evidently respects the different degrees of reward that will [be] bestowed on Christ's faithful servants at his coming to judgment, according as they have been more or less profitable. For doubtless by the nobleman that travelled into {a far country} to receive {for himself a kingdom}, is meant Christ's ascending to heaven to receive an heavenly kingdom; and by his returning

-- 616 --

again to call his servants to an account, is meant his coming again to judgment.

Third. We seem to be directly taught the same thing [in] 1 Corinthians 3:14–15. The Apostle [is] speaking of teachers that build the foundation of religion, are built on Christ as their foundation; but some build gold, silver, precious stones: others, wood, hay, and stubble. He declares that both shall be saved, but one with far less advantage and reward than the other; because his works (his wood, hay, and stubble) shall be burnt up. Those works won't follow him: he will have no reward for them; as a man that escapes out his house on fire, and has all his goods burnt up, and does but just save his own life. "If any man's work abide which he hath built thereon, he shall receive a reward. If any man's work shall be burnt, he shall suffer loss: but he himself shall be saved."

Fourth. The Scriptures declare that God will hereafter reward every man according to his works; as Matthew 16:27, "For the Son of man shall come in the glory of his Father with his angels; and then he shall reward every man according to his works"; and in many other places. Now, by this we but justly understand only that Christ rewards everyone according to the quantity of his works, viz. that he will reward good to them that have done good, and evil to them that have done evil; but also that the reward will be in proportion to men's works. Thus it shall be with the wicked: their punishment will be in proportion to their wicked works; as is abundantly manifest. Thus we read, it shall be more tolerable for some of them than others at the day of judgment. And Christ signifies the different degrees of punishment in hell by the different degrees of capital punishments among the Jews, in Matthew 5:22. "Whosoever shall be angry with his brother without a cause shall be in danger of the judgment." And as wicked men shall in this sense be rewarded according to their works, viz. in proportion, so doubtless will the godly. Yea, the rewards being according to our works, our labor is expressly in this sense applied to the godly by the Apostle. 1 Corinthians 3:8, "Now he that planteth and he that watereth are one: and every man shall receive his own reward according to his own labor." And,

Fifth. Good works can't be properly rewarded at all, unless there be diverse degrees of happiness proportioned to men's works. God has abundantly promised to reward the good works of the saints in another world. Christ has said that if we do but give a cup {of cold water only, we shall in no wise lose our reward}. But how can this be, if it be so that whether they do more good works or fewer, all that have just the same reward? When a person has a good work before him to be done, how can he say with himself to encourage himself to do, "If I do it, I shall be rewarded for it; I shall

-- 617 --

in no case lose my reward"; if at the same time it be true5 that he shall have as great a reward, if he lets it alone as if he does it; and he shall have as much future happiness, if he does few good works as many? There can be no such thing as any reward at all for good works, unless they are rewarded with some additional degree of happiness. If nothing be added, then there is nothing gained. If the happiness entitled to remains just the same after the works as before, then what reward has he? If nothing be added to him for what he has done, then surely he has no reward for what he has done; for a reward denotes some addition. And there can be no promise of reward to stir him up to do it, because he'll be as well in't without doing it as with. If there is no addition of happiness, then 'tis no way for a man's future interest to strive to do good works. And if it ben't for his interest, how is he rewarded? A reward in the notion respects the interest of him that is rewarded. Again,

Sixth. This may be argued from John 14:2, "In my Father's house are many mansions."6 'Tis difficult to conceive what should be intended by this, unless by "many mansions" is meant different degrees of glory. It seems to be an allusion to a king's palace, which is great, and has many apartments of different degrees of beauty and honor. Some rooms are more excellently adorned than others, and esteemed much more honorable, and for more honorable uses; as in kings' courts, there are persons of various degrees of honor and dignity.

There are various other things that this might be argued from; as the different degrees among the angels, and the different privileges Christ admitted his disciples to when on earth, and others. But what has [been] said may suffice. Before I proceed to the next proposition, I would speak something to one or two objections.

[Obj.] 1. It may be objected that all shall be perfectly blessed, and how can any be more.

Ans. That all shall be perfect in happiness don't hinder but that there are different degrees; for all saints' perfection admits of degrees. They won't be perfect in happiness, in the sense that God is, with an absolute and infinite perfection. Their perfection will be finite. But whatsoever is finite admits of degrees; for whatsoever is finite has bounds, and that which has bounds, its bounds can be exceeded; those bounds may be either extended or shortened.

As the happiness of the saints, so their knowledge shall be perfect in its

-- 618 --

kind. 1 Corinthians 13:9–10, "For we know in part; but when that which is perfect is come, then that which is in part shall be done away." But yet it don't follow that some don't exceed others in knowledge. It don't follow that the angels, or the man Christ Jesus, won't know more than the saints. And so it is with their happiness.

The happiness of all the saints shall be perfect in two respects, viz. first, as they shall be so happy as to be perfectly free from all trouble and all evil; and, second, as everyone's capacity shall be filled with happiness, but yet the capacity may be different. Every vessel may be full, and yet some may hold more than others. If many vessels were cast into the sea, everyone would be full; but yet bigger vessels would hold more than little ones.

He that is full of happiness, he has perfection of happiness: his capacity being full, he is satisfied, and craves no more. But yet another man's perfection of happiness may exceed his.

Obj. 2. One saint deserves no more happiness than another, for all is of free grace.

Ans. Though all be from free grace, yet this is one way in which the freedom and sovereignty of grace is manifested in distinguishing some from others. The way in which God manifests his sovereignty, is by distinguishing those that desire no distinction; that the distinction should be of God, and not of ourselves; as is evident by what the Apostle says. 1 Corinthians 4:7, "Who maketh thee to differ?" 'Tis with the gifts of glory as 'tis with the gifts of grace: everyone receiving not according to his debts, but "according to the measure of the gifts of Christ" (Eph. 4.7); who distributes to "everyone severally as he will" (1 Corinthians 12:11).

And though the good works of the saints deserve no reward, yet God of his free grace has been pleased to promise a reward to them, to encourage them to diligence in his work and service; as a kind father will encourage and reward what is done by a little child, wherein it shows respect to its father, though its doings are but the doings of a little child, and are no profit to its father.

Though what the best the saints do is exceeding polluted, and deserves nothing, but if God beheld the pollution would deserve his wrath; yet for Christ's sake he beholds not the pollution, and accepts the sincerity, and testifies his acceptance by a glorious reward.

Obj. 3. How does this doctrine of different degrees of glory consist7 with heaven's being given for Christ's righteousness, and not for ours? Christ's righteousness is imputed to all, and so 'tis the same righteousness that is

-- 619 --

imputed to one as to another. If Christ's righteousness be imputed to one as well as another, why don't it merit as much for one as for another?

Ans. Christ purchased that everyone's capacity should be full of happiness; but the degree of the capacity was not determined by Christ's purchase, but was left to be determined by the sovereign pleasure of God. What Christ purchased was eternal life for all; that is, that all should be perfectly blessed, or have as much happiness as their capacity would hold. But the purchase of Christ did not decide how large the vessel should be. That is left as much still at God's arbitrary determination as before. Christ's righteousness purchased perfect happiness for the whole body; perfect happiness for the hand and for the foot, for the eye and for the head. But yet the eye or head may receive more happiness than the hand or foot, because by the sovereign pleasure of God set in [a] higher place, and in a greater capacity; for happiness needs more happiness. Christ's purchase did not meddle with that matter, which number should be in one capacity and which in another; but that is left to the arbitrary disposition [of God]. And having so left [it], God may dispense in this matter according to what rule he pleases. It has been God's pleasure to fix that matter by the proportions of men's holiness and good works.

This is evident, for what Christ did was to fulfill the covenant of works for us; but the covenant of works did not meddle with this matter. If Adam had fulfilled the law, he and all his posterity would have had perfect happiness. But God would still have been left at liberty to {dispense in this matter according to what rule he pleases}. The angels obtain eternal life by a covenant of works, whose condition is perfect abundance. All have performed the condition; but yet some are higher in glory than others, according to their several capacities. That was a thing that their perfect obedience meddled not with, but was determined according to the arbitrary pleasure of God. And if the covenant of works don't meddle with that matter, it follows that Christ's righteousness don't; for that only fulfilled the covenant of works. If Adam's perfect obedience would not have been concerned in this matter, then Christ's is not.

Obj. 4. Some may be ready to object against this doctrine, that it seems as if it would diminish the happiness of those that are in the lower degrees of glory to see so many above them.

Ans. 1. No principles will be remaining in their hearts, whence this should be any diminution of their happiness. It won't diminish, because they won't have enough; for they shall be full, and their capacity, and so their cravings, satisfied. And then there shall be no remainders of a spirit of envy. There shall be such perfect love, that through the whole society

-- 620 --

there will admit of no shadow of any such things, no remains of pride. [And then there shall be] perfect humility:8 they shall [be] fully contented in their lesser degrees of glory. As the little stars are as full of brightness according to their bigness as the greater, so shall it be in heaven. And every spiritual star there, both great and small, shall rejoice in his own perfect brightness and luster with perfect satisfaction and contentment.

{There shall be} perfect resignation. Everyone shall now [be] perfectly coincident with the will of God, and perfectly rejoicing in his will; rejoicing in that will of his in so disposing and ordering the various degrees of glory, and assigning them such a degree as he has.

2. Those that are higher in glory will be lower in humility. For as we have observed that a finite perfection of happiness admits of degrees, so does a finite perfection of holiness, and so of humility, which is a great part of creature holiness; and those that are highest in happiness, will be also in holiness. As was observed before, some will know more than others; but the more they know of God, the more will they see of their own distinction, or comparative littleness, and so will be the most humble. The more Jesus Christ, that excels9 all in happiness, as the sun excels the stars, will proportionably excel in humility, so those that1 will be lower in glory will have no temptation to envy. For though they will see those that are higher exalted above them in happiness, yet will see 'em abased below 'em in humility.

3. The seeing the superior happiness of others not only won't diminish [them], but such will be their love, that it will rather add to it. They not only will not be grieved to be {lower than them}, but so perfectly will they love them, that they will greatly rejoice in it, that they are so happy; and so shall as it were in a measure be, by love, partakers with them in the joy of their superior glory. Such a perfect harmony will there be then in the body of Christ, that they2 shall be perfectly fulfilled. 1 Corinthians 12:26, "If one member be honored, all the members rejoice with it."

Prop. II. We ought to seek high degrees of glory in heaven. This is evident by the following things.

First. We ought to seek it, because God has revealed and offered it in his Word to that end, that we might seek it. Those things that God offers we ought to seek, otherwise we shall show a contempt of the offer that God makes. It would be a great sin for us to show such a slight of any of the

-- 621 --

blessings God has offered to stir us up to our duty, as not to seek after them. It shows the wonderful grace and love of God, that he will offer such high and superior degrees of glory for such imperfect, miserable services as we perform. But if now, when God has offered them, we won't so much as seek after them, we shall show a great contempt of this wonderful grace.

If a prince holds forth a jewel of distinguished brightness to a subject to encourage him to extraordinary service, if the subject won't so much as seek after it, he offers an affront to his prince: he affronts his kindness in making such an offer. If we won't seek after that which God offers, we shall, as far as in us lies, frustrate God's end in making the offer to us. The offer is made to stir us up to our duty; but how can we be stirred up to do anything by that which we don't seek after? The Apostle by divine inspiration told the Corinthians, that they that sowed bountifully should reap bountifully, to stir them up to sow bountifully. But how could they be stirred up by this, if they would not seek after a reaping bountifully? Eminent glory in heaven [is] set up by God as a prize for us to run for; but if we refuse to run for this prize, if we refuse to seek what God offers, we shall oppose the will of God revealed in making the offer.

Second. We ought to seek high degrees of glory, because God will in many respects be most glorified in those that are highest in glory. He will be so in three respects, in a particular manner.

1. The glory of God's grace will be most manifested in them. As the glory of God's grace is manifested in bestowing happiness, so the more happiness he bestows the more abundantly does the glory of his grace appear. God will glorify his grace exceedingly upon all the saints in heaven; but his grace will be glorified most in those that partake of most of the fruits of it.

2. They that are highest in glory will be capable of rendering the largest tribute of praise. Everyone's happiness, and praises, too, will be perfect according to their capacity. But both the one and the other will be greater in those of larger capacities than in those of smaller. As the capacity of the soul is enlarged to receive, so it will be proportionably enlarged to give and render again for what is received. As those that are nearest God in glory will know most of him, and enjoy most of him, so they will render most praise to him. So that love to God as well as ourselves, ought to stir us up to seek high degrees of glory.

3. This is a duty expressly commanded. Matthew 6:19–20, "Lay not up for yourselves treasures upon earth, where moth and rust doth corrupt, and where thieves break through and steal: but lay up for yourselves treasures in heaven, where neither moth nor rust doth corrupt, and where thieves

-- 622 --

do not break through nor steal." By laying up treasure in heaven is not only meant obtain some inheritance there, but to be adding to it; as is evident by the comparison made between this and what is forbidden, viz. laying up treasure on earth. By which Christ don't mean that we should get nothing in this world, but not do as worldly-minded men do, be striving insatiably to hoard up, and keep adding to our worldly good things; but rather strive to add to our inheritance in heaven, and heap up treasure there; labor daily to increase our interest there by doing good works, and abounding in them; as appears by [the] Luke 12:33. "Sell that ye have, and give alms; provide yourselves bags which wax not old, a treasure in the heavens."

Obj. 1. If I am but certain of being satisfied with happiness in heaven, what need I desire more if I shall not then, when the time comes, desire more than I have? Though I have a lower degree, why should I desire more now?

Ans. Though the capacity of everyone will be filled, yet it don't follow but that it will be better to have a larger capacity filled than a lesser. Though everyone will be satisfied, yet those that have highest degrees of happiness will be best in't; otherwise it would not be an higher degree of happiness, if they were not better in't. Then they would not be happier; and if they were no happier then, how are they in higher degrees of happiness? Everyone's capacity will then be filled, and so everyone's cravings; but they are most happy that have the greatest capacity, and have that filled. 'Tis true, in heaven they won't desire to have a greater capacity than they have, because 'tis the will of God they should not; and they will perfectly and sweetly rest in that will of God forever. But it don't follow that we should not desire an high capacity now, when the will of God revealed concerning it is that we should desire it, and seek it.

Obj. 2. It will show a selfish spirit for me to seek high degrees of glory.

Ans. 1. Self-love is a good principle, if well-regulated. 'Tis no irregular thing for us to love our own happiness: 'tis not this that is what is properly called selfishness; but 'tis the inordinancy of self-love: it's being ungoverned that denominates a person selfish.

Self-love, when directed and regulated by the will and word of God, is a good principle. Many things are proposed in the Word of God to work upon self-love in the saints. Yea, happiness was offered to Adam in innocency to influence his self-love, and the angels and saints in heaven love themselves, yea, and the man Christ Jesus. He therefore prayed for his own happiness and glory. John 17:5, "And now, O Father, glorify me with thine own self with the glory which I had with thee before the world was."

-- 623 --

2. As has been already observed, love to God should influence us to this, in that God will be most glorified in them that are highest in glory.

Obj. 3. Will it not show a spirit of pride for us to seek high degrees of glory? Will it show a better spirit to be content with low degrees?

Ans. 'Tis no pride to seek the honor that is of God. Christ blames and condemns the Jews, that they sought the honor that was of men, and not the honor that was of God; but in the same place blames 'em, that they did not seek the honor that is of God (John 5:44). And we never find any man blamed for seeking {the honor that is of God}. Yea, desire after this kind of honor is greatly encouraged in Scripture, by such great honor being offered and promised to draw forth the exercises of such a principle. Thus God tells us that honor him, he will.

And so we are encouraged to seek glory, honor, and immortality by the promise of eternal life as the reward for it (Romans 2:7). And glory, honor, and peace is promised to them that work good (Romans 2:10). And hence Christ so often promises a crown and kingdom, and the honor of sitting with him on his throne, and judging the world, or even judging angels. Such great honor is set forth for us to seek after; and we never find any blamed for seeking such honor too much, or too much of such honor.

APPLICATION.

In an Use of Exhortation: only to exhort all to seek after high degree of glory. All, both natural men and godly men, should seek this. Those that are hitherto in a natural state and condition should seek glory, and high degrees of it; and to that end make haste, that they may get into a converted state. For such as are converted early are under greater advantages to obtain an high degree of glory than others: they are more likely to have time to grow in grace and opportunity to do more good than others.

And those that are godly especially should seek after high degrees of glory. And here I would, first, offer some things for motive to this duty, and then conclude with some things by way of direction.

[First.] For motive, consider,

1. What great pains and labors men are at to obtain high degrees of earthly good, that they may excel others, and be above their neighbors in earthly estate, and in honor; that they may make a finer show, and may be in [a] higher place, and be more accounted of. How exceedingly are men wont to lay out themselves for those things; how indefatigable in taking pains; how much exercise of care, and contrivance, and art; how many methods tried; what difficulties and labors patiently gone through. How

-- 624 --

do multitudes spend their lives in earnest pursuits of this nature. And what is it that great parts of the world are so busied in continually, day and night, compassing sea and land, leaving no stone unturned, but to get up high in the world. And if men think it thus worth the while to be of such pains to labor, and toil, and go through such difficulty for such mean, worthless things, such shadows as those that they may excel in those things, how much is it worth our while to lay out ourselves for high degrees of that eternal glory and blessedness that God has prepared in heaven; for much of those treasures, those heavenly treasures; for a crown of life fit with jewels of distinguished brightness; for mansions of peculiar glory in heaven. And how great indeed will be the folly of neglecting such things as these, when God holds 'em forth to us to run for; when we experience within ourselves so great an aptness to be eager after the high things of this vain world, to have a bigger dust heap or dung heap than our neighbors.

2. Let it be considered that the least addition to heavenly glory will be more worth than all the high things of this world put together, if we could obtain them; and that, both from the excellent nature of that glory, and also from the eternal duration of it. Those heavenly things are of such an excellent nature that they are infinitely more precious than earthly things. The least jewel in an heavenly crown is of such an heavenly excellency and brightness as to be more worth than all earthly crowns put together. The least mite of that heavenly treasure, the least dust of that gold, is of more value than all that all the treasures of princes and all the mines of the Indies contain. And then 'tis all incorruptible. Those things are vanishing things: they are but shadows: those crowns are fading crowns. But these things are incorruptible and undefiled, that fade not away. This eternal duration adds in infiniteness of value to every part of those riches and that glory, and therefore makes it infinitely exceed all earthly things.

3. Consider what advantages we are under to obtain high degrees of glory, much greater advantages than most of our fellow creatures. Most that live under the gospel have all ordinances of [the] gospel. {Most have} all with full liberty. {Most} have seen the wonderful works of God's {grace and love}. As our talents are many, so we have opportunity for the greater increase, and so to receive a greater reward. When the talent was taken from the slothful servant, his3 lord said, "Give it to him that hath ten talents. For to him that hath shall be given, and he shall have abundance" (Matthew 25:28–29). They that have many talents, and improve well to have many more, shall have the greatest reward.

-- 625 --

4. We may be as covetous as we will of high degrees of heavenly good; yea, we are commanded to covet 'em earnestly. The Apostle directs the Corinthians to covet earnestly, to excel in "spiritual gifts"4 in this world (1 Corinthians 14:1); and if so, much more may [we] earnestly covet the best gifts of glory, that are so much more worthy to be coveted and sought after. We must not hoard up for this world as covetous men do; but we are commanded to do this with respect to heavenly treasure. We may seek as high degrees of glory as we can possibly attain to, and can't be too eager in it, if we seek it in a righteous way. And what this way is, I now proceed to show.

[Second.] And in order to give direction in that way in which you ought to seek high degrees of glory, I will briefly consider what it is that degrees of glory hereafter will be proportioned to. And here, if we search the Scriptures, we shall find that future degrees of glory are proportioned to these three things.

1. Degrees of holiness in this world. Glory in another world is bestowed according to what is done in the body. [Future degrees of glory will be] in proportion to the eminency of men's graces, according to the eminency of their faith: "Cast not away your confidence, which hath great recompense of reward" (Hebrews 10:35); and according to the eminency of [their] love: "Receive a crown of life, which the Lord hath promised to them that love him" (James 1:12). And high degrees of glory are especially promised to eminency in humility. 'Tis said that he that "humbles himself shall be exalted" (Luke 14:11). And Christ tells us that he that "humbles himself as a little child, the same is greatest in the kingdom of heaven" (Matthew 18:4).

Therefore, as you would seek high degrees of glory {in heaven}, earnestly seek that you may be eminent in holiness, that you may excel in {faith, love, and humility}. And to that end, you must be diligent. You never can reasonably expect to excel without striving, wrestling, fighting, agonizing. 2 Peter 1:5–7, "Give diligence to add to your faith virtue; and to virtue knowledge; and to knowledge temperance; and to temperance patience; and to patience godliness; and to godliness brotherly kindness; and to brotherly kindness charity." You must not lose your precious time.5

And seek after humility, in a particular manner.

2. Future degrees of glory will be in proportion to persons' self-denial and sufferings they undergo for God. He that serves God, and does his duty under great temptation, and through great difficulty, and in bearing

-- 626 --

[the] cross, shall have a greater reward {in heaven}. This is abundantly revealed in Scripture. Matthew 5:10–12, "Blessed are they which are persecuted for righteousness' sake: for theirs is the kingdom of heaven. Blessed are ye, when men shall revile you, and persecute you, and shall say all manner of evil against you falsely, for my sake. Rejoice, and be exceeding glad: for great is your reward in heaven: for so persecuted they the Prophets which were before you." Matthew 19:29, "And everyone that hath forsaken houses, or brethren, or sisters, or father, or mother, or wife, or children, or lands, for my name's sake, shall receive an hundredfold, and shall inherit everlasting life." 2 Timothy 2:11–12, "It is a faithful saying: For if we be dead with him, we shall also live6 with him: if we suffer, we shall also reign with him." 1 Peter 4:13, "But rejoice, inasmuch as ye are partakers of Christ's sufferings; that, when his glory shall be revealed, ye may be glad also with exceeding joy." And many other places.

When you are backward therefore to deny yourself, shun the cross, and boggle at difficulties in religion, how much do you miss, how do you rob yourself of unspeakable glory by it. How may this consideration be enough to make Christians deny themselves with the greatest cheerfulness; yea, to do it at all times according to Christ's direction, rejoicing and being exceeding glad. How well may Christians gladly deny themselves of their appetites. {How well may they} deny their worldly interest. {How well may} their honor suffer reproach, and suffer all that comes in the way of their duty, when God has promised such glorious rewards.

3. {Future degrees of glory will be} in proportion to the good that they do. If persons have equal eminency of holiness of heaven, yet he that God is pleased to make the instrument of greater good shall have a distinguished reward; as is evident by Jeremiah 17:10, "I the Lord search the heart, I try the reins, even to give every man according to his ways, and according to the fruit of his doings." And Daniel 12:3, "They that turn many to righteousness as the stars for ever and ever." 1 Thessalonians 2:19, "Ye are my joy and crown of rejoicing." John 4:35–36, "[They are] white to harvest. [And] he that reapeth receiveth wages."

The good that persons do is twofold, either in directly glorifying God's name, or doing good to their fellow creatures. They that glorify God here, God will reward them by crowning them with glory hereafter. 1 Samuel 2:30, "They that honor me I will honor." And they that do good to others shall be rewarded, whether to their souls or bodies. They that do lay out themselves, and are the means of good to others' souls, shall have indeed the

-- 627 --

greatest reward. And therefore 'tis said that they that turn many to righteousness shall shine as the stars. But they that do good to others' outward man shall not lose their reward, especially in ways of charity to the poor. He that gives to the poor lends to the Lord; and that which he gives him, he shall pay him again (Proverbs 19:17). Christ tells us that which is done to one of the least of his brethren, he looks upon as done to him (Matthew 25:40). And his promise that not one cup of cold water given to these little ones, [shall in no wise] lose its reward (Matthew 10:42).7

And nothing is more insisted on in Scripture as a way to heap up treasure in heaven than that 'tis represented, as though what is laid out here in this way is laid up in heaven, and a most excellent treasure (Luke 12:33). 1 Timothy 6:17–19, "Charge those that are rich in this world, that they be not high-minded, nor in uncertain riches, but in the living God, who giveth us richly all things to enjoy; that they do good, that they be rich in good works, ready to distribute, willing to communicate; laying up in store for themselves a good foundation against the time to come, that they may lay hold on eternal life." Let those things stir us all up earnestly to seek that we may do good; that we may not live in vain; that we may be the instruments of God's glory, and the good of our fellow [creatures]. And,

Don't let any that owns the name of a Christian be so shamefully negligent of the reward of a Christian, as to be backward and strait-handed towards his poor brethren; but abound in deeds of charity, and do it with cheerfulness and joyfulness on all occasions, knowing that great is his reward in heaven.
