CONTINUING GOD'S PRESENCE

NOT unexpectedly, there is a falling off of rhetoric in the month or so following Joseph Hawley's suicide, "a thing of a very awful nature," Edwards calls it here, "which it is too soon to forget." Only two sermons survive the period, one in June on a text from I Corinthians, "Now all these things happened unto them for ensamples: and they are written for our admonition, upon whom the ends of the world are come" (1 Corinthians 10:11); and this in July from Ezekiel, after an admonition to the house of Israel. As befits the time, Edwards' tone is calm and reassuring; his directions suggestive and practical; his metaphors, but for the army of the saints "bear[ing] down all afore them," modest; his scroll of eight Old Testament texts to end the Doctrine full of God's "great encouragement." Much of the sermon is personal, the Application, three-quarters of the text, couched in the language of forbearance: "I would entreat" and "I would desire," "I fear" and "I hope," "I should be glad" and "I would pray you." He recalls fondly the "very great number of truly pious persons in the town, yea, for the prevailing part," the "extraordinary dispensation of providence" that befell them, and the "extraordinary things" that surrounded them: "There is perhaps no place upon the face of the earth that is so highly advanced as this town has been of late in point of privilege." To the question of the "special rebukes from heaven" recently and the possible withdrawal of God's Spirit, he tenders love and justice, the second table of the Law, a paraphrase of Matthew, a line from Micah. "Religion towards God and duty to man," he reminds them, "ought to run parallel." Against their late "great profession" of love to God and mankind, he reckons their unpaid debts to their neighbors, their parsimony to those in need, their failure to be "proportionable" in compassion to the "great alteration" in their spirit. "Words are but wind," he counters, "and are very cheap"; deeds are the stuff of religion. He alerts them to the workings of pride for the mercies bestowed upon them (and the lack of sufficient gravity in their talk about it), and recommends secret prayer to keep God's Spirit from departing the town, "days of prayer" or "parts of days."

Once again, he sanctions private religious societies as a suitable means for prayer and reflection, but rather than restrict them to young men as

-- 391 --

he had a year and a half earlier, he now suggests that "both sexes and all ages do meet together" as well as apart, citing Acts 16:13 to justify, if somewhat tentatively, societies of women, especially young unmarried women: "There seems to be some account in Scripture of a society of women meeting together by themselves to pray to God." Edwards is convinced that a sort of spiritual contagion spreads in such companies, much as the recent "stir" and sudden conversions in town and elsewhere "affected and moved" others to salvation, and that godly neighbors may yet stir the ungodly with advice and example, and elders the young, and parents their children though always, he cautions, with discretion and without compromising his own appointed calling. That "the tokens of God's presence may be continued," he counsels a broad, reciprocal accountability: the unconverted "to lay in with some person" of conviction to "watch over" them, and the converted, in turn, to keep "a watchful eye" on the unconverted, as well as on themselves and one another, saint on saint. With that kind of Christian charity abroad in the town, Edwards ends bravely, "I dare be bold to say God won't forsake us."

* * *

At twenty-five duodecimo leaves, the manuscript is second only in length in this volume to Undeserved Mercy, an unpublished sermon occasioned by the fall of the meetinghouse gallery on March 13, 1737, and a work, incidentally, based on another hopeful verse in Ezekiel. Here, the first of two preaching units ends with the proposal for women's religious societies and the prospect of "some other things at another season," in all probability the directions for a community of beneficence included in the second preaching unit. That perhaps none of them found their way into later sermons is implied by the absence of repreaching signs or symbols in the text. Indeed, there is hardly anything remarkable in the appearance of its many pages: the date, July 1735, is in its usual place above the citation (L. 1r.); there is a single transposed passage (L. 12v.), two interlineations in a reduced hand (LL. 5v. and 20v.), three partially filled pages (LL. 12v., 13r., and 25r.), any number of routine deletions of words and phrases, and a mixed bag of indefinite articles and aspirated hs: "a happy" and "an happy" (LL. 1v. and 2r.), "a humble" and "an humble" (LL. 10r. and 21v.), "a hand" and "an hand" (L. 17v.). More notable is a long (and caustic) deleted passage on sleeping during public worship (LL. 22r.–22v.) later replaced by a short (and temperate) version (L. 23r.).

CONTINUING GOD'S PRESENCE
But I have gathered them unto their own land, and have left none of them any more there. Neither will I hide my face any more from them: for I have poured out my Spirit upon the house of Israel, saith the Lord God. Ezekiel 39:28–29

THE Prophet in the context is speaking of the redemption of Israel from the countries where they had been carried captive. "Therefore thus saith the Lord God: Now will I bring again the captivity of Jacob, and have mercy upon the whole house of Israel, and will be jealous for my holy name" (Ezekiel 39:25). This promise of their return from their captivity, is attended with several other gracious promises that are continued in the text.

1. That God would at that time pour out his Spirit upon them: "For I have poured out my Spirit upon the house of Israel." They were carried into captivity because of their sins, for their ignorance of God, and departing from him, and rebelling against him. "And the heathen shall know that the house of Israel went into captivity for their iniquity: because they trespassed against me, therefore hid I my face from them, and gave them into the hand of their enemies: so fell they all by the sword. According to their uncleanness and according to their transgressions have I done unto them, and hid my face from them. After that they have borne their shame, and all their trespasses whereby they have trespassed against me, when they dwelt safely in their land, and none made them afraid" (Ezekiel 39:23–24, Ezekiel 39:26). Therefore 'tis foretold that when he brings them again from captivity, he will pour out his Spirit upon them to cleanse them from their sins, and to reveal himself unto them, that they may worship and serve him, and no more worship idols. This is the promised fruit of God's pouring out of his Spirit. "So the house of Israel shall know that I am the Lord their God from that day and forward" (Ezekiel 39:22). "Then shall they know that I am the Lord their God, which caused them to be led into captivity among the heathen: but I have gathered them unto their own land, and have left none of them any more there" (Ezekiel 39:28). And then it is promised that for this reason:

-- 393 --

2. [That God] would not leave any of them any more in the land of their captivity. "But I have gathered them unto their own land, and have left none of them any more there."

Two things are here implied: first, that when he did gather them out of their captivity, he would gather them all; and, second, that when they were brought out of captivity, they should return into captivity no more, and that is promised as a fruit of his pouring out his Spirit upon them. And also

3. That he would not hide his face any more from them; i.e. he would no more take away from them the tokens of his presence with them, and his mercy and favor towards them. And this God promises to accomplish, because he had poured out his Spirit: "For I have poured out my Spirit."

DOCTRINE.

When God pours his Spirit upon a people, 'tis a thing greatly to be desired that God would continue the tokens of his presence and mercy amongst them, and no more depart from them.

Prop. I. When God pours out his Spirit on a people, there are many blessed tokens of his presence and mercy amongst them. God is present everywhere,1 for he fills heaven and earth; but the way in which he is especially present with his people, is by his Spirit. When he pours out his Spirit on a people, he does as it were leave the heavens, and come down and dwells. And then there are many glorious tokens of his favor and mercy to be seen amongst them: it makes a most happy and blessed alteration amongst a people. When God is thus amongst a people, he brings salvation with him.

There is at such a time a happy and blessed alteration made in many persons. Sinners are reformed of their wicked practices; and not only so, but many are savingly changed. They are turned from darkness unto light, and from the power of Satan unto God. Poor captived2 souls are delivered and rescued from Satan's cruel bondage. 'Tis a time of redemption out of captivity: 'tis a joyful time amongst poor enslaved captives, when there comes a time of redemption, that they may return again to their own land. A time of the pouring out of God's Spirit, is a time wherein Satan's kingdom

-- 394 --

falls, and the kingdom of Christ is erected: Christ rides triumphant amongst them in the chariot of salvation. Such a time is a time wherein there is an happy alteration in many persons to the appearance of the world. Many that used to be vicious persons, profane persons, or sinful persons, or contentious persons; that used to behave themselves very unbecomingly, and do a great deal of mischief, and were a nuisance where they lived; they become virtuous, religious, and pious persons, and become blessing persons. That is an happy time, when "the wolf dwells with the lamb, and the leopard shall lie down with the kid." Such a time is a time, wherein a spirit of love and peace prevails amongst a people, which renders an happy time. An end is put in a great measure to a spirit of contention and strife, whispering, backbiting, and revenge.

Such a time is a time, wherein salvation comes into many houses; and there is great cause given for the voice of joy and praise in many families. Such a time is a time, wherein are the tokens of God's presence and blessing with ordinances. At another time, [the] Word {and ordinances are} preached in vain. {At another time}, ministers study and labor from week to week to but little purpose; {they} sow much, but bring in little; {there are} but little fruits to be seen. {At another time}, men give the Word a hearing, but go on careless and {senseless}, as they used to do: the fisherman toils all night and catches nothing. But at such a time as when God pours {out his Spirit}, the Word and ordinances have some good effect: the husbandman sees the fruit of his labor.

[At such a time, there are more of the] tokens of God's presence on sabbaths. Sabbaths are like sabbaths, prized {amongst a people}, precious days. {Sabbaths} are kept in some measure, as sabbaths ought to be {amongst a people}: and at such a time, a sabbath's blessing is given to many on sabbath days.

[At such a time, there are more of the] tokens of God's presence in public assemblies. At another time, there is indeed a form of public worship; but there is a great appearance of dullness and lifelessness. {At another time}, many seem not to regard what they are about. But at such a time, God is worshipped more in the beauty of holiness.

At such [a] time, there are more of the tokens of God's presence in private families and companies. Whereas at other times, the conversation is chiefly about things that are vain and unprofitable, now it is chiefly of the things of God and religion. Such a time is a joyful time in a town, as 'tis said when there was a great pouring out of the Spirit of God in the city of Samaria. Acts 8:8, "[And there was] great joy in that city."

Such a time as this, seems to be intended in Canticles 2:10–13. "My beloved

-- 395 --

spake, and said unto me, Rise up, my love, my fair one, and come away. For, lo, the winter is past, the rain is over and gone; the flowers appear on the earth; the time of the singing of birds is come, and the voice of the turtle is heard in our land; the fig tree putteth forth her green figs, and the vines with the tender grape give a good smell. Arise, my love, my fair one, and come away."

[Prop.] II. When God gives a people such a privilege and happiness, 'tis greatly to be desired that God would continue the tokens of his presence and mercy amongst them, and no more depart from them. Indeed God is greatly to be praised for even granting such mercy to a people, though it be for never so short a time. But yet 'tis a melancholy thing, after God has thus appeared amongst a people, and given such blessed tokens of his presence and favor, to have him withdraw and hide his face from [them].

So much the more excellent is the mercy, so much the more sorrowful and melancholy is the withdrawment. But to be more particular in the reasons of it,

First. This is the greatest blessing that a people can enjoy, when God is pleased to pour out his Spirit on a people, and therein to give tokens of his mercy and gracious presence. It tends to make a people an happy people above all other things. 'Tis a great mercy to have health among a people. But the pouring out of God's Spirit, tends much more to their happiness than that; for this tends to the health of their souls: a people with healthy bodies and sickly souls, are but a miserable people. [It] tends more to a people's happiness than outward peace. 'Tis a great mercy to have peace in our borders: [to] go out and in, and have none to make 'em afraid; [to] lie down safely. But this tends to peace with God. If we are at peace with man, what will it avail us, as long as God is our enemy? If we have no earthly enemies around us, when we lie down to sleep, what can that avail us, if sin is lying at the door? [It tends more to a people's happiness] than outward plenty. Then God gives food for a people's souls, the bread of life which came down from [heaven], that a man may eat thereof, and live forever. Then is given not the meat that perishes, but the meat that endures unto everlasting life.

At such a time, God bestows on a people spiritual and heavenly riches, which makes 'em a more happy people, than if they flourished in the greatest outward wealth. [They] are received, many of them, to be the children of God, which is much better than if they were made earthly Princes.

They are the happiest of all men, that they may be poor in this world, that are "rich in faith," heirs of the heavenly kingdom (James 2:5).

-- 396 --

Second. This is a blessing that a people do, above all others, stand in need of. They stand in need of other things for the body; but they stand in need of this for their precious and immortal souls. Without it, many will perish forever; they will [go] down into the pit of everlasting misery.

Without God's Holy Spirit, and his gracious presence, all other mercies will be in vain to them. They will do them no good. If God gives 'em aplenty of worldly good things—if they have health, if they have peace, if they have wealth, and flourish now so much in outward respects—it will avail them nothing. It will do them no good without this: yea, without this, all other blessings will prove curses, and will only work together for their ruin and destruction. Psalms 92:7, "When the wicked do grow as the grass, and all the [workers of iniquity do flourish; it is that they shall be destroyed for ever]." And therefore if God has poured out his Spirit on a people, and afterward withdraws from them, it is the greatest loss that they can sustain.

Third. A people don't only stand in need of enjoying this blessing for a time, but they need it at all times. They don't only need to have it once bestowed, but they need to have it continued. They need to have God always thus dwelling among them. They need it for the carrying on the work of conversion. If God withdraws, there are many will be like to perish eternally. And they stand in continual need of it for the upholding and building up of saints; for the saints are every moment dependent on the Spirit of God for all spiritual good, and can't do without it. The once giving, the Spirit of God won't answer the necessities of a people, but they need to have it continued. The fruits of the earth need the influences of the visible heaven, showers and shines of the sun, from day to day, in order to their growing and flourishing, and indeed in order to their [being] kept alive: 'tis not sufficient that the sun should shine till the seed is come up; but the plant stands in need of the sun still as much as it did before, to keep it alive, and to cause it to grow and bring forth fruit, and to bring it to perfection.

And if God, when he bestows such a mercy on a people, should continue it; if he, when he pours out his Spirit on them, should no more withdraw; how happy indeed would that render a people. This would exalt a people to an high pitch of blessedness. Indeed happy would that people be that should be in such a case: it might be said concerning 'em, "What people is like unto this people that hath God so nigh to them?" And yet,

Fourth. This is not too great a blessing to be sought. Though it be a blessing that would render a people exceeding happy, yet this [is] not a happiness too great for a people to desire and seek. So merciful is God that those blessings that are the greatest blessings, and tend most to our happiness,

-- 397 --

are those that we have the greatest encouragement to seek of any. We have more encouragement given us in the Word of God to seek the blessing of the Holy Spirit than any other mercy whatsoever. Luke 11:11–13, "If a son shall ask bread of any of you that is a father, will he give him a stone? or if he ask a fish, will he for a fish give him a serpent? or if he shall ask an egg, will he offer him a scorpion? If ye then, being evil, know how to give good gifts unto your children: how much more shall your heavenly Father give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?"

We may, therefore, doubtless seek not only the bestowment of it, but the continuance of it We are allowed to seek the continuance of outward mercies. Thus we are directed to seek the continuance of our outward food, and to pray that God would give us day by day our daily bread. But we stand in more need of the continuance of our spiritual food, of having that bestowed from day to day, and much more than our outward food. 'Tis true that when such a blessing is bestowed remarkably on a people, 'tis ordinarily but of short continuance. But yet that is no argument that we ought not to seek, or that it is too great a happiness for a people to desire, or ask of God. For God has given great encouragement in his Word that God will dwell amongst his people, and not forsake them; that he will not only give remarkable tokens of his presence, but continue them. This is promised in the text, and Deuteronomy 4:31, "For the Lord thy God is a merciful God; he will not forsake thee"; 1 Samuel 12:22, "For the Lord will not forsake his people for his great name's sake, because it hath pleased the Lord to make you his people"; 1 Kings 6:13, "And I will dwell among the children of Israel, and will not forsake my people Israel"; and Isaiah 42:1–4, "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my Spirit upon him: he shall bring forth judgment to the Gentiles. He shall not cry, nor lift up, nor cause his voice to be heard in the street. A bruised reed shall he not break, and the smoking flax shall he not quench: he shall bring forth judgment unto truth. He shall not fail nor be discouraged, till he have set judgment in the earth: and the isles shall wait for his law"; and Ezekiel 34:24–25, "And I the Lord will be their God, and my servant David a prince among them; I the Lord have spoken it. And I will make with them a covenant of peace, and will cause the evil beasts to cease out of the land: and they shall dwell safely in the wilderness, and sleep in the woods"; and Isaiah 62:4, "Thou shalt no more be termed forsaken; neither shall thy land any more be termed desolate: but thou shalt be called Hephzibah, and thy land Beulah: for the Lord delighteth in thee, and thy land shall be married." And when God has poured out his Spirit, we make use of it as an argument. 'Tis God's own work, and we may

-- 398 --

therefore plead, as Psalms 138:8, "Forsake not the works of thine own hands"; [and] may plead as Moses did, Deuteronomy 3:24, "For what God is there in heaven or in earth, that can do according to thy works, and according to thy might?" God in the text mentions this as a reason why {he poured out his Spirit}: "For I have poured out past mercies."

APPLICATION.

The Use that I would make of this doctrine, is to put us of this town upon Inquiry, what our duty is, or what we have to do, that God may not withdraw from us. God has of late very wonderfully appeared amongst us, and many glorious effects have there been of his presence with us. But we still need the continuance of this blessing, and it will be a melancholy thing, if God should withdraw. 'Tis not in our power of ourselves to uphold that religious concern that has lately been carried on amongst us. We have no more power to keep the Spirit of God of ourselves, than to get it before 'tis given; as we, with all that we can do, can't cause a shower of rain in a time of drought, or continue a shower, when it comes over us.

But yet without doubt, there is some duty incumbent on us, something that 'tis proper for us to do, that the pouring out of God's Spirit may be continued amongst us. Though the continuing of the influences of God's Spirit amongst us, is what we can't effect of ourselves, nor have we anything to do under that notion, yet there may [be], and doubtless are, many things that we have to do, as a proper and suitable way of seeking of it of God. And here I would mention several things that it may be proper for us to do, to which I would entreat the diligent attention of the congregation; for [it is] of the greatest importance that we should have the continuance of the presence of God amongst us; and we should therefore gladly attend to any directions to that end, and to use all means proper in order to it. And if we do so, there is great reason to hope that the Spirit of God will not withdraw from us. And here,

[Use] I. Let us inquire, and make diligent search, what there is to be found amongst us that might provoke God to withdraw from us. It is to be hoped that many things that were provoking to God among us, have lately been reformed; and that the town has, through the influence of God's Spirit, in a great measure been purged of them. But we should diligently inquire, whether there mayn't be still remaining amongst us, those things that are offensive, and whence there may be danger of God's being provoked to withdraw.

God is an all-seeing God. His eye searches all things; and he is of pure

-- 399 --

eyes that cannot behold iniquity. Is there nothing remaining amongst us that we may be charged as a people, as being too general, or too much allowed amongst us, that God is offended with, and which there is danger may provoke him to depart from us? I would entreat every one of God's people, to take this matter into their consideration, and to make diligent inquiry.

We must take heed that we don't entertain too high an opinion of ourselves, and begin to think, because there has been so much of an appearance of religion lately amongst us, that therefore we are now pure, and that God now finds nothing in us to charge us with.

We have reason, upon several accounts, to be searching; for we han't been without special rebukes from heaven of late. There has [been] a thing of a very awful nature fallen out in the town, which it is too soon to forget;3 and God has been pleased to let Satan loose on several, so as very strongly to tempt and buffet them. And we have reason to see whether or no there be not some degree of withdrawing of the influences of God's Spirit from amongst us. These things should put us upon searching ourselves.

Let us see whether or no there has not been some notable defect amongst us that we have too much o'erlooked, and han't duly considered of; and han't there been, some way or other, some misimprovement of the great mercies that God has lately bestowed upon us. An ill improvement of great mercies brings great wrath. We are corrupt creatures and exceeding prone, even in the best of times, and under the best advantages, still some way or other, to swerve and corrupt our ways before God. I desire that persons would make diligent search, with respect to everything; but I would mention two things in particular.

1. Let it be inquired, whether or no the flourishing of that part of Christianity that respects our duty to men, keeps pace with our profession of religion towards God.

There is a great profession of religion, as it respects God amongst [us]. There is a great profession of knowledge of God; of seeing the excellency and sufficiency of Christ; of being humbled before God; of trusting in Christ; of love to God, and love to Christ; and of a great sense of the dying love of Christ; and other such things. There are great numbers that make professions of such things: there is a very great and remarkable alteration in the town lately in this respect. But is there as great an alteration, with

-- 400 --

respect to duties of the second table of the Law, that respects our duty to our neighbor? In times past, without doubt, there was a great defect in many respects here; as, for instance, before the beginning of this stir, there was a great defect in many with respect to paying their debts. Many would lie in debt to their neighbor from year to year, and say nothing to him about it; [and] would neither pay him, nor go to him to give a reason, why he did not, or could not, and desire his forbearance.

Is there a remarkable alteration in this respect, since the Spirit of God has been so wonderfully poured, in proportion to the profession of religion in other things? Don't some that make profession of a work of conversion, still live in wrong towards their neighbor in this respect? Don't the old debts of some of 'em that have lain some years, still lie unpaid; and you have never taken any care of them? And let it also be inquired, whether a love and charity to our neighbor, has flourished amongst us, in proportion to the profession of religion amongst us.

Are we become so much the more kind and openhearted one to another, concerned for one another under want, ready to distribute, willing to communicate, ready to bear one another's burdens, and so to fulfill the law of Christ? Has there been a proportionable flourishing of liberality amongst us, towards those in the town that are in want?

There has been lately amongst us, a great deal of the profession of contempt of worldly enjoyments, and of love to mankind. One and another tells how they find that they love everybody, and how they have the world under their feet. Does this proportionably appear in distributing to the supply of others' wants?

Do we appear free to part with those worldly enjoyments that we profess such contempt of, for the good of our neighbor that we profess such love to? To pretend to have the world under our feet, and to have such great contempt of the worldly enjoyments, and yet to gripe,4 and keep it close, and very loth to part with any of it for our neighbor that needs it, hardly seems to agree well together.

If we profess that we have a spirit of love to everybody, we should show it in what we do. We should not love only in word and in tongue, but in deed and in truth.

Religion towards God and duty to man, ought to run parallel: six of the Ten Commandments respect our duty to man. There should be a proportion kept up: when things are proportionable, then they are beautiful, and most acceptable to God. 'Tis beautiful to see the parts of the body

-- 401 --

of a man proportionable one to another: it is not lovely to see a great head, and poor withered body and limbs.

Virtue to our neighbor, is often in Scripture mentioned as the evidence of the truth of our religion to God. 1 John 4:21, "He that loves God should love his brother also." They are mentioned together in Scripture.5 It is said, to love God with all our heart, and with all our soul, is the great commandment of the law. And that the second is like unto it: to love our neighbor as ourselves. God very much insists on our duty to our neighbor. Micah 6:8, "What doth the Lord thy God require of thee [but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God]?"

God has doubtless expected that there should be a great alteration amongst us in these respects. God has remarkably poured out his Spirit. God expected that we should make this improvement of it, to reform all that is wanting in these respects. God might well expect that we should be an eminent people, with respect to duties of justice and charity; that we should be renowned in this respect. Let us inquire, whether there has been such an alteration as is proportionable. There has been a great alteration, with respect to a profession of duty to God. God is very much offended, when the religion of [a] people seems to be partial, or when [one] part of duty seems to be regarded, and another neglected. Hosea 7:8, "Ephraim is a cake not turned"; i.e. half roasted, and half raw; one side only was baked.

Let us inquire, whether or no that ben't too much the case with us. We seem to be very religious in one part of our duty, viz. with respect to experiences towards God. But ben't we in some measure like a cake that is not turned, that has but one side baked?

If it be so, without doubt, God is offended by it; and we han't made that improvement of the pouring out of his Spirit, as he has expected. And let us amend what is amiss in this respect, that God may not be offended and provoked through such a neglect and misimprovement to take away his Holy Spirit from us.

2. Let us inquire, whether or no we don't offend God by being too much lifted up with the things that God has lately done for us. Our hearts should be exalted, and lifted up in praise; but we had need to take heed, lest they should be lifted up with pride. Let it be inquired, whether or no we han't been lifted up and been proud of the great and distinguishing mercies that he has bestowed on the town. Han't we been ready to exalt ourselves

-- 402 --

above our neighbor, and to take some of the glory of it to ourselves? If So, God may justly take away these distinguishing mercies that we are proud of. And let particular persons inquire, whether or no they han't been guilty of spiritual pride; whether they han't been proud of the things God has done for them. Han't we been proud of our experiences?

Let no man amongst us think himself so good, as that he is out of danger of being proud of his goodness. It is true, it is not the spirit of a saint, as a saint, to be proud of his goodness. But yet a saint has so much corruption left in him, that he had need to be exceeding watchful, lest he be proud even of his godliness. And persons should be especially watchful, lest pride should work, when they are telling of their own experiences. Let no man think himself out of danger in such a case. There is abundance of pride, even in the hearts of converted persons; and they that think themselves most out of danger, are really most in danger.

Let everyone inquire, whether or no he han't been guilty sometimes of telling of what he has met with, when his aim has been in a great measure his own honor. Han't you been secretly proud that you have had such a good story to tell? Han't you been lifted up, when you have been telling, and you see that others, that set round you and heard, thought well of it, and seemed to admire, and to think that you had met with great things, and with greater things than they? Han't this fed and nourished your corruption? And han't some others, when they have set in companies, and have been telling their experiences, found it hurt your pride, because your story was not so good as others. And han't you for your own honor, tried to set it out, and make better out [of it] than it was? You had need to search narrowly, and be exceeding strict; for there is no corruption so secret in its ways of working as pride: no lust works in such hidden ways. Many have abundance of the workings of this corruption, and ben't sensible of it; and often those that have most of it, are least sensible. There has been, without doubt, through the great and sovereign mercy of God, much of Christian experience amongst us; but has not there been too much of an ill improvement of it this way, viz. to be proud of it? Let everyone search, and if it has been so, let this thing be reformed, lest God be provoked by [it], to take away his Holy Spirit from us, that we, instead of praising him for [it], take the glory of [it] to ourselves. Let there be no appearance of any such thing, either in an overforwardness in telling of our own experiences, or talking of them without a suitable manifestation of a sense our unworthiness. Let those that have had gracious experiences rejoice; but let it be with a humble joy, with brokenheartedness, and poverty of spirit. And when those that have lately been converted amongst

-- 403 --

us, get together, and are talking together of things of religion, let it not be with an air of lightness. I fear that some have been guilty of an error in this respect. When they [have] been together, they have talked of the things of religion with too little gravity.

They may rejoice and be cheerful, and ought to be, but the direction is to "rejoice with trembling" (Psalms 2:11). The meaning is not that they should tremble for fear what their condition is, but that [they] should rejoice with a reverential trembling, that they should be [in] a holy awe and reverence of God, and the great things of God. At the times that persons rejoice in God, a light, airy joy is not a becoming joy for us to rejoice in God with.

That is the best joy, the sweetest joy, and most to God's honor, that is the most humble joy that is accompanied with brokenness of heart. A Christian should rejoice, but yet he should be a mourner, too. He should mourn for sin all his days, and always lie in the dust. And there ought to be no such appearance of lightness in Christians' talk together that seems unsuitable to such a spirit as this.

And [a] light spirit of cheerfulness, when talking of things of religion, don't honor religion. It rather gives offense, and prejudices others against it, and therefore should carefully be avoided.

Second. The second thing I would mention, as a proper thing for us to attend that the Spirit of God may not leave us, is prayer. This will be a proper acknowledgment of God as the only author of this mercy. As [God] expects to be inquired of, and sought to, for the bestowment of such a mercy, so likewise for the continuance of it. And besides prayer for such a mercy of God in secret, and in our families, and in our ordinary public worship, I should think it would be a fit and suitable thing that, at sometimes, days of prayer should be kept by private societies and, at other times, parts of days, to beg of God the continuance of his presence amongst us. But in our prayers we should be sure not to forget to bless God for what we have received.

We may not only hope hereby to obtain this, as God is a prayer-hearing God, and so to obtain it as an answer of prayer; but the keeping up of such religious meetings will [have a] tendency to this, as a means to influence minds of those that attend them. There is this benefit in private religious meetings, that that religion has not so much the appearance of a thing neglected. By thus joining together for a religious purpose, one influences another. If persons attend on religion only just when it is according to a fixed rule, when all look upon themselves in ordinary cases bound to attend, and it would be a disgrace not to attend it, religion be not altogether that honor; as when persons, besides the stated seasons of

-- 404 --

public worship, do also attend it, sometimes when 'tis more voluntary, as a thing of their own notion.

And I should think it advisable not only that there should be sometimes private meetings, wherein both sexes and all ages do meet together; but that there should be also other religious societies, wherein persons of a particular sex and age are associated by themselves, to seek and wait upon their Creator; as young men by themselves, and young women by themselves. It has a tendency to have influence on the minds of persons, to join with others in religious performances; but tends yet in some respects to have a further influence: to be from time to time engaged in the actual exercise of social religion, and to bear their part, with others of the society, in leading in the performance, and to be sometimes with those only of the same sort. Religion hereby has the face of a thing more belonging to them, and that they are more nearly concerned in, and that they highly prize, and are not ashamed of. Religion seems to be more their act, and one stirs up another more. 'Tis a thing desirable that all sorts, not only men, but women, should be thus associated.

Persons are wont to associate themselves into societies and different companies in temporal concerns; not only in public societies, where all sorts are together, but each rank and sort with those of their own sort. And why is it not also becoming that it should be so in spiritual affairs?

I hope by what I have heard, and would be thankful for it, that the young men in the town, are already most of them associated in religious companies. I would advise that the young women of the town, would also meet by themselves at times for like purposes. I should be glad, if all the young unmarried women that are capable of it, throughout the whole town, were associated in private societies for the carrying on of the worship of their Creator by themselves. It may be God will smile upon them in it, and it may be a good means to contribute to the upholding a spirit of religion amongst us. Religion in the social exercise of it, and to be leading in the performance, is no less becoming of those of the female sex, when they are by themselves, than those of the male sex. Till when all sorts are together, it most properly belongs to those of the male sex.

There seems to be some account in Scripture of a society of women meeting together by themselves to pray to God. Acts 16:13, "And on the sabbath we went out of the city by a river side, where prayer was wont to be made; and we sat down, and spake unto the women which resorted thither." And they were greatly smiled on of God in it; for there at one of their meetings, Lydia, one of those praying women, met with her conversion; as it follows, "And a certain woman named Lydia, a seller of purple,

-- 405 --

of the city of Thyatira, which worshipped God, heard us: whose heart the Lord opened, that she attended unto the things which were spoken of Paul" (Acts 16:14).

'Tis a thing desirable that all sorts and both sexes, both male and female, should be trained up in the exercise of social religion; not only as joining in the exercises performed by others, but also as performing themselves.

I would mention some other things at another season, if God gives opportunity.6

Third. I will proceed to inquire particularly, what may be proper to be done—first, by the unconverted; and, secondly, by those that are esteemed godly amongst us—that so God may not withdraw from the town.

1. What may be proper to be done by those that are yet in a Christless state and condition. And the direction that I would give them, shall only be with respect to themselves, what they should do that the Spirit of God may not depart from them. The generality of the natural persons in the town, have lately been in some measure awakened, and have had a degree of the strivings of the Spirit of God; and if the Spirit of God should withdraw from us, it will appear in their growing senseless and careless again. There are three things that I would at this time advise such persons to, that the Spirit of God may not depart from them.

(1) Great watchfulness and striving against the beginnings of a senseless, careless spirit. There is great danger that those natural persons, that have lately been under convictions, will now by degrees lose their conviction. The present time is a time wherein they are exceedingly exposed to it.

For now the novelty [of] that extraordinary dispensation of providence, that has lately been amongst us, is past. The newness of anything that is extraordinary, makes it abundantly the more affecting; and that because, while it is new, it is strange. What is strange and unusual of any kind, will affect persons abundantly more than other things. Hence, when any new or unusual thing happens, people's minds are struck with it, and that for a while is all the talk. 'Tis so in temporal affairs.

And so it has been in the present affair. While this stir and concern was new amongst us, people were greatly affected by it: one affected and moved another. It was a very strange and unusual thing, and this made everyone take great notice of it. They could not but think of it, and could

-- 406 --

not but speak of it. It was partly the natural effect of the extraordinariness and newness of the thing, to make it the common subject of talk and conversation in all companies.

People at first were much affected with the conversion of others: to hear of the conversion of one person a little while ago, much affected many persons; to have two or three converted in a week, it was taken great notice of. And for a considerable time there was still something new to move and affect people. When the stir first began, it began in an extraordinary manner, and made swift progress. This greatly affected persons, and tended to increase the progress of it still more. There were some very sudden conversions, and that was a new and strange thing, and that affected persons. After a little time the concern became almost universal, and that was new and affecting.

And there were many aged persons, and many vain and careless livers, and little children converted: and those were strange and unusual things, and so were the more affecting. And many converts, when first converted, were full of joy and comfort, and that also was new and affecting. And after a while the Spirit of God began to be poured out upon other towns, upon one town after another, and to spread wider and wider; and this was a very unusual thing, and so tended to affect people. Thus for a great while, we had still some very new and strange things to affect us.

But now those things begin to grow old: we are used to 'em: they don't seem so strange. And the talk about them begins to cease, and conversation about other things begins to prevail.

And now therefore it is a time of exceeding great danger. There is great danger that many that have been awakened, will by degrees cease to be affected as they have been, and that they will lose their convictions. It will be a difficult thing for persons to retain that moving sense they had of things. They will be exceeding apt to wear off, and die away.

There is great danger that their convictions will go away insensibly; that the distemper will gradually steal upon them, till it overcomes them, and undoes them. And 'tis to be feared that the danger will grow greater and greater. For, as in the beginning of this stir, one's being awakened, awakened others; so now one's growing senseless and secure, will tend to make others so. 'Tis to be feared that the talk will grow less and less about the things of religion, and talk about worldly things, and about vanity, will grow more and more; and so that a spirit of deep sleep and security will come upon us, as the darkness gradually comes upon us, when the sun goes down.

Therefore you that are yet in a Christless condition, had need to be exceedingly

-- 407 --

watchful, and earnestly to struggle against the beginnings and prevailings of a senseless spirit. Your danger is exceeding great: you had need to strive for your life.

The circumstances of those souls that are left senseless, after such a time as we have lately had amongst us, will be very lamentable. It will be very lamentable, because their hearts will probably be more than ordinarily hardened.

To go through such a stir as this has been—a time so extraordinary—and stand it out, and stand proof against it, and grow hardened after it; how dreadful hard will you be at last. For always the greater the means and advantages, the more hardened is the heart, if it stands it out. Your circumstances will look very dark upon this account, viz. that 'tis not at all likely you will ever have so much to move you, and work upon you again. If what has lately been amongst us, and in other towns, won't move you, and thoroughly engage you, what do you ever expect will? If all this is ineffectual, what do you expect will be effectual? What extraordinary things have you been lately surrounded with in this town to work upon you, and then besides that, what you heard of the neighboring towns. There is none living amongst us ever had so much to move them, as sinners have now had. And if all be to no purpose to your soul, but you grow senseless after all, what is it that you can flatter yourself with? Indeed God can convert you, and may convert you, notwithstanding; but when can you expect there will ever be such a probability and advantage for it again, as there has been?

It is probable that now in a little time, it will come to that in this town, that most in the town will either be converted or dreadfully hardened. We have no reason to suppose any other, than that there is a number that will perish eternally; and from the present circumstance and appearance of things, they will probably ere long be a parcel of dreadfully hardened wretches.

And those that do perish out of this town, after there has been so much of the presence of God, and of his wonderful works in it, they must expect no ordinary place in hell. There is perhaps no place upon the face of the earth that is so highly advanced as this town has been of late in point of privilege; and so much the higher persons have been advanced in privileges, so much the lower will they be thrust down in hell, if they perish at last. Matthew 11:23, "Thou that art exalted up to heaven, shall be thrust down to hell." Let these things rouse you, and awaken in you a great concern, lest you should lose the strivings of God's Spirit.

If God seems to be leaving of you, don't rest in a dull complaint, expressing

-- 408 --

your fears that you are going to be left, and saying with a melancholy, discouraged tone that you are afraid you are going to lose your convictions. Don't think to quiet yourself with complaining, and expressing your fears and discouraging apprehensions; but be thorough in endeavors that it mayn't be so. If God is leaving of you, don't sit still complaining of it; but arise, and run, and cry after God, that he may not utterly forsake you.

Seek earnestly, that whether the Spirit of God leaves others or no that yet it may not leave you. If others grow careless and worldly, take heed that it ben't so with you. If the Spirit of God should leave the town, and the unconverted in general should grow senseless and careless; yet if you persevere, and continue steadfast and immovable, in seeking and striving with your might, there will be great hope for you notwithstanding. If it should be universally a dull time with respect to others, yet if you are thorough, there will be great hope for you. It will look as if you were distinguished by the mercy of God, and look very hopefully, as if he had a design of bestowing distinguishing favor upon you.

(2) Don't take it ill of others, when they are admonishing you, and endeavoring to stir you up; but, on the contrary, be open to advice, be willing to receive others' counsels and warning.

If your neighbors at any time are putting you in mind of the danger and dreadfulness of your condition, and the need you have to improve your opportunities, and the danger of backsliding, don't scornfully reject it; but listen to it, and labor to get good by it. And if any think that they observe in you signs of backsliding, and put you in mind of the danger of the way that you are in, be willing to be reproved. Don't make any objections against it, from the character of the person that does it, if you think that they don't live very well themselves, and had more need to be concerned about themselves than other folks.

And though you may think that they are not proper persons to warn you, and that they go out of their place in doing of it, yet don't think of that; but think of the reasonableness of their advice, and the warning that is given. Consider whether or no there be reason and truth in what they say to you, or not. If there be, let it be your care to conform [to] it; and strive that it may have a due effect upon you.

If you are ready to suspect that they don't do it out of good will, but out of pride and conceit of themselves; if it be so, they have done you no wrong by it, but themselves. And therefore such a suspicion should not hinder your harkening to their advice.

Persons are very ready, through the corruption of nature, to be uneasy

-- 409 --

at others' counsels and warnings; and to be trying rather to find some fault in the admonisher than in themselves; and are ready to take it in ill part, and to lay it to their pride rather than their good will. But if at such a time you find any such disposition rising in you, be advised for the good of your own soul to suppress it. And keep and endeavor at all times to submit yourself, and lay yourself open to others' advice; so far as it is agreeable to truth and reason, and that whatever opposition corrupt nature makes against [it]. The more self-denial there is in it, the better on some accounts; for the way to heaven is a way of self-denial, and the more persons deny and cross their corruptions, the more likely are they to obtain salvation. Consider what is said of those that, through pride and contempt, reject the warnings and counsel of others. Proverbs 13:1, "A scorner heareth not rebuke"; and Proverbs 15:12, "A scorner loveth not one that reproveth him: neither will he go unto the wise."

If you are in danger of backsliding and lacking convictions, you should be glad that anyone will say anything to stir you up, and to help you against a senseless spirit. You should rejoice, when you find a disposition too much prevailing to let yourself alone, that others won't let you alone. If you are backsliding after such a time as has lately been amongst us, you are going down a dreadful precipice, and are sinking into a dreadful gulf, and should be glad, if any will lend a hand to stop you: a drowning man will be glad of any that will lend him an hand, let him be who he will; though he be a child, a servant, or anyone never so much his inferior.

(3) I would advise those that have been under convictions, and remain yet in a natural condition, to lay in with some person that they can confide in; to have a watchful eye over you, and not let you alone on a way of backsliding.7 Go to some persons that you yourself have a good opinion of, that you yourself think are faithful and sincere persons; and whose good will you can confide in, and whose words will be most likely to be of weight with you; and desire them to take notice of you; and if at any time they think they begin to see signs in you of being more careless and unconcerned than you used to be, that they would warn you, and admonish you, and not let you alone in such a way; and resolve with yourself to be ready at all times to give ear to them, and to be ready from time to time to open your case to them. I would pray you to consider of it, whether or no it be not a proper expedient for the promoting of your soul's good. If you are truly concerned for your soul, you will be glad to [do] anything that

-- 410 --

is proper to be done, and is likely to tend to your soul's spiritual good. Don't be against it, because it seems to you to be needless; for you need all manner of helps. If you think you see no great danger of losing your convictions at present, yet there is danger of it; and you can't be too thorough in your precautions against that which [is] so awful a calamity to you, if it should befall you.

Such a thing as this may probably be a great help to you. It is a great advantage to have one to give counsel and warning thus, when there is need. Proverbs 25:12, "As an earring of gold, and an adornment of fine gold, so is a wise reprover upon an obedient ear." And your only having done such a thing, may probably be a great help. The remembrance of your having thus laid in with a person to observe you and watch over you, for such an end, will tend to prevent your backsliding. It will be ready to fly in your face, if at any time you begin to be more careless. It may give conscience a great advantage, and tend to make you more watchful over your own heart. When you have desired another person to watch over you, it will tend to prevent your letting down your own watch. I would now proceed,

2. To inquire what may be proper to be done by those that are esteemed godly, that so the Spirit of God may not withdraw from us. It may well [be] expected of those, that they should all with one heart be ready to hearken to proposals for the reaching such an unspeakable good, as the continuance of the presence of God in the town; for that is the spirit of the godly, highly to prize the presence of God, and greatly to desire it in the places where they dwell, and to prefer the prosperity of Jerusalem above their chief joy. And there are so many that are esteemed godly amongst us, that if all such would be thorough to do their part for the upholding and promoting religion,8 we have reason to think it would be successful; and that the presence of God, and the influences of his Spirit, would be continued amongst us. One great cause of the dullness and deadness that there is in religion in many places, is the small number of godly people. But, blessed be God, we have not that calamity here. But we hope there is a very great number of truly pious persons in the town, yea, for the prevailing part; and therefore if religion fails, and it be a dull time here through their neglect, they will be very inexcusable. When the number of lives is added to in a place, the interest of Christ is thereby strengthened in that place: Christ's army is thereby added to. And God has made the number of the saints so great in this place, that if they will do their part, and acquit themselves

-- 411 --

well, and as good soldiers of Jesus Christ, with such a blessing as might be justly expected, they can bear down all afore them here, as it were. Though our strength ben't in ourselves, yet Christ's church is his army, and he is the captain; and he won't forsake his army, when they go forth to fight his battles. Deuteronomy 1:30, "The Lord your God which goeth before you, he shall fight for you."

'Tis not at all likely that irreligion will ever get head9 here in this town again, if the saints don't greatly fail, and ben't very negligent. In many other places, religion is not in credit, because of the lowness of the godly, and far the prevailing part are wicked men. But there is no danger of religion's coming into discredit amongst us, unless it be by the saints slumbering, and sleeping, and being woefully negligent. The saints have a vast advantage put into our hands, for the upholding the interest of religion continually amongst us, by the great increase of those that are of their side.

When a small company are going forth against a very powerful enemy, they are glad by any mercy, if they are reinforced by an addition to their army. It has a tendency to enliven 'em, and revive their courage. God has made a vast addition to his army amongst us: they are become a great army. Don't let us be a dull and cowardly one; but, on the contrary, "terrible as an army with banners."

Let God's people from this consideration be roused, and put upon it, to bestir themselves vigorously, to exert themselves in all proper and prudent ways, against all irreligion, and for the promoting of religion. And here I would mention some things that may be proper to be done by them, that so the tokens of God's presence may be continued amongst us. And here,

(1) Let their first care be with their own hearts, that they don't by degrees grow dull and lifeless. The warfare of Christ's army should, in the first place, be against their enemies that are in their own hearts. If the wise virgins slumber and sleep, no wonder if the foolish virgins do so, too.1 If those that have lately been converted amongst us grow dull, and lose the lively sense they had of things, it will be observable; they won't be able to keep it hid. And if unconverted persons observe this, it will have very much of a tendency to abate the force of their convictions. If those that are accounted converted seem lively, and there seems a very great abiding change in them, it will have a convincing tendency on the minds of natural persons. It will make them think that it is a real thing, and stir them

-- 412 --

up to seek the same blessing; whereas the contrary will be a stumbling block to 'em.

It often is so that converts, after the newness of their comforts are over, grow much more lifeless; and oftentimes it is through want of their keeping up that diligence and strict watch that they ought to. The way for you to continue lively Christians, is to be earnest and laborious in religion.

Some of you were very earnest before you had your comfort; and if you would have your comfort continued, and be lively Christians, you must be as earnest still, and as long as you live, as you was then; and be more and more, if possible, as being sensible that your conversion was but the beginning of your work, and not the finishing of it.

If those that have lately been converted amongst us, do generally continue lively in Christianity, there is no danger of the work of God's ceasing in the town. And if they ben't wanting in watchfulness and diligence, there is no danger but that they will generally continue lively.

(2) Another thing that those that are accounted converted should do, that the Spirit of God may be continued amongst [us], is to be exceeding careful to bring forth fruit meet for repentance. Let such not only be very watchful of the inward frame of their hearts, but also of their outward behavior. If our converts believe unsuitably, and walk unanswerably to the great things God has done for them, there is danger that God may be provoked by that to withdraw from amongst us. God is sometimes greatly provoked by the sins of his children. Deuteronomy 32:18–19, "Of the Rock that begat thee thou art unmindful, and hast forgotten God that formed thee. And when the Lord saw it, he abhorred them, because of the provoking of his sons, and of his daughters." If those that have the reputation of saints amongst [us], should many of them behave very unsuitably to their profession, nothing will have a greater tendency to put a stop to the progress of religion amongst us; for there is, it may be, nothing whatsoever that is a greater stumbling block to natural men, than the ill carriage of those that are accounted godly. Romans 2:23–24, "Thou that makest thy boast of the law, through breaking the law dishonorest thou God? For the name of God is blasphemed among the Gentiles through you."

On the contrary, when the godly walk becoming godliness, there is nothing tends more to the promoting religion. When persons are accounted godly, others take great notice of their walk and conversation, and have just warrant so to do; for the Scriptures often tell us "the tree is known [by his fruit]"; "let your light so shine before men."

And there are four things I would desire that those that {are esteemed godly} would observe with respect to their behavior.

-- 413 --

1. That they would not only be religious in words, but in deeds. Seeing persons religious in words, won't have half the effect on natural men, as to see them so in deeds. Words are but wind, and are very cheap.

Their being religious in talk, won't convince men in any wise of the reality of religion; as when they are ready to do to put themselves out of the way, and to deny themselves. This looks as if there was something in them besides a mere notion, something powerful and effectual.

When persons are accounted converted, 'tis natural for others that are unconverted to watch and observe to see what they do. They hear of a great difference between converts and other men, and they naturally watch to see whether they do more than others. And there is nothing would have a greater influence to convince and influence others, than to see godly persons of a self-denying disposition, and ready to [do] every good work.

Let this therefore influence you to inquire, what you shall render {the unconverted}; what you shall do {to convince them}; which way, by denying yourself and laying out yourself, you shall serve God, or your fellow creatures.

2. Be especially careful that there be nothing appearing in you of your former evil ways. When persons that behaved themselves ill in times past are accounted converted, they will be especially watched with respect to the ill practices they used to live in before.

Thus if a young person pretends to be converted, that used formerly to be extravagant in company, or used ill language, or was much given to mirth; no other can be expected than that they should be narrowly watched, whether they don't do as they used to. And if there be anything of that nature seems to appear, it will have a tendency greatly to stumble unconverted persons; for they have often heard that conversion makes a great change; and they expect, and that justly, that there should be a remarkable difference in their behavior from what used to be.

Therefore be warned to be especially careful that there be nothing appears of your former evil ways, that you walked in before. Be at a greater distance than ordinary that the most watchful, jealous eye may find nothing to lay hold of to accuse you, or stumble at himself, of that nature. And that those [that] are watching for occasion may find none, it may be expedient sometimes to abstain even from things that are indeed innocent; that you keep the farther from anything bordering on your former evil practices.

3. Be very careful that there may be manifest in you an humble and meek behavior. This will have a great tendency to convince and win others.

-- 414 --

And natural men are very ready to conceit2 that those that are converted are proud of it, and scorn others; and that has a tendency to prejudice them against religion.

Therefore be especially careful as to this. Watch against the workings of pride in your hearts, and labor to walk humbly before men. Let there be nothing to be seen of any contentious or envious spirit; for that shows pride. Let there be no appearance of any boasting of your experiences. Show at all times an humbling sense of your former sins and ill practices, and also of your present imperfections. Carry it as one that looks on others better than yourself (Philippians 2:3).

4. Labor that there may be much of a spirit of love appearing in your behavior. If there be much of such a spirit appearing in you, it will be taking and winning to the unconverted. Unconverted persons are ready to have a prejudice against them that are accounted converted; but if they observe such a spirit of love in them not only one to another, but there seems evidently to be a spirit of hearty good will to the unconverted and to all; it will tend very much to abate of that prejudice, and to beget in them a good thought not only of them, but of godliness itself.3

Thus those that are converted, should bring forth fruit meet for repentance.

(3) The next thing that it may be proper for the godly amongst us to do, that the Spirit of God may not depart from the town, is to admonish one another. The godly often need to be admonished and warned by others.

Let the godly amongst us maintain a watchful eye not only over themselves, but over one another. And if they observe any of their brethren that seem to grow dull, and to lose the sense of things of religion, or to grow worldly, or in any respect to carry themselves unsuitably; let them friendly

-- 415 --

be stirring one another up. The godly have dullness and hardness, and often great corruption of heart, and many enemies to grapple with, and need one another's help. The wise virgins are apt to slumber and sleep, and need to be wakened by one another. This [is] a duty required in Scripture (Romans 15:14; Colossians 3:16).

And there is generally much too little of this done amongst Christians. If it was done as it ought to be, it would have a great tendency to uphold religion among us. [It would be] proper for older Christians to be exhorting younger.

And here I would particularly desire that you would not suffer those that sit by you, to sit sleeping at meeting; but wake one another, when anything of that appears. And let none of the godly give way so much to their corruption as to take it ill, when others admonish them, when others jog them to wake them, either out of their natural sleep in time of public worship, or their spiritual sleep, by friendly admonition.

(4) Another thing that it may be proper for the godly to do, that {the Spirit of God may not depart from the town, is to} have a watchful eye over natural persons that have been concerned; and if they seem to be losing their concern, to be stirring of them up. If those that have been under concern seem to be backsliding, don't let them be let alone [in] it. Don't let their neighbors, who have their eyes open, and see where they are going, stand all round them, and see them going in a way that tends so much to their everlasting ruin, and say nothing to them. I would not advise that any should take upon them the work of a minister or teacher, to go about and make a business of warning and exhorting others, and neglect the business of their proper calling. For any man to assume this to himself, unless [he] be orderly appointed thereto, is disorderly and presumptuous. Romans 10:15, "How can they preach, except they be sent?"; Hebrews 5:4, "No man taketh this honor to himself, except he that is called of God."

God has made provision by a stated ordinance that teachers should be set up in his church. And 'tis not for one and another to start up, because they find themselves inwardly stirred up to it, and make a business of teaching and exhorting others, neglecting the business of their proper calling. 1 Corinthians 7:24, "Let every one, wherein he is called, therein abide with God." The role of the gospel is for everyone to wait on4 his proper business. Romans 12:7–8, "Or ministry, let us wait on our ministering; or he that teacheth, on teaching; or he that exhorteth, on exhortation." They whose business it is, must wait on those things, but not others.

-- 416 --

But yet private Christians ought, according to a gospel rule, to be privately admonishing, and stirring up one another, as they have proper occasion and opportunity; and not only so, but 'tis fit sometimes to be seeking opportunity.

And if the godly would themselves have a watch over those that have been concerned, they may probably be a means of preventing their backsliding, and of their holding on in seeking salvation. But let that be done with all possible prudence and discretion. Let persons especially admonish such as they judge: it will be most likely that they can [have] influence upon [them]. There are now so many godly people in the town, that there is scarcely any one natural person that has been under concern, but there is some godly person or other, but what may probably have influence with him. And there [are] perhaps but few godly persons that are adult, but what know of some that they can probably have influence upon. And especially it may be proper for elder persons to be exhorting and admonishing younger persons. Parents should have a watchful eye over their children, and solemnly to be warning them, if they see their convictions seem to be going away.

And this direction must be attended with the former, about keeping a strict watch over ourselves. If we are much in admonishing others, and don't walk well ourselves, our admonitions won't be likely to have much effect. A holy conversation makes a man strong, gives force to his words, gives him great advantage to influence others by reproofs and warnings. On the contrary, an unsuitable, unfruitful behavior makes him weak; it takes off the force of his words.

(5) Let the godly use their influence to keep up religious discourse in the town. If religious discourse be kept up, it will have a great tendency to keep up a religious concern; for what men hear a great deal of talk about, they will be apt to think about.

And as to the keeping up religious discourse, there are so many godly persons now in the town, that if they are resolved upon [what] they can do, there is enough to govern in that matter throughout the whole town, and in all societies. For there [are] many persons of all sorts and all ages and in all relations in the town that are godly; so that if it ben't godly men's fault, religious discourse won't die in the town.

Thus I have finished what I would say on this subject; and I would entreat that what has been said may be attended. 'Tis probable that most will say that these directions are good. I have that confidence that what has been said will be generally approved of. But don't let us content ourselves with verbally approving of what has been said, and saying they were good

-- 417 --

directions; but let them be put in practice. Don't let them only be talked of with approbation. Don't let there only be a little talk in the town about for day or two; but let us apply ourselves to it that the things may be done, and done in a steady way. The good that we seek, viz. the continuance of the presence of God amongst us, is worthy of it. If we practice what has been heard, that good will without doubt be attained. If we generally, vigorously bestir ourselves in an attendance on these rules, I dare be bold to say God won't forsake us.
