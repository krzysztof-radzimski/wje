"Narrative of Communion Controversy" (Dwight Copy) Communion Journal (Dwight copy) Jonathan Edwards

Note to "Journal" ("Narrative of Communion Controversy")

Edwards’s original manuscript of the "Journal" or "Narrative" of the final controversy with his church does not exist, but a later copy, made by Sereno Dwight, does. Dwight’s copy, in the Andover Newton Collection (Miscellaneous Papers, f. 8), is a quarto notebook of 68 leaves, numbered pp. 1-136 by Dwight. The document was published in the Dwight ed., 1:313-99.

I have had difficulties in my mind for many [1] years1 with regard to the admission of members into Church who made no real godliness. These gradually increased, & at length to such a degree, that I could not with an easy conscience, be active in admitting any more members in our former manner without better satis faction. In consequence of this I determined more closely to apply myself to an inquiry into the matter and search the scriptures & read & examine such books as were written to defend the admission of persons to the sacraments without a profession of saving faith. And by reading & study I found myself more strengthened in my reasons to the contrary. On which I came to this determination, that if any person should offer to come into the church without a profession Godliness, I must decline being active in his admission; which, I was sensible, would occasion much uneasiness & public noise & excitement. However I came to this conclusion that I would still continue a diligent search improving the opportunity which Divine Provi dence should give me to that end untill somebody should offer to come into the church, from time to time weighing the matter, with renewed consideration & inquiry. But withal I judged, that it would not be best wholly to conceal my difficulties untill then, lest some inconveniences should arise; and particularly I thought of this, that if some per son should offer to come into the church, whom & my principles would oblige me to reject, I should give no intimation of these my principles until then, it might be suspected that I rejected the person from personal prejudice & that my alleging scruples2 was only to cloak my ill-will. Hence I took some opportunities [2], some years ago, freely & openly to express my opinion before several of our people; which occasioned it to be talked of among many in this town, & in other parts of the land I also designedly gave some intimations of my notions of visible christians in my work on Religious Affections; but was aware that when I came to be necessitated to act upon my principles & on this footing decline admitting any who should offer themselves to be received to the communion, this would occasion a more general noise & tumult; and therefore I determined, if I lived to have such occasion, that I would in the first, go & freely & fully declare the matter to Col. Stoddard. But it was so ordered, that no person offered to join the church for several years, & not untill after the Col’s. death.3

But some time the last of December (1748) a young man, was about to be married, came and offered to come into the church. I told him my opinion. He told me he hoped he could make such a profession as that I insisted on, & would take the matter into consideration. After some conversation it was agreed, that I should draw up a profession of religion that he might see when he come again. Accordingly I did so; & when he came again I showed him the profession I had drawn, but told him I should not insist upon a profession He might draw one himself in his own words; & if the [3] more essential things of true religion were contained in it I should be content. He desired time for farther consideration, & accordingly I let him have the profession I had drawn to consider of. He afterwards came again & returned the profession I had drawn, & manifested that at present he declined coming into the Church in this way, inasmuch as though he hoped he could make a profession of godliness, he did not think that he was obliged to make it in order to admission into the church. The report of this soon made great uneasiness in the town.

Some time in February 1749 I declared the matter fully to the committee of the church & proposed it to them whether they were willing that I should deliver the reasons of my opinion from the pulpit. This was strenuously opposed by several; one or two spoke in favour of my preaching on the subject; but the prevailing voice seemed to be zealously against it. Yet the necessity of the church being in some way informed of the reasons of my opinion, seemed to be allowed by all; & therefore those, who opposed my preaching proposed my printing my reasons, & doing it with all speed. And although there was no note taken, this seemed to be the general conclusion that they must be informed of my reasons from the press. Accordingly I applied myself with all diligence to prepare something for the press. After this, a young woman #4 came to my house to join with the church, having heard of my opinion ; the town by this time being full of noise[4] talk of it & noise about it. I mentioned to her my opinion concerning the qualifications of communicants. She told me she had heard of it but hoped she could make such a profession as I required. Then upon inquiry she gave me a hopeful account of her religious experience & the operations of Divine grace upon her mind; & manifested herself ready publicly to make a profession of religion agreeably to what she had now professed in private. I then desired her to prepare for examination with respect to her doctrinal knowledge & to come to me again & I would draw up a profession agreeably to what she had expressed to me against she came again. I accordingly did so. After some time she came again & I read to her what I had drawn up. She declared herself ready to own that profession, but said 5she was afraid by what she had heard that there would be a tumult, if she came into the church in that way & she did not desire to be the occasion of a tumult by coming into the church. I asked her if she would be willing publicly to make such a profession if the Committee of the Church would consent to it. She said she was6.

Now I perceived so great a ferment in the town, that I was satisfied it was not best to preach upon the subject for the present and supposed it probable there would be no opportunity to be heard with any tolerable degree of calmness and attention, before what I was writing on the subject was published. I therefore prosecuted my writing with the utmost possible diligence.

About the middle of April I called the committee together [5] and informed them that as they seemed to wish at their last meeting that I should print the reasons of my opinion, so I had labored much upon the matter, & had almost prepared something for the press. And as they chose that I should print, so I now chose it also, since I had laboured so far in it & might probably say to this purpose as I am inform ed I did, that the frame of people’s minds was now such that they would be likely to hear in a great ferment if I should now preach upon the subject. But told them withal that the people ought not to proceed to vote for a separation untill they were informed of my reasons in some way or other. To this one of them replied, "No that would be unreasonable;" & nobody said any thing to the contrary; but all seemed to acquiesce in what I proposed & in waiting for my reasons from the press.

I then mentioned to them the case of the young woman aforesaid who desired to desired to come into the church, & read to them the profession of religion she had manifested herself ready to make, & asked them whether they were willing she should make such a profession publicly rather than be kept out: the case being as it was that I could not in conscience be active in admitting persons without a public profession of godliness. One or two spoke for it but others objected against it saying that for the church to consent to this was giving up the case or to that purpose. I told them that I thought that the church would

[blank][6]

nevertheless have the same advantage to insist on my re=[7] ceiving those, who could not make such a profession, & that I was then willing7 never to make use of it as a precedent; & for their farther security, I offered them a written promise in the following words:

I the subscriber do hereby signify & declare to such as it may concern, that if my people will wait untill the book I am preparing relative to the admission of members into the church is published, I will resign my ministry over this Church, if the church desires it after they have had opportunity pretty generally to read my said book & after they have first asked advice8 with regard to the regular steps to be taken previous to their vote: The following things also being provided, viz. That none of the brethren be admitted to vote in this affair but such as have either read my said book, or have heard from the pulpit what I have to say in defence of the Doctrine, which is the subject of it; that the Society will engage that I shall be freed from all rates; And that a regular Council do approve my thus resigning my pastoral office over this Church.
Northampton, April 13th 1749.
 Jonathan Edwards

But still when the affair of the admission came to be put to vote there were but three out of the fifteen who voted for it.

Soon after I sent my book to the printer, urging him very much not to delay the printing. Accordingly the impression was very speedily begun[8] even before the printing of the proposals for subscription. From time to time, I renewedly urged the printer to hasten the impression, and also wrote to Mr. Foxcroft to his utmost to forward it, who accordingly did so as he informed me. Some time in the latter part of July the people grew very uneasy, supposing that the printing was needlessly delayed; & therefore they of themselves called a meeting of the members of the Church, or at least of many of them, to determine whether to wait any longer for that book. And, as I was informed, after some discourse on the matter they determined that Col. Dwight9 who was going the next week to Boston, should make inquiries whether the book was likely to be speedily finished, & send word.

Col. Dwight, when he returned from Boston about the middle of August, brought a number of the books with him, & about twenty of them were dispersed in the town. After this there seemed to be less noise in town untill some time in October.

On the Sabbath, Oct. 15th I stayed the Church and proposed our setting apart a day for fasting & prayer; & put to the matter to vote in the following words - "That a day be set apart for solemn fasting & prayer, to pray to God that he would have mercy on this church, under its present dark & sorrowful circumstances; that he would forgive the sins of both minister & people; that he would make us to be of a right spirit, & enlighten us all that we may know what the mind & will of God is that that which is agreeable to his will & that alone [9] may be established; & that God would restore peace and prosperity to the church." This was voted by a general concurrence. Then I proposed that the services of the day should be carried on by some of the neighboring ministers as supposing that their services would be more acceptable & less liable to suspicion than mine. I particularly mentioned proposed Mr. Woodbridge of Hatfield, Mr. Williams of Hadley, and Mr. Judd of 10 they being most nearest.

There being now several persons in the town who privately made a credible profession of godliness, who were not in the church & hitherto had been kept out of it,-- The Committee of the Church having disallowed of their admission in the way of their making such a profession publicly as aforesaid; therefore I now made a proposal to the Church in the following words.

That these ministers who shall be called to assist at the fast be sought to for advice with respect to the admission of such persons as are able & willing to make a credible profession of true godliness; not that11 minister or people should be bound by their advice to any thing contrary to their consciences; but to see if they cannot find out some way, in which these persons may be admitted consistent with a good conscience in both the Pastor and church, which may be proceeded in for the present untill our present unhappy controversies can be brought to an issue." Some objections were made against this - particularly that it was high time that the whole affair was brought to an issue with [10] regard to the admission of others as well as of those who stood ready to make a profession of godliness. But he who made the objection afterwards explained himself only to mean that some course ought speedily to be taken to prepare things for an issue & particularly that advice should be taken asked concerning measures to be taken in order to the people being generally informed of my reasons for my opinion respecting qualifications for full communion in the Church: -- the people being now in this way to be informed, there being but few of my books in the town, & that they had not been generally read, & were not likely to be at least for a very long time, which others confirmed. He therefore proposed that some of the neighboring ministers should be consulted, with regard to a proper course to be taken by the church in order to a proper information of the grounds of my opinion that things might be speedily ripened for an issue. Upon this, some offered it as their opinion, that I had better deliver the reasons of my opinion from the pulpit. Others objected against it; and it was alleged by some that there had been sufficient information of the reasons of my opinion already or to that purpose. That the leading part of the Church had read my book or most of the leading men in the church, & that it was therefore time that a Council was called to bring the controversy to an issue. I then made the Church this offer, that, if they insisted upon it I would not oppose a council being called which should give us advice in our affairs in general & which should [11] have power if they saw fit, to bring our whole controversy to an issue; though I would12 not advise to it as not supposing the state of things to be ripe for it.-- The people appearing to be of very different minds about the matters which had been discoursed of, they were referred for further consideration to the next sabbath, & it was determined that the fast should not be until the Thursday following that sabbath.

The next day being Monday Oct. 16th, a number of the inhabitants of the Precinct drew up & signed the following writing directed to the committee of the Precinct viz.

"To the Precinct committee for the first Precinct in Northampton.

"We the subscribers that there my be a precinct meeting as quick as may be for the Precinct to take into consideration Mr. Edwards’ doctrine with respect to the admission of members into full communion into the church.

"1st We desire that Mr. Edwards, by the Precinct or by a Committee which the Precinct shall appoint, may be friendlily & in a christian manner treated with, & intreated13 to recede or come back from his principles, which he has pretended to maintain in his late book, against his own practice & Mr. Stoddards practice & principles, with respect to the admission of members: which if he refuse,

2. To see if the Precinct will come into his notions or principles about the admission of church members: which, if the town refuse,

3. Then to determine whether the Precinct do not think that [12] it will be more for the honor of God, & more likely to promote the interests of Religion, & peace & comfort in the precinct, to endeavour after a separation, or any thing else which the Precinct shall see cause to come into: which we desire may be done in the most friendly & christian manner possible.


John Hunt Josiah Pomeroy James Lyman Gad Lyman Jonathan Strong, Jr. Jonathan Hunt Ephraim Wright John Lyman Joseph Wright Gideon Lyman Seth Pomeroy

"Northampton Oct 16th 1749."

Accordingly the Committee issued a warrant in terms agreeable to this demand, & a Precinct meeting was warned to be on the very next Thursday, and was convened on the day appointed viz. Thursday, Oct. 19th. At the meeting it was moved & insisted on by some that it should be put to vote whether I should not be desired to deliver the reasons of my opinion from the pulpit & it passed in the negative. And there being several who objected against proceeding on the business specified in the warrant, that it was very improper seeing we had agreed upon a day of fasting & prayer to seek light from God, that such steps should be taken before that day was passed, the meeting was therefore adjourned for a fortnight.

The next sabbath Oct 22 the Church was stayed, according to the sabbath before, & it was proposed that there should be some farther discourse on what had been proposed the preceding sabbath concerning asking the advice of neighboring ministers about the admission of such persons as stood ready to [13] make a profession of godliness into the church without delaying untill our whole controversy14 was brought to an issue. It was urged that it was uncertain whether our affairs in general could be brought to a speedy issue, that if a council should be called which should have the power to issue them, it was uncertain whether they would think it best immediately to put them to an issue, & particularly that it was questionable whether they would think our affairs ripe for an issue untill the generality of the church had either read or heard the reasons of my opinion & conduct with regard to the admission of members. Then it was said by one of the brethren that it would be proper to see whether the church would agree to what I had proposed with regard the admission of those persons inasmuch as the Church had never yet passed any vote upon it however it had indeed been negatived by the Church committee. Yet it was time enough to ask advice of ministers, when it was seen that the church & Pastor could not agree. Whereupon it was put to vote whether the church would allow those who were able & willing to make a profession of godliness to be admitted into the Church in the way of publicly making such a profession for the present, till our controversy could be brought to an issue & there were but few votes for it. Then the forementioned proposal was put to vote viz. to ask advice of neighboring ministers concerning this matter; & for this also there were but few votes.

Then another thing was proposed to the church viz. that the church would manifest their willingness that I should declare the reasons of my opinion from the [14] pulpit, seeing that it was a thing that seemed to be acknowledged & not disputed that the members of the church in general had not been, nor were likely to be, informed of my reasons in any other way, & that it was most reasonable that they should be informed before they proceeded to act any thing as determining whether I should be cast out of my pastoral office it being an affair of vast consequence to me & my family.

I told them that I asked a manifestation of their consent, not because I doubted of my right to preach what I was satisfied was the counsel of God, without asking their consent, but I chose to proceed in the most peaceable manner possible, & in that way which that would tend most to prevent occasion of strife. After very much said against it by many of the brethren it was put to vote in the following words: - "I desire that the Brethren would manifest their consent that I should declare the reasons of my opinion relating to full communion in the church in Lectures appointed for that end, not as an act of authority or as putting the power of declaring the whole counsel of God out of my hands, but for the sake of peace & to prevent occasions of strife." - It passed in the negative.

Then I told the church that one thing yet remained which I desired of them viz. that it should be left to a few of the neighboring ministers, whether it be not all things considered reasonable that I should be heard in this matter from the pulpit before the present affair should be brought to an issue. Some things were objected with much strenuousness against it & I was charged with15 much abusing the church [15] by my management with respect to the admission of members. One said that if I preached for my opinion, somebody else ought to be allowed to preach against it. I replied that my business was to defend my own opinion: the brethren might use what means they pleased for the defence of the contrary opinion or to that purpose. After much said by many of the brethren, the leaving this matter to neighboring ministers was put to vote & passed in the negative.

The next Thursday Oct. 26th we had our fast according to appointment.#

The next week on Thursday Novem. 2. 1749, the Precinct met16 according to their adjournment & chose a committee of nine to confer with me & consider what measures are proper to be taken in ord17 to issue the dispute between me & my people concerning the qualifications for full communion in the Church, or to that purpose & then adjourned themselves to Thursday the week following. The same committee came to me the next day & told me for what they were chosen by the Precinct & asked me whether I had any measures to propose. I told them that I had already proposed what I supposed to be reasonable in that in the first place I18 proposed that my people should give me a fair hearing of the reasons of my opinion from the pulpit & that they should previously manifest their consent to it; seeing that such previous manifestation of consent would so evidently tend to peace & to prevent tu# A blank was left here in the M.S. probably to give some account of this fast, but it was not filled up. mults or ferments; & secondly that when they had refused[16] this I had proposed that it should be left to some of the neighboring ministers, whether it was not reasonable that they should comply with this proposal. And I told the Committee that I still insisted on19 it as a reasonable thing that they should consent to hear my reasons from the pulpit, & told them withal that they might if they pleased use means to know what could be said on the other side. They might either get20 ministers to preach against it in my pulpit, or they might get whom they pleased to write & publish his reasons against it. They then told me that before they came they had agreed to make me this offer viz. That if I would consent to it they would endeavour to bring the the Precinct to yield that I should preach in defense of my opinion, either on lectures appointed for that end or on the sabbath as I pleased, provided I would first draw out each sermon that I intended to preach at large in a legible character & give it to them & give them opportunity to carry it to some minister, that he might see it & prepare an answer to it before I delivered it & that then I might deliver it, if I would consent that he should from the pulpit deliver his answer immediately after it. I told them that at present I could not think it to be my duty to comply with this proposal, unless it were also allowed that I should beforehand see the discourse of my antagonist, as he was to see mine, that I might stand on even ground against with him.

I then gave them some reasons why I thought it not a reg=[17] ular proceeding for the Precinct to take the consideration & management of this Ecclesiastical affair into their hands in the manner they had done. But they insisted on it that it was not irregular. They then went away without concluding any thing.

The next Monday, Nov. 6, the Committee met again by themselves at another house & concluded upon & drew up the following report

"At a meeting of the Committee chosen by the first Precinct of Northampton to concert what measures are proper for said Precinct to take in order to issue the dispute between the Rev. Mr. Edwards minister of said Precinct and the Precinct respecting the admission of persons to complete standing in the Christian Church said Committee determined to report that they judge that it is expedient that the Precinct endeavour that there may be a meeting of the Church in said Precinct to see if the Church will apply to some of the neighboring ministers for their advice & counsel respecting measures to be taken by the Church in the said affair, which application to the ministers aforesaid said Committee judge the best expedient in the present difficulty which conclusion the Committee came into unanimously, having previously conferred with Mr. Edwards that they might the better determine what would conduce to the end aforesaid.


Ebenezer Hunt Ebenezer Pomeroy Noah Cook Seth Pomeroy John Clark Samuel Mather Joseph Hawley Joseph Wright Noah Wright

Northampton Nov. 6. 1749."

This writing was shown to me by one of their number the Wednesday following, on the evening before the Precinct[18] meeting to which they were to make their report.

The next day Thursday Nov. 9th, the Precinct met again according to adjournment to receive the report of the Committee; & then I sent the following letter to the Precinct:

Dear friends & brethren
"I never heard that any such thing was proposed or thought of by the Committee of the Precinct as is proposed in their Report untill yesterday; their determination was shown me last night by a messenger from them, one of their number; & I have had no objection opportunity to confer with the Committee about or to offer any objection to them against their proposal. I therefore think it requisite that I should at this time signify to you the reasons why the thing proposed by them appears to me not to be regular or reasonable.

1. As the proposal of the Committee is expressed they desire that a church meeting should be warned to see if the Church will not call a council or meeting of ministers to advise to measures to be taken by the church in order to issue the dispute between the minister of the Precinct & the Precinct, which I think is not proper. If the Church call a Council, it will doubtless be in order to be assisted with regard to some controversies or difficulties of its own, & not to remedy the disputes of the Precinct. The business of a Precinct meeting is to manage the affairs of a Precinct; & the business of a Church-meeting is about the affairs of a church & not about the affairs of civil societies. It is not yet certain that there is any dispute or differ=[19] ence between the Pastor & the Church, for this has never been properly tried.

2. If I do not misunderstand the Report of the Committee, it is therein proposed that the Church meeting should in the warning be limited to a particular method of managing the business they meet upon viz. to consider whether to call a council of neighboring ministers to advise to measures &c. I am not against warning a church meeting, if you desire21 to consider of proper measures to be taken to secure & promote the interests of religion, & the church’s own welfare under its present circumstances. But I do not know why the Church should be limited to any certain method of proceeding which the Precinct has thought of. The Precinct has no more business to limit or direct the Church to a certain method in managing its affairs, than the Church has to direct & limit the Precinct in the management of its affairs. It is not yet known, that the Church will not themselves agree on some measures to bring their own difficulties to an end, &22 they will not think proper to choose a Committee of their own to that23 end, who may be successful in contriving a method, to which the Church may agree, which may supersed the need of a Council.

My end24 in sending25 this writing to you is not to perplex you nor clog any reasonable proceedings, but to do my to you as your guide in religious matters & that I may do what is proper to prevent any just blame that you or I myself might hereafter fall under; & therefore I hope that what I have said will be taken in good part, from your affectionate pastor, who desires that you may go in the way of your duty & in the way of God’s blessing & may be happy in God’s26 favour.[20]

Northampton, Nov. 9, 1749. "Jonathan Edwards"

The Precinct, notwithstanding this, at this meeting accepted the Report of the Committee, & passed the following vote: -- "Voted that Deacon Noah Cook & Deac. Ebenezer Pomeroy wait on Mr. Edwards & desire him to call a meeting of the first Church in Northampton to determine by a vote in said meeting, 1st whether there be not a dispute between Mr. Edwards Pastor of the Church in said Precinct & the Church, respecting the question he hath argued in his book last published; & if it shall appear that there is a dispute between him & them respecting the question aforesaid, then, 2ndly To see if the church will apply to some neighboring ministers for advice as to what course the Church shall take." They also added ten more to the Committee of the Precinct chosen at their former meeting; so that the Committee for the management of this affair for the Precinct now consisted of nineteen. Those who were added were


Col. Dwight Josiah Parsons Lieut. James Lyman Capt. Baker Capt. John Lyman Lieut. Hunt, & Jonathan Strong Increase Clark Eleazar King. Capt. Roger Clap

This meeting was still continued & adjourned for four weeks. The next day Deacon Cook & Deac. Pomeroy came to me as they were directed & brought a copy a vote of the Precinct desiring me to warn a meeting of the church &c. as aforesaid. The sabbath following, Nov. 12th, I warned a meeting of the church in the following general terms:-"I desire a meeting of this Church in this place to-morrow at one oclock in the afternoon, to consider what course ought [21] to be taken by this church under its present difficulties with respect to the admission of members into the Church." The church accordingly met the next day Monday, Nov. 13th. The meeting was opened by prayer. And after some things27 said, as much blaming me for warning the Church meeting in such general terms & not in the manner I had been directed by the Precinct & being told that if I refused the Precinct would warn a church meeting themselves without me; I gave the reasons why I did not, when I warned the meeting, specify in the warning those particulars on which the Precinct insisted: As 1. That I judged it would28 a bad precedent & a thing of hurtful consequence for a Church thus to allow itself to be subject to the prescriptions of a precinct meeting; and said further to this purpose, that it was an unreasonable way of managing my29 Church affairs, to bring them first into a precinct meeting & there to consider & debate them & come to a conclusion what should be done; & all this in the absence of the Pastor, he being designedly excluded; & then after all things are settled & ripened for execution in the Precinct-meeting, to send their orders to the Pastor to call a church meeting to pass those conclusions of theirs into church acts, & execute what they had before determined should be done. It appeared to me a way that a tendency wholly to make void all the power of churches & to render Church meetings a mere nullity, & to set the Pastor aside altogether as a cypher, so that he shall not so much as be present, when ecclesiastical matters are debated & ripened and brought to a determination, to have any opportunity to speak his mind, or say one word as attempting to enlighten the Church with regard to what is to [22] be done; but is only made their organ or an instrument in their hands & subject to their will to bring things to execution which they have settled & resolved on wholly without him.

2. That as to the latter thing for which I was directed to call a church meeting, viz. --, To see whether the church will apply to the neighbouring ministers for advice, as to what course the church shall take - I looked upon it unreasonable because all the neighbouring ministers except one were professedly on the side of my people in the controversy between me & my people. And though it was only to give advice what course to take, yet their advice might be such as might in effect finish the whole affair. Such a foundation might be laid by previous advice as might very much determine what remains. But I told the Church that I would not dispute about the former of the particulars & stood ready now immediately to put it to vote; & accordingly put the vote in the following terms:-- "I desire that those who have a dispute or controversy with the Pastor of this Church respecting the question he hath argued in his book last published, would manifest it."-- The major part of the Church here upon manifested that they had such a dispute.

Then instead of the other thing proposed by the Precinct to be put to vote, viz. whether the Church will apply to some neighbouring ministers for advice, as to what course to take; I insisted "That a Council should be called mutually chosen to consider of the present circumstances of this Church relating to the controversy subsisting between the Pastor & people concerning the qualifications of30 communicants, & to give their advice what course we shall take to bring [23] this dispute or controversy to an issue, & in general what is to be done in our present circumstances in order to the Church’s peace and prosperity." To the appointed meeting of the Committee, on Tuesday Dec. 5, I sent the following letter:

Dear Brethren,
I would now lay before you some reasons, why I think that your votes at your late meeting, on Nov. 27th, are not to be approved of; which I would do in the spirit of meekness, & desire that they may be in the same spirit be weighed & considered: 1st. It is manifest that in these votes, you are in various instances41 inconsistent with yourselves:

1. Your votes imply that it is your mind that a Council should be called previous to any endeavours after a separation between Pastor & People & also previous to what you call a definitive Council: that is previous to a council which shall determine whether Pastor & People shall be separated or not: & yet, [28] in your seventh vote, you have voted that it shall be proposed to the first Council, whether the church shall not use means immediately for a separation: which implies that this first Council should pass their judgment whether Minister & People ought not to be speedily separated; which is the very business of the last Council, who, as you yourselves suppose are to determine that matter. If the first Council are to have no power to determine it, then why should they take it upon them publicly to enquire, & judge,[?] & give their voice how it ought to be determined. If there be a certain Consistory to whom it does not belong to decide a matter, & it is also determined beforehand that they shall not decide it, but that it shall be decided by other judges; I think they would but do the part of Busy-bodies to meddle with it so far as publicly to take cognizance of it, & pass their judgment in it. -- According to Congregational prince[ples, on which this church seem to insist, the utmost which any Council under heaven has to do is only to give their judgment, without laying proper obligation on those whom they advise.

2. In your concluding vote in your second paper, you have given your voice, that if I do not agree to what you shall finally insist upon, or to that purpose, it shall be proposed to the Church immediately to call a council to dismiss me. And surely such a Council, if they have any thing to do as Counsellors, will have to judge whether I ought to be dismissed or not; & whether it be consistent with the interests of religion & the peace of the town & Church that I should continue42 here. And yet in the same vote you have voted to propose it to the [29] Church to take this work of the Council into their own hands, & to determine themselves, in the first place, Whether my continuance here is consistent with the interests of religion & the peace of the Town & Church; & Whether a separation ought not to be sought: -- which is first judging the very thing which they are to call a Council to judge of & direct them in. Herein you are inconsistent43 with rules of decency & order & all usual methods of proceeding. -- In so great an affair as the separation of a Pastor & a people, it is by no means proper for a people, whatever their private thoughts may be to proceed to declare their judgment in public votes44 untill they have had the voice of a Council to lead & conduct them.

3. You yourselves, the gentlemen of the committee, have taken it upon you to do that which is properly the business of what you call the Definitive Council, in your third vote wherein you vote that if I continue45 in my principles, I ought not to continue the Pastor of this Church. This vote you have passed as a Committee of this Church; & if you persist in it it must be a part of your Report to the Church intended for their direction; nor can such a vote of yours be of any other use. And so herein you give your judgment & direction to the Church directly in that very matter, which the Last Council is to judge of, & direct the Church in.

4. You vote that a Council should be called previous to any endeavours after a separation; & yet in this third vote you yourselves do at the same time before any council is called, immediately proceed to that which is properly & directly of the nature of an endeavour that I should be separated, provided I do not retract [30] my opinion. For it must be supposed that you had some end in it & passed this vote as a means to some public effect; & the effect directly looked at is not other than a separation in such a case.

5. The only Proviso made in the said Third vote, wherein it is voted that I ought not to continue the Pastor of this church, is ’If I continue of the principles which I have advanced;’ without adding - Or the Church be brought to be of my mind, or any thing of that nature; --- whereby it is plainly supposed that it is a thing already determined & out of question that the church never will be of my mind. And yet you afterwards vote that a Council shall be called for that very end - to judge whether the Church shall take any longer time to study & peruse my book: -- which, if they do advise to, it must be as requisite in order to a proper trial, whether the Church on proper information will not be brought to be of my mind. So that putting both these votes together, it comes to this, - that you would call a council to judge, Whether there has already been a fair trial, whether on proper information will be brought to be of my mind; & yet would46 tell them at the same time that you have decided this matter already, & have determined that it is no longer worth while to make a question of it & that is clear enough already to be taken as a ground of public acts and votes. And this is in effect to tell the council Council at the same time you call them, that you do not need them; having thoroughly determined the matter already yourselves in which you have called them to advise.

II. You are not only inconsistent with yourselves, but I think several of your votes are47 inconsistent with Reason & Justice.[31] 1st. Your votes imply that I should be allowed to choose none who live out of the county of Hampshire to be members of any Council which shall have any thing to do in judging of our affairs, either in giving advice for our conduct, or to determine & finish our controversy; which as the case stands is contrary to plain reason and universally established maxims of equity, & inconsistent with the most acknowledged rights of mankind.

For it is apparent48 that all matters of difficulty, concerning which the judgment or advice of any Council is needed or proposed, are wholly things appertaining to a controversy between me & the church concerning qualifycations of candidates for christian or ecclesiastical communion; & it is well known that the ministers of the county are almost universally on one side, & against me in this controversy. And I desire you impartially to consider, whether if you should persist in these conclusions it would be doing, as you would be done by. Supposing that it49 happened on your side as it has on mine that the ministers of the county had been as fully on my side in the original controversy as now they are on yours; would you think50 it reasonable, if I should in that case insist51 upon it, that you should not be suffered to go out of the county, to bring ministers for any Council, which was to have any hand in judging, advising or determining in our affairs?

2. If I understand your Votes, you have determined that the council which shall be called to advise us to what course to take under our present difficulties shall be so limited that they shall have no liberty to judge of our circumstances in general, & so advise to proper expedients for our welfare as they shall think requisite; & that [32] particular care shall be taken that they shall not give any judgment or advice, with regard to some things which have been matters of difficulty & controversy between me & the Church. And I think you in effect have voted that they shall be limited to that one single thing viz. Whether the Church shall take longer time to study or peruse my book. For as was observed before, the other things which you mention cannot belong to the business of the Previous Council, but are the proper business of the Last Council. Now against this I object the following things

(1.) To call a Council & limit them in this manner does not at all answer the present circumstances & exigencies of this Church. The present sorrowful state of the Church greatly requires a Council which shall have liberty to look into the whole state of our case without keeping some parts of our difficulties out of their sight, that they may give us advice what course we shall take for our welfare. If ever it was requisite that the whole case of a patient under a most terrible & threatening disease, should be laid before physicians, it is requisite that our whole case should be laid before a Council for their advice with regard to our difficulties in general. What we need a Council for if we need any at all, before a council comes to determine whether we shall be separated or not, is if possible to find out a remedy for our broken confused & perplexed circumstances so that Pastor and People may either walk together in peace, or, at least that things may be so regulated that there may be some peace while we are together. And if finally there should appear a [33] necessity for a separation, that things may be prepared for an equitable & peaceable parting. But to tie up a Council to such a single particular as is mentioned in your votes, is utterly to disable them from answering these ends.

(2.) It would be very absurd in itself for the Church to come into such a determination. It would be for the Church to set itself up in a sort of supremacy & self sufficiency, as above all controll & advice. It would be in effect to say - ’In these & these part of the Controversy between us & our Pastor, we need no advice, nor will we allow a Council to give us any.’-- And it would be unhandsome52 treatment of any Council under any circumstances thus to tie them up. The language of it would be - ’We, in these things, are not willing to trust your judgment, esteeming ourselves wiser than you.’ - If you say that these parts of our controversy, which the Council was53 tied up from meddling with, are very clear & plain, then so much the less reason have you to fear leaving them to the determination of a Council; unless you are confident that you are wiser than they.

(3.) For you to insist on these limiting votes will be very unequal & unfair dealing with me. As the Council is to be called to advise in matters controverted between you & me, one party has no more right to limit the other party as to the controverted points which shall be referred than the other party has to limit the one. If I should claim a power to decide in this matter & should single out a particular point, such as I thought would best serve [34] my purpose, & say - ’I will have this matter & this only judged of by a council; & as to other matters, which you desire that they should advise in, I will not suffer it:’ -- would you hearken at all to it or bear such treatment?

One thing more I think it my duty to observe to you before I conclude. After your other votes you conclude all this with an enforcement of the whole That provided the proposals of the committee res"pecting a Council &54 should not be agreed to by me & there should be nothing else agreed to respecting said council & the ends for which they are to be called, you will propose it to the church to vote my principles so & so pernicious & to manifest a desire of separation & to call a council to dismiss me. I think that this vote with these circumstances55 is properly, of the nature with a threatening that if I do not comply with what you the committee shall finally insist upon; you will propose to the church to deal thus with me.-- As you are a committee chosen to confer with me concerning a method of proceeding, I might reasonably expect as you are christians and christians to whom I stand in the relation of a Pastor you would first have seen whether by friendly conference we could not have amicably agreed on measures to be taken. If you had thought it proper to pass any other56 vote at all & to let me see it; one would have thought that at least it should have been forborne untill you had found by conference that I would agree to nothing reasonable, & that this should [35] have been the last thing you did. But at the very first interview to come with such menaces to threaten terrify me into a compliance with you before a word of conference between us; is indeed carrying things with a high hand of which I entreat you seriously & calmly to consider.

On the whole I desire you would not persist in the votes you have passed, & that you would consider again whether the proposals, which were agreed to by me & the former Committee of the church, are not just and reasonable & the measures therein proposed such as our circumstances require. But if not if you can think of any other measures which are equitable & have any tendency to answer the exigencies of our present circumstances I think57 you will not find me difficult or backward to a compliance.

And then I read to the church what now follows, containing some objections to the Report of the Committee:

Dear Brethren, You very well know that what has been insisted upon heretofore by my people, was, that the neighboring ministers should be consulted as a previous Council to give us advice what course we should take before the calling of a Council to determine whether Pastor & people should be separated; & that I objected against it - these ministers being almost universally, by their open profession, on your side in the grand controversy between you & me; - & that I insistted on it as just & equal that I should have a choice with you in the council of advisers; & that if those whom you choose were known to be on your side in the main controversy, I should have liberty to nominate as many who should be on my side; & that this was as just in a council, which should be called to give previous advice, as in a council which should judge concerning the affair of our separation; because such a a foundation might be laid by the previous advice of the First Council, as might in effect finish the whole affair. but, however, I have not been hearkened to in this matter; & one thing urged in opposition to what I have insisted on was that according to the Platform of Church Discipline such affairs should be judged of by those who were of the vicinity, or neighborhood. And finding after long urging what I looked upon as my due & might [41] claim as one of the common rights of mankind, that all my reasonings were in vain, I have now at length yielded that point, & for the sake of peace which in the whole course of this affair I have earnestly pursued, have complied with that you at first insisted upon - viz. that the neighboring ministers should be desired to give us advice what course to take, previous to the Council called to judge whether Pastor & People shall be separated; & that I would leave it to them to judge, on a full view of our case how we shall conduct ourselves. Now I think you ought not to reject what I offer, & attempt to constrain me to a compliance with the new measures on which the Committee have agreed for the following reasons:

1. It would be a very unjust proceeding. The neighboring ministers, on whom you first insisted, have indeed much to prejudice them against me in those affairs, being decalredly against me in the main controversy. But it is well known that many of the ministers of the County who are out of the neighborhood have had much more to prejudice them. These neighboring ministers are all calvinists in their persuasion & friends to the late Revival of Religion & those who have lived in good neighborhood & peace with me, which has not been interrupted by any remarkable breach with me & them, or any known affront or disgust which they have taken. But with regard to the other ministers of the County it is well known, that four or five of them have heretofore had the reputation of Arminians. Some others of them are known to be69 strenuous opposers of the late Revival of Religion, for which I have been so public an advocate. And you know that the dispute about the late work is a controversy which has equally greatly engaged the feelings of men. There are no less than six of them who have either had a particular difference or controversy with me70, or have in time [42] past openly manifested71 some affront or disgust which they have taken. Another of them, one of the senior ministers of the County has shown a strong prejudice in this particular affair72 in something which he has said to two of the brethren of the Committee of this Church as I have been well informed. Another of them has an own Father in the town who is one of the Committee; & several of his brethren73 are greatly engaged in this controversy.

2. If the church, at the same time that they agreed to the Report of the Committee should withal say that if I had any reasonable74 objection against any particular minister he should not be chosen; still proceeding on this plan would be in many ways of unhappy consequence. It would necessitate me publicly to point out particular ministers of the County, & openly to object those things against them, which would naturally tend to excite unpleasant feelings between those ministers & me - to beget new prejudices & revive & establish old ones. And then it is wholly uncertain whather the Church would esteem reasonable objections & they75 would open a door for new difficulties & endless controversy about the particular members to be chosen concerning the principles & past conduct of ministers and probably with regard to some ministers whether they be in the County or not: it being a matter of controversy, not yet decided concerning three, who used to be reckoned to be of the County, whether they indeed be of the Province.

3. If the church should now depart from what they76 formerly insisted on & I have now offered in compliance with them, & should insist77 on the measures proposed by the Committee they would act very absurdly & inconsistently. For the Platform has heretofore been insisted on as direc=[43] ting ministers of the neighborhood and seems still to be insisted on in the Report of the Committee under the name of the Constitution of these Churches; & yet this same Committee in this very Report, insist on liberty to go out of the neighborhood without being limited by any other bounds than those of the County. whereas it is those ministers whom I have proposed, & they only who are properly the ministers of the neighborhood. The Platform speaks of neighborhoods but says nothing of Counties. Many of the Counties churches of the County are no more in the way of communication with us than some Churches out of the County. The churches in Sheffield & some others in this County are no more in the way of mutual concern & intercourse with us in our religious affairs, than the Churches in Boston nor78 indeed not near so much. So that the Committee insist upon the Platform, & our being confined to the neighborhood, & yet at the same time insist on liberty to deviate from the Platform & to depart from the neighborhood. Yea they are yet more absurd; for one grand point that is in controversy between us, is,-- Whether we shall have liberty to go from the neighborhood for any Council. And yet they insist upon liberty to go from the neighborhood in the first place for a Council to determine whether we shall have liberty to go from the neighborhood; which is the most gross & palpable inconsistency.

As to the determination of your Forefathers 36 years ago, that they would be subject to a Council of the Churches of the County, you of this generation never looked on it as any constitution for you, nor have you ever in instance conformed to it: For you have yet in any one instance, since I have been your Pastor, referred any thing [44] to a Council of Churches, but to Consistories of another nature. And besides the plain design of that vote was that all the Churches of the County taken together should be consociated, as a standing Council, agreeably to Mr.[?] Stoddards Presbyterian Principles who was the first mover in that affair & drew that Vote.

And moreover what I now offer viz. that our affairs should be referred to the ministers of the Association to which we belong is much more agreeable to the design of that Vote. Since the state of the County is so exceedingly altered from what it was then, being divided into different associations, & not only so, but become so much larger the number of Churches vastly increased & more dispersed at a great distance one from another. This alteration in the state of the County renders it impracticable for the Churches to abide by that determination, so as to be obliged to every emergency wherein they need counsel to call a Council of the whole County, consisting of near 60 members from such distant places.

On the whole I renewedly insist upon it that the offer I make you is in itself highly reasonable and fair, yea that therein I evidently depart from my just right in compliance with you. that, if possible, our affairs may be proceeded in with peace & without tumult. What I now propose is what you yourselves till79 now insisted on; & I apprehend there can be no imaginable reason why it should never80 be departed from, unless it be to lay me under still greater disadvantages, & to have opportunity to bring in such into the Council as are still more prejudiced against me." --- One thing I further objected which was against the manner of the draught of the Committee’s report, which it need=[45] less now to rehearse.

And then they proceeded to hear both what the Pastor & the agents of the Church had to offer on those articles which the Council had been desired to judge of & advise in. On the question Whether it be89 not reasonable & best that I90 should be allowed to go out of the county for ministers or Churches to be some of the members of the Council who are91 to judge, whether I92 should be dismissed from my93 Pastoral office here, or not; I94 submitted to the Council the following remarks: In order to determine whether I ought to be allowed to go out [49] of the County in my choice of a part of the Council which is to decide on the question of my dismission, it should be particularly considered -- What the business of such a council will be. And here I would observe.

1. That the business of the Council will not be to judge, - Whether my opinion or the point in controversy be right or not; - for that would be only to determine, whether my opinion & theirs be the same; which is supposed to be a thing perfectly known before the calling of the Council. On such a point, the opinion of ministers & churches cannot easily be hid & they will be chosen on each side, because they are either of the one opinion or of the other.

2. Nor will it be the main business of that Council to judge, Whether or no, I should fairly95 continue the pastor of this church, if the people after all fair means used, & all proper steps taken, to effect an accommodation, should finally desire that I should not be their pastor? -- I have never given this Church the least reason to suspect that I had any such thing in view by any of my conduct. And besides I stand ready to save any Council the trouble of judging in that matter. If the Church would in the first place give me a fair hearing, & take all proper previous steps, & treat me in that respect with justice, & answerably to the obligations which they owe me as their pastor, & yet finally should desire my dismission; I should trouble a council no farther than barely to give me leave to relinquish my pastoral office.

But if I mistake not the business of the Council will consist chiefly in the following things.

1. In determining whether the pastor & church ought to be separated they must have liberty to do what they can towards effecting an accommodation. It will be unreasonable to call a council to decide on the question of separation [50] & yet so tie up their hands that they shall be obliged to proceed on the supposition that the disease is desperate without allowing them to judge of that matter for themselves, or to use any means or endeavours for a cure. The separating of Pastor & people will be an important event, an event followed by great extensive & very unhappy consequences, & ought not to be done without obvious & irretrievable necessity. That necessity ought not to be determined merely by the parties at variance but by the Council which judges whether we must be parted or not. The desperateness of the disease should not be determined by the patient but by the Physician. That Council must have our whole case laid before them, & then they must judge whether it will be worth while to use any endeavours for an accommodation. And if they judge it is worth the while, then they must have liberty to use the best of their skill in order to effect it. For my own part though I confess there appears to me no probability of our difficulties ever being adjusted; yet I feel that I am not infallible nor able certainly to determine that they cannot. I cannot certainly say that a council cannot enlighten me so as to make my conscience easy as to any point of practice so as to proceed in it with a good conscience. Nor can my people as I apprehend, certainly determine that no Council can ever satisfy them as to any point on which we are at variance now divided. It is worth the while to try the skill of some of the ablest divines in our land, & indeed it is necessary that it should be done before we proceed to an act fraught with such important consequences96 to Pastor & people. And here the question arises. What sort of a Council is proper to be employed in such an attempt? a Council wholly consisting of divines on one side in the controversy; or a Council of some on both sides?[51]

2. If they conclude that there is no hope of an accommodation they will then be called upon to decide - Whether the parties are now ripe for a separation. And the grand point here presented to them will be, What justice demands with regard to each party. The claims of both parties must be weighed by them as in a balance. On the one hand they must determine what are the just claims of the people, & whether my continuing here can be consistent with their rights. On the other they must consider, what I can claim by virtue of my relation to the people as their pastor; whether the steps which ought to be taken previous to a separation have actually been taken, whether they have given given me a hearing on the question in dispute & have done me justice in this controversy so that nothing remains which I can fairly demand of them, before they can fairly demand a release from all their obligations to me as their pastor. The case presented to them for their decision will therefore be a case of simple justice & equity between two parties at variance. And here the question again arises, What sort of a council is proper to be employed in deciding such a case - a Council wholly consisting of divines on one side in the controversy; or a Council consisting of some on both sides?

3. If the future Council should decide on an immediate separation between Pastor & people they must also set forth to the world in their Result the reasons of their decision. They must explicitly declare whether it is for any thing blame worthy & scandalous in the pastor which renders him unfit for the ministry & worthy to be dismissed from it; or whether he is innocent in the affair, how far he has conducted himself well and treated his people justly, & how far they can recommend him as fit to be employed elsewhere in the work of the ministry. This is what is usual in such cases & what the very nature of things renders just & necessary. But the state of the present case renders it necessary in a peculiar manner, and that on several accounts. One is, the well known fact, that [52] many reports have been industriously circulated through the country, relative to my conduct in this affair, which are greatly to my disadvantage. It is continually asserted by my opposers that I wish to lord it over God’s heritage, that I am contentious & quarrelsome, that I am obstinate, stiff, & inflexible, & that I would not yield an ace in my opinion to save myself & my family from ruin.- Another is, that my themselves have rendered it absolutely necessary, in that, from time to time I have been publicly blamed & highly charged with regard to my conduct. As this appears evidently the prevailing disposition of my feeling to cast blame upon me & they do it here openly & publicly from time to time, I have no reason to think they restrain themselves abroad. And as there is a great multitude of them, many mouths to reproach me, & they are very much abroad in various parts in N. England & I have only my own single voice to defend myself with; so there seems to be no other way for my defence than by the inquiry & judgement of an impartial Council.-And then, besides the reproaches of my people by word of mouth their public conduct towards me is such as casts a reproach upon me. The whole series of their conduct towards me is such as casts has this language, uttered too with a loud voice, that I am most insufferably criminal. This is particularly true of their openly refusing once & again to receive the Sacrament of the Lord’s supper at my hands. It has this look -- that I am a scandalous person: this is the language of it, it has this appearance to the world. Of course this future Council will unavoidably have to judge between me & my people in this matter. And here again the same question presents itself. What sort of a council is proper to be employed in deciding on my conduct & character -- A council Coun consisting wholly of those who are known to be against [53] me, & to side with my opposers; or a Council consisting of some on both sides?

These three things, it is plain, will constitute the main business of the future Council; & the question What kind of a Council is requisite to judge in such a case & to judge decide on these points, cannot I humbly conceive be a matter of any difficulty. It must be evident to every man of the least reflection that an Impartial Council is indispensable, or at least a Council so constituted that it may be as near to impartiality as may be. This will appear, if each of these three points, on which the Council must judge is duly considered. If they are to attempt an accommodation or to bring the two distant parties together; surely it is proper that the Council which is to do this should be themselves in the middle, or97 not all on one side, or with one of the distant parties. If they are to decide Whether the Church have done me justice in this controversy as to what I can demand of them, before I98 can demand a separation? need I ask, whether the tribunal which is to decide a simple point of equity, between the99 parties at variance, in a case deeply interesting to both, ought to be impartial? and if they are to judge between the100 parties one of which blames & condemns the other in a very open manner & it is their duty to decide whether these accusations are just, & whether the party accused is innocent or guilty; does this venerable Council need an argument from me to prove to them that impartiality is an essential qualification in the tribunal which is to judge between two such [--] parties & that the members of it ought not all101 to be on the side of the side which lays the blame & brings the charge; but a part of them on the side of the party blamed in the original controversy. Since in this case we cannot expect to obtain a Council which shall be impartial102 -- but all must be supposed to be on one side or the other in the main controversy103, the other should also be allowed to choose such as [54] are on his; & neither party tied up to such limits in his choice, that all opportunity of any tolerable degree of impartiality in the Council should be precluded.

Hence it must be reasonable that in the choice of the future Council, I should be allowed to go out of the limits of this County for some of the members: it being a fact perfectly well known, concerning the ministers & churches of the County, that they are almost universally on one side in the original controversy. And this is the point now to be determined by this Rev. Council. I would endeavour therefore, in the first place, to show that it is reasonable & necessary that I should be allowed this liberty, as impartiality is to be sought in the Council; & in the second, would mention several circumstances which render it highly expedient.

I freely own that it is a good general rule, that Councils, which are to judge of difficulties arising in particular churches should be constituted of neighboring churches. But to say that this is a rule so established by the word of God, or the reason and nature of things, & made so universal, that it never will or can admit of any exception & never in any case whatsoever ought to be dispensed with, is carrying the matter to such an unreasonable length, as no one of the members of the104 Rev. Council would sanction. Let us suppose a case which is not impossible, that a whole neighborhood of ministers were nearly related to one of two parties, between whom a council was to judge; would any one say in such a case that they & they only must be the judges because they live in the neighborhood. Would any one imagine that the mere circumstance of vicinity, or of county limits as fixed by the civil power, ought to outweigh such an essential circumstance as consanguinity; however the ministers of the neighborhood might be men of wisdom & great integrity. Now though perhaps it may be disputed, whether unity of [55] sentiment in matters of religion has an equal tendency to prejudice the mind in favour of particular persons & their behaviour, with consanguinity; yet I suppose it to be a point beyond dispute that it has a powerful tendency; & that diversity of sentiment has an equally powerful tendency to prejudice the mind not only against the doctrines which are opposite to them105 we embrace but against the persons who introduced & maintain them. In all ages & nations diversity of religious sentiment has occasioned uncharitableness & censoriousness in mankind one towards another; & the strongest prejudices which have appeared among men have been owing to this cause. Very often has this been true when106 the difference has been in things not fundamental. Such is the weakness of human nature on this point that few men get the mastery of this temptation. Here & there an eminently great man appears to have conquered its influence. Yet even among great men instances are rare. How evident is it that men of distinguished learning & talents, & of eminent piety are often powerfully influenced by this prejudice & that insensibly to themselves. And if we examine the history of ages past we shall find abundant evidence, that even consanguinity itself does not render us more liable to powerful prejudices than this very cause.

The prejudices to which we are thus exposed are not merely against the persons of individuals, but against their conduct, especially against that part of their conduct which is immediately connected with their opinions, in avowing and maintaining them, & endeavoring to introduce & propagate them. How greatly have the members, & especially the ministers of the Church of England, even those among them who are great & good men, been prejudiced against the persons & conduct of Dissenters; & how have they accused them of bigotry & blind zeal & perverseness. And how fully has our [56] liability to prejudices of this nature been exemplified of late in New England in persons of opposite opinions respecting the late extensive Revivals of Religion, how strong have been the prejudices occasioned thereby against the persons & conduct of many individuals. Especially is this true when the controversy about the opposite religious107 is in the height of agitation. Above all is the temptation great, with respect to the individual who is the first & main occasion of the controversy & appears as the head & spring of the whole debate as moved & maintained in the given time & place; which is precisely my case in this existing controversy. And the influence of this cause to bias the minds of men has been strikingly manifested exemplified in this very case in ministers of good character & such as in other respects have been very friendly to me. Since this controversy has existed at Northampton, I have had occasion to converse with many gentlemen in the ministry on both sides of the question; & I find a vast difference, between those on one side & those on the other; in regard to their charity with respect to me & my conduct. Those on one side are more apt to give heed to reports, which they have heard to my disadvantage, & to be inquiring with concern into such & such parts of my conduct. They receive with hesitation & difficulty the explanations which I give, & the reasons which I offer & entertain surmises & jealousies of my design & of the motives by which I am governed. But with the ministers of the other side I find nothing of this nature.

It is very obvious that the members of this church themselves are perfectly aware of the tendency of religious opinions to bias the minds of men in this very controversy. When one of the brethren at a late church meeting, spoke in my favour [57] on one of the points now to be decided by the Council; one of the influential members, an officer in the church & one of the Church committee rose & told the Church that what that brother had said was the less to be regarded because he had manifested himself to be of my opinion with respect to the qualifications for communion. And the public acts of this people show how fully sensible they are of the strong tendency which sameness or contrariety of opinion will have108 prejudice ministers & churches. To what other cause but such a consciousness, shall we attribute the fact, that they strive so laboriously & perseveringly to confine109 exclusively in the ultimate decision of this controversy to judges who are now110 on their side of the question; & that they have hired able counsel, to plead in their behalf for this very purpose. If identity or diversity of religious sentiment has no tendency to bias the mind, why all this anxiety & effort & expence & struggling to confine me to judges who differ from me & agree with themselves.

As to the neighboring ministers, I sincerely profess a very honorable esteem of them, & desire to be thankful that I have lived in peace & friendship with them; & I doubt not that they are gentlemen of too much candour to regard it as a personal reflection, when I suppose them, as well as others, liable to prejudices from this cause. I presume none of us are unwilling to own that we are the111 subjects of the common infirmities of human nature; & doubtless we have found this the fact in so many instances that we should in some cases not think it wisdom to trust our own hearts.

This then being so evidently the case, if the decisive Council are generally of an opinion contrary to mine & the same with that of my opposers on the matter in dispute, they cannot be regarded as impartial; & of course I shall have [58] no fair chance for justice from them; & shall not, in debating & de determining the matter in controversy stand on equal ground with the other party. The point then is plain beyond all question that I ought not to be confined to such a Council.

How tender does the judgement wisdom & justice of all civilized nations teach them to be towards every one who has a deeply interesting cause depending, with regard to the impartiality of his judges. When he has any objecttions against any one proposed as a judge, how easily do they admit them, if there be the least appearance of any circumstance, tending to bias & prejudice the mind. How readily, for example are such objections admitted against any who are nominated to be of a Jury.

Local proximity, I fully admit, ought ordinarily to be regarded as a circumstance of weight in a Council who are to be judges in a religious controversy; but in no measure of equal weight with the essential qualifications of the judges themselves. And as to the qualifications of a judge, what is so essential as Impartiality ? What can be more essential in a balance, which is to determine the true weight of things, than that the scales be even ?

Thus I have given my reasons why I think the rules of equity & a regard to justice to the common rights of mankind do most evidently require that I should be allowed to go beyond the limits of this County in my choice of some of the members of the future Council. I now proceed to mention several circumstances which render it highly expedient that I should have this liberty.

This Rev. Council cannot but be sensible that it deeply concerns my reputation & my future usefulness as well as the subsistence of my family that I should have final justice done me in the Result of the future Council.112 [59] That some of the members of that Council should come from beyond the limits of this County deeply concerns my future usefulness in another respect viz. That if I am ever employed in the work of the ministry hereafter it is not probable that it will be in this part of the Country, or any where in these western parts of N. England; and it will have a vastly greater influence as to my reputation in other parts of the country farther eastward than Hampshire, if some ministers of note who belong in those parts having had full cognizance of those affairs do recommend me.

It is the more expedient that the separation of the minister & people of Northampton, if it take place, should take place under the direction of a council having some of its members from distant places & of chief note in the country, as it will be an event of great & extensive influence on the interests of religion, & the Church of God. Northampton having been a place much heard of & extensively observed by the Church at large as to its religious concerns, & the past state of things between minister & people having been much known; the report of our separation must needs produce an extensive & great effect as great & on some accounts much greater on places than on places that are near. People at a distance have been more ignorant of our former imperfections & have been ready to look on Northampton as a kind of heaven upon earth. The result of the final Council will undoubtedly be published to the world & will be regarded with deep attention by many not only in New England but113 the other provinces of North America as well as by some perhaps in England & Scotland. Hence surely it is best before this unhappy event of a separation shall take place that some of the wisest and ablest men in the country should have opportunity [60] to look into our affairs & give us their advice & use their wisdom if possible to prevent this calamity & that if it must take place a just report of it with its causes & circumstances may be given to the world by men whose characters are known & respected in other parts of the world. Both my reputation abroad & the interests of religion greatly require this.

It is the more reasonable that in the determination of an affair of such extensive influence I should not be confined to the limits of this particular neighborhood, because as things are constituted in this county114 there is an115 appeal from one Council to another, no appeal from a Presbytery of a vicinity to a Council or Synod from larger limits. But if the case goes to the vicinity that is the last resort, & they will have as much power in the case, as the general Assembly of the whole nation would have in the like case in Scotland. I beseech this Rev. Council most deliberately & impartially to consider these things & give them their due weight, as I doubt not they would govern themselves by those good rules of equity and charity, "Thou shalt love thy neighbor as thyself," & "Do unto others, as thou wouldst that they should do unto me.116"

I now proceed to answer some objections. In reply to all the arguments derived from the Platform of Church Discipline, as a constitution or establishment binds117 these churches, I would suggest the following observations

1. I know of nothing possessing the force of a rule or establishment to bind particular Churches of Christ without an express act or consent of their own, unless it be the Word of God. [61] On the principles of Protestantism, I know not by what rule, a Council, which sat ninety years ago could make a rule or establishment which could bind the present Churches without any free act of theirs, or without making it their own rule.

2. The present Church of Northampton never made that platform their rule or had the least regard to it in any one public proceeding since I have been their Pastor & I know so much of their present & past state that I may be bold to say they have never pretended to make this rule a directory in ecclesiastical matters since any one of the present members of the church was a church member. I never heard it mentioned by any of the church on any occasion until now; & I verily believe that untill a little while since the great body of the members never knew there was any such thing in existence.

3. It is inconsistent with the118 compilers of the platform119 itself, that it should be of the nature of a constitution or establishment obligatory on future churches, or on present Churches any farther than by their own free act120; for the compilers of that Platform plainly shew it to be his121 own opinion that each particular Church has under Christ all power of discipline within itself without being bound by the determinations of other churches; & that the government of the Church is congregational & not national nor Provincial nor Classical, & therefore not subject to the decisions & constitutions of national, provincial, or classical Synods unless by their own free act.

4. The Platform itself allows expressly of departing from the vicinity when the nature of the case leads to it. [62] The words of the Platform are "There should be liberty without offence to make use of other churches as the nature of the case & the advantage of opportunity may lead thereto." I think I have plainly shown already that the nature of this case does lead to it & absolutely requires it.

As to the Vote of this Church, in the days of our fathers 35 years ago. That they would be subject to a Council of the Churches of the County until some superior Judicature were established in the province- it may sufficient here to suggest the following brief considerations

1. No persons professing Protestant principles will maintain that Christians of the present generation are bound in affairs of religion & the worship of God by the determination of their forefathers unless they have adopted the act of their forefathers & made it in some way or other their own122 act or consent either expressed or implicit.

2. This appears still more obviously in the present case from the very different circumstances of the county in this generation & in the last. This act change in the state of things shews this act to be void unless it has been renewed since. Had their circumstances been like ours, our forefathers, we have the best reason to believe would never have formed such a determination. We have now two associations. The Churches are far more numerous & more dispersed. And as the state of things which was the ground of this act of our ancestors has ceased we must suppose the act itself to cease as to any obligation on us unless it has been renewed.[63]

3. There has been no recognition of this act by us, either explicit or implicit123. Certainly there has been no explicit obligation recognition. Who can point out any act or vote of us124 by which we have recognized its binding force. Neither have we implicitly consented to it. Since I have been the pastor of this Church though we have on many occasions had the subject of Councils before us we have never in any one instance paid the least respect to this125 act; nor do I remember that it has ever been once mentioned before the Church.

4. We have implicitly renounced it in several ways. Our proceedings have always been inconsistent with it. The vote refers to a Council of Churches; whereas we have been connected only with councils of ministers. The Vote refers to a stated Council of the Churches of the County according to Mr. Stoddards known Presbyterian principles and not to elective Councils; whereas all the Councils with which we have been connected have been elective. We have also implicitly renounced Presbyterianism, which that vote was intended to introduce & have adopted congregationalism.

5. If it had not been renounced but made our own act as much as we could make it so, it must be understood only as a general rule & could not be of force in extraordinary in which it would be contrary to reason and the rights of mankind to adhere to it; for as far as it is contrary to these it is contrary to the law of nature which is the law of God, & the law of Christ, the great head of the church.

6. In this very case the church themselves propose to renounce this act. The vote speaks of a Council of all [64] the Churches of the County, taking them as they are without election; whereas the Church have only insisted on a small part of these Churches designated by election. But126 if all the vote is not binding, no part of it is binding. As to what I wrote 14 years ago in the controversy concerning the settlement of Mr. Breck127 of Springfield wherein I say, - That the affairs of Religion when128 not confined to single churches, properly belongs129 to the neighboring churches; -- I would observe as follows

1. It would be unreasonable to understand me otherwise than I really intended viz. that this ought to be regarded as a general rule & an ordinary point of regularity. --- I suppose that the neighboring ministers think it a good general rule as well as I, & have perhaps expressed themselves to that effect, yet I have130 reason to think there is not one of them, who thinks it a rule that will admit131 of no dispensation. Which of the Rev. ministers here present, who knows what the state of things has been of late in Connecticut, with regard to some of the Associations there, & especially with regard to one, would think that every church & every minister within its bounds, in all ecclesiastical affairs in which they needed the help of other churches & ministers ought to be obliged in every case to make use of, & submit to the neighboring ministers & them only. Yet their Associations & Consociations have much stronger claims to be regarded as an establishment, than any thing of a like nature among us.

2. It is not merely on this occasion, when I myself am concerned & my own interest touched, that I have insisted that there ought to be exceptions in extraordinary cases from this general rule; for I insisted on this in that [65] very controversy respecting Mr. Breck. There is abundant evidence also that I expressed the same opinion long ago before the controversy between me & my people was begun. Some of the ministers here present are my witnesses, that I expressed the same opinion on occasion of the transactions of some of the associations in Connecticut. And this whole church are my witnesses to the same point: They know that I signified as much publicly in word & deed, when the Separate Society in New Haven sent to this church to assist them in their132 Council by their pastor & a messenger. And the Church themselves did on that occasion publicly consent to a departure from this general rule without any objections made by even one individual; & accordingly a messenger was actually chosen133 to go with me as a member of the Council at New Haven.

3. Suppose it could be134 made to appear that in what I wrote 14 years ago in that controversy I expressed myself in universal terms - that I declared it to be my opinion that there ought to be no exception in no case whatever -- & that I could not prove that I135 ever changed my mind untill now when it comes to be my own case; yet even in that case the question to be decided by this Council as I humbly conceive is136 not what my opinion once was; but what is really just & right in its own nature. The judgment is the Lord’s137, & not mine nor any other man’s. They are to decide according to the rules of reason, & the word of God, what are God’s rules; & not by what once was, or is now my opinion. If a man had a cause depending before a civil tribunal, & it could be proved that this man 14 years ago had given it as his opinion in [65a] another man’s case that such a thing was according to law; which opinion, if now adopted as a rule by the judges would operate138; I conceive that the judges ought not to be determined, even if he expressed himself in an unqualified manner, by what he then declared to be law in his opinion; but as they are to judge for the King & country, they must judge according to what they themselves find to be law which is the rule they are to go by.

If any shall say that it is but just that I should be paid in my own coin that I should be dealt with as I have dealt with another; I need not inform the venerable Council that the christian rule is to deal with139 another not as he hath dealt by me or by his neighbor but as I would that he should have dealt with140 me, & as he ought to have dealt by his neighbor. But if any should insist that this is but a proper punishment for my dealing in that manner141 by Mr. Breck; omitting many things that might be said concerning the difference of the two cases. I would only say that my crime in that case, if there was any for which I deserved to be punished, was not doing or acting any thing whatever in opposition to Mr. Breck’s being settled by ministers from a distance. I had no hand at all in opposing those ministers, in any thing they did; for all that was done of that nature was done when I was not in New England, & when I was totally ignorant of any thing that was done untill all was over. My crime was merely defending what others had done at their request. What I wrote was at the request142 of my honored uncle Mr. Williams of Hatfield & other ministers who had been concerned to [66] justify what they had done. So143 if what I then wrote even supposing144 I had expressed the opinion that there should be no exceptions to the rule, would145 justify this Council in proceeding with me in this case on principles which otherwise would not be righteous; it must be because the Council esteem it a sufficient reason to depart from what is just & equitable in itself in their dealings with146 to do according to an opinion which I expressed many years ago in the vindication of others. Far be it from me to entertain so low an opinion of the wisdom & justice of this Rev. Council.

Having made these observations, there is no need of my saying any thing further concerning a practical agreement of the Churches of the County to manage their ecclesiastical affairs among themselves. I would only briefly observe that what I say of this in what I wrote relative to the case of Mr. Breck, obviously had reference to the Ordination of ministers. That was a case of ordination & I do not remember that I ever knew or had heard any thing of a Council of ministers in this County on any other occasion but the ordination of ministers. But had my meaning been different, the preceding remarks are sufficient to shew that nothing could have been inferred from it which ought to govern in the present case. If there has rarely been a Council of Churches in the County in cases of controversy in particular churches; then the instances do not amount to an established rule for all cases both ordinary and extraordinary. And if the instances had been ever so numerous, yet custom can establish nothing contrary to Christ’s own rules147 of reason & the rules of natural righteousness & equity. It is also objected that to allow ministers [67] to go out of the County for a Council, in cases like this will open a door for error which cannot be closed; & that I assert the same in what I wrote concerning the ordination at Springfield. To this objection a very brief reply will be sufficient.

1. What I assert in the communication referred to is this That to allow one party to select148 all his own judges, will be to open a door to error -- And it is very true that when that is done there can be no security to the other party against149 injustice or any thing else that is bad. But I never asserted that to call an impartial Council was the way in which either truth or justice could not be defended. Had I done so, I should obviously have asserted the grossest absurdity.

2. If it had so happened that half of the ministers in the county had been of my opinion on the qualifications for communion; according to this argument it would as effectually have opened the door to error to choose my part of the Council from within the County as it now will to choose it from without; & if so, it would have been in that case a good argument against my having any choice at all. And this is in effect saying that there ought to be no judges allowed in the controversy except the people themselves who constitute the parties; because, for them to have all the judges of their own choosing is one & the same thing as to be their own judges. And if it had happened that churches in the county had been almost all of my opinion, as now they are of theirs; then the argument would be just as strong for their going out of the county as now for confining me to it. Can this venerable Council lend their sanction to a rule which works such mani=[68] fest injustice?

3. This rule will no more defend truth than expose it. If a particular church is in the right & the rest of the county happens to be in the wrong, then that church in summonning a Council receives by adhering to this rule, just as much disadvantage, as they would receive advantage if the reverse were the fact. And we all know that there are as many churches, & counties, & countries which are erroneous, as orthodox -- nay many more.

4. The objectors in making this objection implicitly admit, that those who are on one side in a particular150 controversy are not likely to be impartial & therefore not likely to do justice to the other side. Otherwise how would the cause of truth be exposed by a Council though they should all be on my side, much less by one half of them being so. And if their being all on my side will naturally bias them in favour of me & my conduct; then, by parity of reason, their being all on my side will naturally bias them in favour of them & their conduct. If this be so; it proves the necessity of a balance in the Council to bring the whole taken together to an equilibrium.

I will151 take notice of one objection which may possibly arise in the minds of some of the Council, viz. That if I am allowed to go out of the County, on the ground that this is an extraordinary case, it will be a bad precedent & others will insist on the like liberty, & will on some pretence or other claim that their case is also extraordinary; & thus it will be difficult to maintain any rule or keep any bounds in any case. To this I would reply

1. If this be a case of such a character that righteousness152 requires that I have this liberty, the fear of others claiming the [69] same without reason ought not to prevent justice from being now done to me. It is a very common thing because exceptions & peculiar liberties are granted in extraordinary cases to demand them in others where they ought not to be granted; but surely this is no good reason for not granting them in a case where it would plainly be the grossest injustice to refuse them. But not to enlarge on this point I observe.

2. That in this case, as it now stands, there can be no possible danger of a bad precedent in allowing me to go out of the county on the ground that justice imperiously requires it; because I have first submitted the decision of the matter to a Council of the neighboring churches, who are to determine whether this be a case which requires this liberty to be granted or not. So instead of its being a dangerous precedent, it is a precedent which will tend rather to scren the churches from all the inconveniences feared; because it is not an instance of going abroad for a Council in neglect & contempt of the churches of the neighborhood, but the churches of the neighborhood are first applied to & they themselves are made the judges whether the case is thus153 extraordinary or not. If we could actually form as strict & firm an establishment with regard to a limitation of Councils to a neighborhood as any of us could desire; I should think no man could desire a greater strictness than this -- that no minister or church should depart from such a strict consistory even in extraordinary cases, but with the approbation & by the allowance of that consistory. If we wished to contrive a method which should effectually prevent the mischief of extraordinary cases being made precedents [70] for ordinary ones, we could not contrive one more effectual than this - That the churches of the neighborhood themselves should be the judges of those extraordinary cases. In this way the neighborhood has all the opportunity for self preservation which it can possibly desire.

I ask the pardon of the Rev. Council for being thus particular in my argument on this subject: the case being one as may easily be seen of vast consequence to me & my family. I hope notwithstanding, that every thing which has been said will have its due weight with the Council, & that since I have submitted this question so deeply interesting to myself, to judges who are all of a contrary opinion from me, in the main controversy between the two parties between whom you are to judge that I shall experience the happy effects of your steady & unshaken integrity in your righteous determination of this important point.

When the agents for the Church had said what they thought [71] proper in reply to the preceding remarks, I154 presented to the Council the following considerations on the question - Whether the state of things is now155 ripe for a Council being called to judge, whether I shall be dismissed from my pastoral office here, or not156?

I suppose the state of things not to be157 ripe for the calling of a Council to decide on the question of dismission for the following reasons.

1. It is very apparent that my people have never158 given me a proper hearing on the great question, which is the que origin of all our present debates & difficulties, & which must be the ground of159 rejecting me from being their pastor, if ever this is160 done in the issue of the present controversy. I say the ground of their rejecting me - for if I am removed from being their pastor, they must reject me; fir they must first vote for my being removed. Though this alone will not dissolve the relation between me & them, yet as a precedent step it is indispensably requisite. But if any rules of reason or religion are to be regarded, this cannot be done untill they have first given me a fair hearing on that point which is the cause of their demanding such a dissolution. In the solemn transaction of my ordination & settlement as their pastor I on my part took the charge of their souls; & they, as in the presence of God, solemnly committed the care of their souls to me; & thus the relation which now subsists was established between me & them by mutual covenant. Hence if ever they reject me from being their pastor, & are active in withdrawing themselves from under161 my ministerial care it must be on one of these two accounts; either162 because they suppose me obviously unfit to be a minister or 2. secondly Because they suppose that I fail of performing some of the essential duties of a minister;[72] such as are made essential by Christ’s appointment. The former of these reasons has not been insisted on. If they suppose that I fail in the latter respect, & that I neglect to perform some of the essential duties of a minister; & I insist on my own justification & plead that what I do is agreeable to the word & institutions of Christ, & therefore my duty as a minister of Christ; they are bound to give me a fair & full hearing before I can be rejected by them, or they released from their sacred obligations to me as my people. Especially is this true if the point on which they insist was never to be163 settled in the Church of Christ, as to be regarded indisputable, & still more if a very great proportion of those who have been universally esteemed orthodox christians & divines have without dispute been on my side. In such a case as this what pretence can a people have for bursting the sacred bonds of their covenant with their pastor without hearing him.

If the determination of no such important public act of theirs depended on their judgment of the matter, but I merely offered to deliver the doctrine for which I insist as a part of the counsel of God; on this account alone my people would have been164 obliged, on a disputable point to give me an impartial hearing. A minister by his office is to be the guide & instructor of his people. To this end he is to study and search the scriptures & to teach the people, not the opinions of men, of other divines or of their ancestors, but the mind of Christ. As he is set to enlighten them so it is a part of his duty is to rectify their mistakes &, if he sees them out of the way of truth or duty, to be a voice behind them saying This is the way, walk ye in it." Hence, if what he offers to exhibit to them as the mind of Christ be different from their previous apprehensions, unless it be on some point which is established in the church of God as fundamental, surely [73] they are obliged to hear him. If not, there is an end at once to all the use & benefit of teachers in the Church in these respects as the means of increasing its light & knowledge, & of reclaiming it from mistakes & errors. This would be in effect to establish not the word of Christ, but the opinion of the last generation in each town & church, as an immutable rule to all future generations to the end of the world. Thus it would evidently be the duty of a people to hear165 their pastor in such a case, if no such important act of theirs with respect to him as their rejecting him from being their pastor depended upon it. But when they are proposing to withdraw themselves wholly from him, to cast off all the bonds of their covenant with him, to withhold his maintenance casting him & his family on the wide world, & to renounce all the obligations & duties which they owe him as their pastor; & their doing this depends on their judgment of the doctrine which he offers to preach to them as the mind of Christ; for them resolutely & finally to refuse so much as to give him a hearing, is one of the most flagrant instances of injustice in a people towards their pastor which perhaps has been heard of in these parts of the world.

Surely the state of things among a people cannot be regarded as ripe for such important proceedings as these till they are in a capacity to act in them understandingly and as knowing what they do. But it is obvious that this cannot be untill they have given their pastor a fair hearing; nor can they ever be regarded as having prepared themselves for thus rejecting their pastor as having no farther concern with him in a pastoral relation, until they have first discharged the debt166 due to him as their pastor; & this cannot be done untill they have heard him, untill they have heard what he offers to teach them as the [74] mind & will of Christ, have heard what he has to say in for himself in this matter wherein they are offended with him as supposing that he fails to perform the duty of a minister of Christ towards them.

This Church in its first establishment held the very principles for which I now contend, but Mr. Stoddard was was of a contrary opinion & from the pulpit he freely & abundantly delivered what he supposed to be the mind & will of God. And was it ever doubted whether the people were obliged to give him a hearing? In the days of Mr. Mather the Church was congregational in its principles & practice. Mr. Stoddard was a Presbyterian & abundantly preached his presbyterian principles; & did any body doubt of their obligation to hear him. Yea it never entered into the hearts of the people that any proceeding of theirs so important as their rejecting him from being their minister depended on their judgment of his doctrine.

That my people have never given me a fair hearing on the point of controversy between us is exceedingly apparent.

They have never generally read the work which I have published on the subject. This is publicly confessed. Only twenty copies have been procured. Many of those who might have read even these have shewed an utter aversion to reading it. One of my most strenuous opposers declares that the people are in no way to be informed of the reasons of my opinion for two years to come; while others have asserted that they are never likely to be generally informed. Numerous witnesses declare that they had already167 refused to read it & that others have said that they would not even let the book come into their houses. If the Council are at a loss on this point & any one should call it in question [75] I suppose there are numbers present who can say enough with regard to it to satisfy any reasonable person. From the narrative which has been given the Council also see how stiffly & inflexibly the people Committee168 have ever declined hearing the reasons of my opinion from the pulpit, when proposed from time to time before the printing of my book & since; & how they have been so much engaged in the matter that they have repeatedly refused to have the question whether it was reasonable & best that I should preach upon the subject169 to the neighboring ministers or to any Council whatsoever. Before the publication of my pamphlet they would not consent that I should preach on the ground that it was best I should publish; & now I have published they will not read.

Finally though I have often desired it they have never given me an opportunity to state the reasons of my opinion even in private conversation.

Thus the great part of my people have refused to give me any hearing at all, or to suffer themselves to be informed in any way whatsoever of the reasons of my opinion. Nay, they have industriously guarded against it, as what they have so much dreaded, that they would by no means allow of any advice, or any thing which they thought might open a door for it; but have been engaged to have me hastily thrust out before there should be any farther opportunity for the people to be better170 informed least it should make proselytes171 to my sentiments & thus divide the people & make parties among them. If this be not a violent proceeding for a christian Church toward their pastor I never expect to know what is. Herein they have not only violated the rules of christian charity & gentleness & acted inconsistently with moral righteousness, but [76] very inconsistently with themselves. They themselves from the beginning have implicitly acknowledged that I had a right to be heard by them, & that172 the people should in some way or other be informed of the reasons of my opinion. If there was no need of this, then what need of their desiring me to print my reasons, & what need of their waiting some time for the impression173; & what need of their meeting together in July to see if they should wait any longer for my book; and what need if their desiring Col. Dwight to enquire when the books were likely to be done & to send them word & what need of their determining if the books were likely to come speedily that they would wait for them & what need after the books came of their forbearing to act for about two months.

It may be said that although the people in general have not read my pamphlet, yet most of the leading men in the church have read it. To this I answer; If I have a right to be heard by some I have a right to be heard by the body of the people. What need of any being informed if there be no need of the people in general being informed. If one can be justified in refusing to hear or read why not another; & why may not every one be justified in refusing to hear or read a word. And thus why might not all174 the people have thrust me out immediately as soon as they knew my opinion without giving me any opportunity to print or speak any thing for myself. The controversy on this subject is between me & the Church & not between me & the leading men of the Church; & if I have any right to be heard at all it is by them with whom I have this controversy. It is not merely the leading men but all the brethren who are to have a hand in the act of [77] the Church which must make way for a dissolution of my pastoral relation with them if it be dissolved. They have lately expressly & deliberately refused to leave the government of the Church to the leading men in a public formal consideration of the matter; but would have it in the hands of the whole society. If others beside the leading men are not set aside as cyphers in acting with regard to my being turned away, they ought not to be aside as cyphers in learning & judging. The grand reason continually urged & insisted on why my reasons should not be heard from the pulpit has been, that there was danger of its making parties in the town. Now I beseech the Rev. Council to consider for a moment what sort of an objection this is -- The very reason why it is thought just that a person, in a cause in which he is liable to suffer, should be allowed to plead his own cause is that he may have a fair opportunity before he suffers to convince others that his cause is good. What a strange reason then is it, why a man in such a case should not be allowed to plead his own cause & why his plea should not be heard - lest some of those to whom he offers his plea should be convinced that his cause is good. So unreasonable is this opinion that the very end of a man’s pleading his cause & the very thing which is the sole ground & reason why it is accounted fair & just that a man should be allowed to plead his own cause viz. a fair opportunity to convince others that his cause is good - is by the people made the main objections why I should not plead my own cause - viz. because if I do, there will be an opportunity, & so a possibil=[78] ity, of convincing some that my cause is good.

The case would be the same if it were a cause on the issue of which my life depended175. This discussion might occasion parties & unhappy contentions. This is not only possible but often has actually been the case with regard to the execution of persons in a public capacity. How often have cities & nations been set into a ferment on such occasions. Must we therefore say that the suspected person shall have have no hearing because the people -- the judges on whose voice176 his life depends are united in condemning him;177 there is danger of their being divided if he is allowed to speak for himself.

And besides my people in the very making of this objection are condemned out of their own mouths. The objection in the very terms of it, is an implicit acknowledgement, that there has been as yet no sufficient trial what the minds of the people would be on a fair & full hearing of what I have to say for myself. Yea a confession that they suspect & that very strongly, that the opinion of many if I should have a full hearing would be far otherwise than now. For, if not, how would the people after hearing me be divided into parties any more than they are now ? But if as they thus confess there has been sufficient trial what the minds of the people would be after a full hearing, certainly there ought to be a sufficient trial & they ought not to strive to hinder it; for in striving to hinder it they do directly & avowedly strive to have me condemned & turned out of the ministry & with my family deprived of maintenance without a fair & proper178 trial;[79] which is certainly the most barefaced injustice. But it may be asked - Why didn’t you preach,179 why did you not use your right, without waiting for the consent of the people ? -- To this question I have several answers.

1. When for the sake of peace, I have repeatedly proposed to the people that, with their consent, I would preach upon the subject, they have, as I just observed uniformly refused it; & have also from time to time even to submit the point whether it was reasonable I should preach upon it to the neighboring ministers. After such refusals the question should scarcely be asked180.

2. The state of the people has been most obviously such that if I had taken any opportunity on the Sabbath without their previous consent, it would have been the occasion of tumult on that holy day to the extreme dishonor of Christ & wounding the interests of religion. Noise & uproar have risen to such a height already, that I cannot think it was my duty or that it would have become the prudence & moderation of a minister of the Gospel. It would have had the appearance the of great strenuousness; & I thought it better that the people should first be prepared by the advice of a Council which would greatly tend to prevent the mischievous effects.

3. If I had appointed lecture there is181 not the least probability that the people would have attended them. Of this the Rev. Council will be satisfied by considering the account they have had of their conduct.

On the whole I thought it the most prudent course to wait for a more favorable opportunity: It may be said that the people are the more to be [80] justified in rejecting me & turning me out from my office, without hearing me because I was settled on the contrary principles.

I answer that this objection can be of no force unless they mean by it that I settled on Mr. Stoddard’s judgement as my rule. If I did I did not settle as a minister of Christ, but as a minister of Mr. Stoddard. Even if it had been so, that I had settled in this manner on Mr. Stoddard’s principles this was one which I found among his principles which he expresses in one of his works in these words -- "He who believes principles because our forefathers affirm makes idols of them; & it would be no humility but baseness of spirit, for us to judge ourselves incapable of examining principles which have been handed down to us. If we are any wise fit to open the mysteries of the Gospel we are capable of judging in these matters."---

It was implied in my ordination vows that I would study the Scriptures; that I would make the word of God, & not the word of any man, my rule in teaching my people, & that I would do my utmost to know what was the counsel of God, & to declare it. This was implied in my covenant with God & the people at my settlement; & it was implied in their covenant with me God & with me that in my so doing they would diligently & impartially hear & examine what I should offer to them as the counsel of God.

It is said that Mr. Stoddard would never have consented to my settling here, if he had foreseen that I should so differ from him in my principles. To this it is sufficient to reply, that he[81] doubtless would have been as much against it if he had foreseen in me any such departure from his principles as has actually taken place in the church since his death with regard to church discipline. I had as much reason given me by the Church at182 my settlement, to depend upon it that they would allow me the same power in Church government which they183 yielded to Mr. Stoddard; as they had to depend upon it that I would allow them the same open door to the Lord’s table. The Church allowed Mr. Stoddard a negative; & never so far as I have heard of, disputed it, at least never in the present184 generation. Now they greatly find fault with me for claiming it, and have departed to the length of Brownism. They have as properly departed from the principles on which they settled me, as I have departed from those on which I accepted a settlement.

If the objection should arise in the minds of any of the members of the Rev. Council,--That if I should be allowed to preach my doctrine to my people, there might be some danger of infecting neighboring churches I hope I need not say much in answer to such an objection. Plain justice must not be hindered & suppressed for fear of some imagined accidental inconveniences. The wiser Heathen could say "Fiat justitia, ruat coelum."-- The neighboring ministers have as much liberty to preach & defend their principles among their people as I desire to have among mine & can do it with far greater advantages than I expect to enjoy. Doubtless they would take this liberty & would take it ill if any one should attempt to restrain them. And I trust they are very willing to do to others as they wish others to do to them.

II. That the state of things is not ripe for calling the propos=[82] ed Council is apparent from the frame & temper of mind which my people have hitherto been in, & especially of late. I am sensible that an Ecclesiastical Council in their advice are not to proceed by any uncertain conjectures concerning the secrets of mens hearts. But yet in adapting their advice to the state of a people they doubtless are to have185 regard to those things which are visible & notorious. What the temper of the minds of this people, at least of the governing part of them, has been hitherto & especially of late, has been as manifest as any thing concerning the state of a people can be. It cannot be hid. It must needs be visible to all around us. It is manifest not only from the customary conversation of the people in private houses but from the whole tenor of their public proceedings -- from the methods which have been taken, from the measures adopted, from the proceedings of Church meetings, & Precinct meetings, & their Committee, from the speeches which have been publicly made & the acts which have been publicly done. It would occupy a great deal of time to set forth all the particulars. But this is needless; as the Council has heard the Narrative of our proceedings.186

The temper which the people have manifested, I humbly conceive, ought to be the more observed by the Rev. Council & to have the greater influence on their determination because I have never offered this people any provocation, unless yielding & condescending, & taking the utmost care to avoid offending them has a provocation. I have sought peace & pursued it & have striven to the187 utmost to avoid occasions of strife. I have clogged them in any reasonable proceeding in this affair though against myself. [83] I told them long ago, even at the very first interview with the Church that if they insisted on calling a council immediately, who should have power to finish our whole controversy, I would not oppose or hinder it, though I could not advise to it. I have yielded to them from time to time in every thing wherein I could do it with a clear conscience. That after examining the subject by the aid of the sacred scriptures in the best manner I am able, I have adopted & still hold the sentiments which I have publicly professed, with regard to the qualifications for full communion in the Visible Church;-- & that too with the fullest expectation of being driven from my ministerrial office & stripped of a maintenance for my numerous family -- I admit.-- Whether in all this I have acted in the fear of God, with a good conscience & in the integrity of my heart the188 Rev. Council may judge. This one thing excepted, if it be an exception I have given my people no sort of occasion in any respect whatsoever for any violent proceeding or the least vehemence; unless yielding & submitting for peace sake be just warrant for189 insulting me the more. For evidence of all this I appeal to the Narrative of our proceedings which has been read here publicly in the hearing of you all.

Now I think the temper & frame of mind which my people discover in the way of taking the important step of dissolving the relation between me & them for the present; & that on two accounts:

1. Such a temper & frame is manifestly utterly inconsistent with a proper & just hearing & considering the reasons which I have to offer for myself in that thing which is the grand controversy between me & them. So that if it could [84] be proved that they had all read my book through, which it is apparent they are far from having done, yet merely in this they do not discharge themselves. They ought to give a fair hearing at least with some degree of calmness, & candor & coolness of consideration; but from facts, which are open & public it is evident that they have been notoriously far from it. Hearing in a high degree of fermentation of mind manifested by continued outward manifestation irregularity & precipitation of proceeding, is no fair hearing, & ought not to stand for any thing, or to be regarded as any hearing at all by wise & just judges. Hence it is most plain that my people are now bound to give me a fair hearing, before they can justly demand a dissolution of my pastoral relation; & with equal clearness is it manifest that means must first be used with them to bring them to another temper of mind any such demand can be heard or accepted.

2. It is190 itself utterly unfit and unbecoming in a christian church to proceed to an affair so deeply affecting their spiritual welfare as the dismission of their pastor in such a temper of mind: especially of one who has been so long their pastor, & between whom & them such a state of things has subsisted as between me & this people. The Apostle says to the Church of Corinth, "Let all things be done with charity"; -- & surely it is exceedingly191 unbecoming Churches of the Lamb of God to manage their religious affairs of the greatest importance in a ferment and tumult; which ought to be managed with great solemnity, deep humiliation, submission to the awful frowns of Heaven & humble dependence on God, & with [85] fervent prayer & supplication. But for a church to undertake such an affair in such a manner as this, will be most unbecoming the Gospel, greatly to the dishonor of God & religion & eminently calculated to prevent the divine blessing.

The Rev. Council will also perceive that the consequences of my being driven away in this manner will be in many respects exceedingly pernicious.

1. It will192 be a great & most extensive injury to the credit and interest of religion. For the story to be circulated that the people of Northampton, a people heretofore so often & remarkably distinguished by the divine favour, drove away their minister in the midst of so much heat & contention, I need not say how it will wound religion abroad.

2. It will be a great wrong to this; not only as they will thereby bring guilt upon193 themselves; but will exceedingly wound their own reputation & interest in the country, & render difficult the future settlement of the ordinances of the Gospel among them.

3. It will be a great injury to me.

4. If the people are countenanced in these measures by a Council so far as to advise to such an issue which the people seek with such a temper & in such a tumultuous manner; it will in its consequences be a great injury to other churches and ministers as it will directly encourage similar proceedings in case of differences between minister & people. This case is likely to be very famous, the eyes of the whole country x194 are greatly drawn upon it, to observe the issue & management of it. It will be likely to be long remembered, & will there fore be so much the more likely to be of extensive & lasting influence as a precedent.

Hence I humbly conceive that there is no ripeness in the present state of[86] things for any immediate measures in order to bring about this event, unless violence of spirit & of conduct be regarded as195 ripeness of a christian church for managing their religious concerns of the most solemn nature & of the greatest importance. Indeed this seems to be the notion which many of the Church have had of such a ripeness from their earnestly driving the matter at the last Precinct meeting to have me voted out of town before this Council assembled i.e. to have it voted that the people desired that I should be gone, so that the people Council might see that they were ripe. But I suppose the196 Rev. Council have greater wisdom than to entertain the same notion of a ripeness for such a proceeding; & I humbly conceive that they will see it to be indispensably necessary that first the utmost endeavours be used to bring the people to juster views & a better temper; before they advise to any steps in order to an immediate separation. And I beseech the Council to use their utmost & most prudent endeavours that if finally we are separated, we may part with one another fairly & peaceably.

I also request of the197 Rev. Council that they will198 do me the justice in their Result,--not merely to advise the people disjunctively, either to read my book or to hear my reasons from the pulpit; but to give it as their opinion that I have a right to preach, & that if I do preach, the people are obliged to hear me. I humbly conceive that this will be no more than the case requires for three reasons

1. if such disjunctive advice be left, it will not tend so much to ripen in any measure to ripen our affairs for an issue; for then indeed they will neither hear me [87] preach, nor be likely to read my book. It will be said that the number of copies is small. The disinclination is great. It will be supposed that the Council do not wish a very strict scrutiny whether they have read it or not. The whole matter will be left at very199 loose ends & in great uncertainty. Thus it will tend greatly to embarrass our affairs & lengthen out our controversy.

2. My people complain, many of them, that on this subject they cannot understand me. Now if unawares I have spoken to them a language they cannot understand, let me have an opportunity to explain myself. Surely it will not be proceeding with christian moderation & charity, - to say at once, "You have been mistaken in your manner of pleading your cause you have spoken to us inadvertently, so that we have wholly misapprehended your meaning; but since you have thus failed of making us understand you, your mouth shall now be stopped, & we will give you no farther opportunity to speak for yourself."

3. I ought, before I leave this people, to whom I have so long stood in such a relation to have an opportunity given me to leave with them a testimony for myself in that matter which proves so great an offence to them not only with those who are in the church but with others both male & female to whom I have stood in such a relation to have an oppor= tunity of whom it can never be expected that they should generally read my pamphlet. The laws of nature & the laws of Christ require me to love this people to whom I have been so related & to value their charity & esteem. I have reason also to think that there are many of my spiritual children,200 in this congregation who now entertain hard thoughts on account of my opinion. Now I ought not to be driven from hence without opportunity to exhibit a testimony for [88] myself before them & so with the people at large. When I have done so, I demand nothing of them but an impartial hearing. I desire not to lord it over their consciences. They have a right to judge for themselves & may use what means201 to see the strength of the arguments on the other side, they please by reading books or conversing with ministers who differ with202 me in judgment.

I humbly trust therefore, that this Rev. Council will not fail to leave behind, in their Result, a direct & full expression of their judgment on this important point. On Wednesday Feb. 7, 1750 the Council met again, & the subject of the Pastor’s going out of the County, in the choice of his part of the Decisive Council, was again very largely de=[93] bated before them, by the Pastor, & the Committee of the Church, & also by some private members of the Church. My own remarks upon the subject were as follows.212

If I should attempt to prove that a vicinity of churches have no jurisdiction over particular churches within their bounds established by a universal unalterable rule which ought never to be dispensed with, in any case whatever; I presume the Rev. Council would regard the attempt as wholly impertinent a needless burdening them with proofs of what nobody would ever dispute. I shall therefore take it for granted unless it should213 be questioned that the rule of confining Councils to a vicinity is only a general rule from which exceptions are to be made in cases especially requiring it. Hence the only question is whether that be the case or not.214

In order to determine this question with clearness & certainty we must, as I observed at the session of the Council in December, previously ascertain what will be the business of the proposed Future Council. The business obviously must determine the qualifications; & if on a strict comparison of business & qualifications, it be found that a different Council is really requisite from that which may be constituted of churches of the vicinity; then it will follow that a different Council must be allowed & cannot be denied; & that, whatever may be said of any customs of churches, or of any parallel customs with regard to civil tribunals; the nature of this particular case must be looked into, & that, & that alone must determine the matter. What the nature of the case requires, that the law of reason requires & justice requires, & that the Law of God requires.

If I may be allowed to recapitulate very briefly some of the remarks then made in order to refresh the memories of [94] the Council I observe that the business of the future Council will be not to decide, Whether my opinion, on the qualifications for communion is right or not - because we know the opinion and practice of every man who will be chosen, before he comes. Nor is it215 whether I shall remain the minister of Northampton if, after all proper steps are taken to effect an accommodation the people still desire my dismission -- because, when that shall have been done I will trouble no Council any farther than barely to give me leave to relinquish my Pastoral office. But their business will be to decide: 1. Whether they ought not to make some attempts to effect an accommodation of our difficulties; & if this decided in the negative, 2. Whether all has been done by all216 parties, which justice requires to be done, previous to a separation; & if this be decided in the affirmative to state in their Result. 3. What are the grounds of my dismission, how far I am innocent, & how far I may be recommended as deserving future employment in the ministry. If this Rev. Council will closely consider the matter they will easily see that these things must constitute their business.

And if this be so, the question, What qualifications does this business require ? amounts to simply this -- Is impartiality, as to the two contending parties, a qualification absolutely necessary in those who are to judge between them? -- To determine this, let each of the points which must be submitted to the future Council, be viewed. If means of accommodation are to be used, ought not those who are to act as mediators, to be in the middle between the parties to be reconciled. If they are to judge with regard to the mutual rights & claims of the two parties, & to decide whether each has done all that the other may fairly require previous to a separation; [95] does not settling points of equity, between two parties at variance, require even balances. And if they are to pronounce before the world, on the conduct of the pastor in this controversy as well as on his general character; may he not justly demand that the tribunal which is to do this shall be impartial?

If these things are plain, & I cannot but think that every person of sober thought will own them to be self- evident; then the only things to be determined are these two 1. What constitutes an essential defect of impartiality as to the two parties in this case ?

2. Whether the defect can be supposed to belong to a Council constituted of the Churches in this vicinity ? As to the first point, it must be obviously be admitted to be a radical defect in the impartiality of the Council, if the members of it are all on one side, or are all known to side with one of the parties against the other, on the main point in controversy. What can be more plain217 that a balance cannot be even, & therefore cannot be fit to adjust matters of equity between two parties, if all the previous weight is in one scale. And is it not equally evident that a Council, who are all on one side in the controversy, are not in the middle between the two parties, as mediators to reconcile them. And since a Council cannot in this case be obtained that is impartial as to individuals, because all the members are chosen, & chosen with reference to their opinions; it is plain that the consistory ought to be so constituted, that one part may balance the other. If we cannot find a balance which has no previous weight in either of the scales, yet surely we should seek one which has not all the weights in one scale [96] but equal weights in both, that the balance may be even. I need not remark again that mankind218 both the wise & unwise, are liable to the strongest prejudices against the persons & conduct of those around them, who differ from them in matters of religion; that this is as true with regard to points not fundamental, as with regard to those which are; that it is especially true, when the controversy is at the height of agitation; that it is preeminently true with regard to those who are the movers & managers of the controversy; & that nothing from age to age has been found to excite prejudices equally strong with this. These points are too clear to admit of denial or doubt. Hence if the future Council be all one side, as to the main controversy between me & my people, it is an apparent & essential219 defect of impartiality; & of course it is most unrighteous to confine the other party to such a Council & oblige him to be judged by them & no other.

As to the other question, Will a Council taken wholly from the County have this defect; it is a fact perfectly known that the ministers & Churches of this County are almost universally against me on the point, which now divides me & my people & makes us two parties. Perhaps there may be one or two ministers who are partly of my mind; but then their churches are all of a contrary mind & on the same side with my people. I suppose that there is not more than one minister fully of my mind with respect to the Sacrament of the Lord’s Supper & not one Church of my mind with regard to either of the sacraments. Of course a Council cannot be obtained wholly from within the County, without the forementioned essential defect.

It having been220 established as I humbly conceive, that the future [97] Council cannot be qualified for the business which will be laid before it, unless it is strictly impartial; that an impartial Council cannot be chosen, unless I am permitted to select from beyond the limits of the County of Hampshire; & that to deny me this liberty therefore would be direct & palpable injustice; I shall proceed to remark on some of the objections which are brought against this measure.

It was urged at the former sitting of the Council- That if there actually is a jurisdiction over particular churches & ministers established in a vicinity, the reason alleged cannot be a sufficient reason for an exception; & that if there is no such jurisdiction actually established by agreement, yet if there ought to be it alters not the case. For if it ought to be established we may fairly look upon it as really established in the law of reason; &, if so, this case has nothing in it to require an exception from such a jurisdiction any more than a case growing out of any other error fundamental or nonfundamental. If a Church should complain of its minister for turning Arminian, & all the ministers & Churches were Calvinists, it is said he ought not therefore to221 go from the vicinity, to get half of the Council to be222 of his opinion, in order that it might be impartial. -- In answer to this objection I would observe,

1. That in order to judge of its force the business of the future Council must be kept in mind. And I hope it will be remembered that the business of the Council will not be to try me with regard to my opinion respecting the qualifications for communion, to find me guilty or not223 or to justify or condemn that opinion. The fact that a differ=[98] ence of opinion subsists between the pastor & the Church, will indeed be taken into consideration, as well as the question, whether on the whole it is best224 we should be separated; but doubtless they would regard it as useless & impertinent, to try me on the question, whether this opinion is heresy or not In such a case they would be called to try a minister as a delinquent, to examine the fact & openly to censure the doctrine.

If this were the business of the Council it might not225 make a great difference as to the manner in which it ought to be constituted for the case of such a minister would be like the case of a person accused of some crime for which he was to be tried. In such a case the impartiality requisite is impartiality as to the fact, but not as to the nature of the crime. Thus, if a man were accused of drunkenness, the judges ought to be strictly impartial as to the question,226 Whether drunkenness be a crime. The crime they ought to abhor, and such abhorrence renders them not the worse but the better judges. Virtue of course prejudices men against vice; & the more virtuous judges are,227 the more zealously opposed to vice, the fitter are they to be judges of vicious persons! Hence in such a case it would be ridiculous in228 the accused to insist that half his judges should be men who approved drunkenness & that, whether there were any established jurisdiction or not. And it would not alter the case whether it were proposed that his judges should be of the vicinity or brought from some other continent. From whatever places collected they ought in229 strict justice to be men, who had an entire abhorrence of the crime of drunkenness.

The seeming force of this objection arises from a confusion of thought in those [99] who were[?] it in losing sight of the real point in question in forgetting the proper business of the future Council, & inadvertently supposing it to be like that of judges who are called to try a criminal. The question of fact whether I hold a given opinion will not come up before them: It is admitted beforehand. Neither will the question whether that opinion be heresy: no man pretends it. That opinion will not be presented to them as a crime or fault to be judged & punished; but merely as the ground of an alleged difference of opinion between pastor & people. It is not on the merits of the cause i.e. of my opinion on which230 they will decide but on the case of difficulty growing out of a difference of opinions231; & as was observed before, they will be called to act as mediators between the disagreeing parties, to settle matters of equity between them, & to judge of the character & conduct of the pastor with regard to the controversy. And with regard to all these points it has been shown that to be on either side has a most obvious232 tendency to bias the mind against the other.

That the seeming force of this objection in the minds of those who urge it arises from losing sight of the true state of the case, & the proper business of the Council is obvious from the very example adduced by way of illustration, viz. That in a civil action it is no valid objection against the justices of the vicinity, who have an established jurisdiction, that their opinion on a given point of law is already known: - because in the present case the business of the future Council will not be, to try the merits of the cause, or to judge whether my opinion be agreeable to the Law, i.e.233 the word of God or not. And with respect to this example I would further observe the following things.

1. If it were really so that the proposed Council were to judge the very merits of my cause i.e.234 the soundness of my opinion, the [100] instance adduced would not be at all parallel or of any force in the present argument. Let the case be put thus: Suppose a man has done something towards a given individual which many regard as a breach of Law exposing him to be disenfranchised & the question turns on a point of Law which has long been matter of warm controversy between235 judges & jurists; & suppose there is no stated tribunal but it is the custom of the Country in cases of controversy for each party to choose half of the judges; & it is known that there are as many jurists on one side in the controverted point of Law as on the other, & there is no appeal from the tribunal chosen but their judgment will be final; would it not be reasonable in this case, if one party chose half of the judges favorable to his side of the question that the other should choose the other half favorable to his side; & that, although all the jurists of the immediate vicinity were opposed to him. If one party had actually chosen his half of the judges who were all on his side, would it not be mere mockery to tell the other party that he also might have the liberty of choosing half of the judges, as well as his adversary, but only he must choose all of them from the side which were opposed to him. Now this is precisely my case. Councils are elective & to be appointed by joint or mutual choice according to uniform practice. This has been the practice of both parts of this County. It was pursued in the lower part of the County in the case of Mr. Allis; & in the upper part of the County in the case of Mr. Rawson, & in the Council of May 3, 1737, of which I was the scribe & have the original papers now by me, as well as in the subsequent Council convened at the same place. My people236 do not pretend that any stated consis=[101] tory exists or that the Council is not to be elective they offer me the choice of one half of the Council, but only would confine me to Churches & ministers of their opinion.

2. In civil affairs appeals are allowed from the justices of the County to others who come from a distance appointed without any regard to vicinity; & the determination of those more remote judges supersedes & sets aside that of the judges of237 the vicinity. Indeed many important are carried directly to these more distant judges without suffering the judges from the vicinity to meddle with them any further than to refer them to the judgment of the distant judges; & that too on account of the great importance of the case. And so it would here in our ecclesiastical affairs if we had regular inferior & superior tribunals like those of Scotland.

3. Difference of opinion on a mere point of law has very little tendency to prejudice the mind against the persons & conduct of others, compared with difference of opinion in matters of religion. No one will dispute the fact that the latter has in all ages excited the deepest prejudices in mankind against each other. But who ever heard of such prejudices, & alienations in individuals, in parties, & in nations, merely because they differed in opinion on point of law.

4. Civil tribunals are not appointed to act the part of mediators between contending parties; except in cases of arbitration in which each party has equal liberty of choice. Their office is to see that the laws be executed; & there is not therefore the same necessity that there should be some of the judges on each side as in the present case.

But to return to the objection itself. It is asked, if a minister [102] should be complained of by his people for embracing Arianism or any other heresy or for turning to another denomination for example to the Church of England & a Council should be required to adjust the difficulty why might he not in such a case as well as in the present insist on the liberty of going out of the County to get half of the Council who embraced the same heresy, or who belonged to the Church of England, that they might be impartial? To this I answer,

1. 238The Council would not come together to consider the question whether the individual, if he had embraced the alleged heresy or had changed to another denomination might be lawfully continued as the minister of a Congregational Church. This point is settled beforehand. They would come simply to find whether the charge against him was true or false. Hence I suppose that the following will be found on the most careful inquiry to be the reasons & the only reasons why he could not claim to have some of the judges of his own side.

(1.) Because in the case of acknowledged heresy, those who are on his side are not fit to be members of the Christian Church. Fundamental errors are scandalous; & the Church cannot therefore consistently with their own profession call such as constituent members of a christian Council & leave their ecclesiastical affairs with those who embrace them for those239 who are not duly qualified to be members of a Christian Church cannot be fit members of a christian Council, to direct & manage the affairs of the christian Church. Or, (2.) because in the case of turning to a different denomination, that of the Episcopalians or the Anabaptists the individual is now statedly of a different denomination [103] communion. For although christians of these denominations may occasionally & transiently join with Presbyterians & Congregationalists in some parts of worship; yet as to what is stated, there is a division openly established. It is a point perfectly settled that, as to their stated240 & their ecclesiastical proceedings, they must act apart. And there would be an obvious inconsistency in a church employing those who are already of a distinct sect, & have no ecclesiastical connexion with them to order & settle their ecclesiastical affairs.

These I suppose to be the only reasons, why it is not proper that a minister, who embraces heresy, other or joins another denomination, cannot choose from his own side half of the Council, which is convened cannot choose from his own side half of the Council not to judge of his doctrines, or to mediate, or to241 do justice between the parties, but to investigate an alleged fact & on finding it to vacate the office; unless in such a case as that of embracing Popery, there might be this additional reason against Papists being allowed to sit on the Council. That Papists are bound to injure, persecute & destroy the Protestant Church as much as in them lies & we cannot be bound to entrust our affairs to those whose avowed design it is beforehand to injure & destroy us.

2. The reason why a minister in such a case may not go out of the vicinity to get half of the council of his own side is not the existence of any established jurisdiction or of242 established rule respecting the vicinity or the County but of a totally different nature, as will appear from a slight examination. Suppose a minister settled over an orthodox church to turn Arian, & all the churches & ministers243 to be professed Arians, it is admitted not only [104] that the church are not bound to the vicinity but that the minister has no right to choose from it, because the vicinity are all unfit to be of any Council. So if he should turn Episcopalian & all the vicinity are Episcopalians it is a point conceded that the Church is not limited to the vicinity & that the minister cannot choose a single member of the Council from the vicinity. Vicinity therefore has no alteration in the cases; which demonstrates the objection to be wholly without force for it plainly shows that it is not any established right in the vicinity, which is regarded in the cases alleged. On the contrary it is the nature of the case in itself considered, & that alone which governs in this matter, & sets aside all considerations of vicinity; which is the very point which244 I have asserted & urged. For I have insisted all along that the nature of this case as it is in itself, must be considered & what that demands must be allowed as paramount to the bare circumstance of neighborhood. So that this objection carefully examined, oversets the position it is brought to prove & establishes & demonstrates the grand point on which I insist.

3. It is the natural right of the people, & their liberty of conscience, & not vicinity which governs in the instantces alleged. But in the present case as it is not an established jurisdiction in the vicinity, so it is not the rights of conscience which should confine me to the County; & therefore nothing at all. It cannot be the rights of conscience which confines245 me, for it is as much against the rights of conscience for me to choose ministers of my own opinion in the County as out of it. The thing which invades the rights of conscience, if they are invaded [105] at all is not the circumstance of nearness or remoteness, but my having a part of the Council of my opinion whether brought from the vicinity or elsewhere. But it has never been disputed nor246 questioned, that I have a right to choose half of the ministers & churches of my opinion, if I can find so many within the County. And my people have urged that some of the ministers in the County are of my opinion & that I may choose them as far as they go though I must not go out of it the County. Now let us turn the tables & suppose that all the churches & ministers in the County were of my opinion would I not have liberty to choose my half of the Council from the vicinity, any rights of conscience in the people to the contrary notwithstanding ? Certainly all who would allow me any liberty of choice at all must admit this. So that it is in effect granted that it is neither any right of jurisdiction in the vicinity nor any rights of conscience in my people which ought to confine me to the limits of the County in my choice. And what then I ask, besides these two things can be conceived of which ought thus to confine me ? - I am not aware of any fallacy in this reasoning, & I wish it to be examined to the bottom.

4. If there be any such thing as an established jurisdiction in the churches of the vicinity, either by argument or the word of God, or the nature of things; then it will follow that all our ecclesiastical judicatories must be stated & not elective. For if there be a settled power or of jurisdiction then the subject of this power or the subject 247in which it inheres, is also settled. If it be settled at all, it is settled somewhere or in some subject; [106] & that subject of course is not left at loose ends, to be determined from time to time by the choice of the parties concerned. But such an established jurisdiction as this, has not been pretended or248 acted upon - either by this church, or by any of the neighboring churches or ministers; but the churches & ministers249 have proceeded on a contrary principle, very lately in various instances; for they have acted in Councils convoked by election without an objection or even an intimation that any established judicatory of the neighborhood ought to have been convoked. Why then should any such establishment be first mentioned & insisted on in my case; where, as we have seen it works the grossest injustice? That usually it is convenient & proper that Councils should be of the neighborhood, I freely acknowledge; but that there has been any such uniform usage as establishes the right now for the first time pretended, I utterly deny; & from those who assert it I unhesitatingly demand the evidence. No such established jurisdiction250 either by agreement or custom or the law of God, or the law of reason - was ever maintained either in our days or the days of our forefathers. In the days of our predecessors in the ministry in this County, nothing like it was known; for Mr. Stoddard & Mr. Williams of Hatfield formerly went, when invited to a Council at Norwich in Connecticut, & if I mistake not to another Council at Lebanon; which surely they would not have done if they had thought the law of God & nature settled such an establishment in vicinities. And if any such thing be now insisted on in order to limit me to the vicinity, it will in fact be a new rule251 invented for my [---][107] case to deprive me of my plain rights - in a case which has much more that is peculiar to require an exception from such a rule, than other cases in which it has never been pretended.

If it should be insisted that the Council ought not to be elective at all; but that we should take the churches as they come, or that we should take the whole county -- though this is a point not referred to this Council -- I observe that it will be the first instance of252 a minister certainly during the present generation, &, as I suppose, since the county was formed. In the case of Mr. Allis the Council was by just253 choice, as was that in the case of Mr. Rawson. In the latter of which I was the scribe the question was whether Mr. R. was qualified for the work of the ministry - on account254 of his learning, his orthodoxy & his morals; the Council was called by mutual election; Mr. Williams of Hatfield & Mr. Chauncey were members; & no person thought of objecting against the mode of convocation as irregular &255 improper. In the second Council in the case of Mr. Rawson, the churches of the Association were indeed convoked; yet it was by a free mutual agreement on prudential considerations merely, & not with reference to any supposed rule binding them to it; as I know from a particular & full enquiry. Elective Councils & not stated ones have hitherto been made use of in all parts of the County both in settling & in unsettling ministers. Very lately, Mr. Webster was settled in the upper part of the County, by an elective Council, & the election was not confined to the County for only two or three were taken from this County to join others from abroad; although it was known beforehand that it was a matter of dispute whether he ought to be settled. Hence most evidently if it be now insisted256 as a [108] rule, that the members of a Council must be of the County it will be a new rule introduced for my sake.

And if my Church should now insist upon it, which they have never yet done, that we should convoke a Council of all the churches of the County without distinction it would be peculiarly injurious in my case, not only because they embrace the opposite opinion257 in the present controversy but because it is well known that numbers of them have manifested a particular dislike of me - some, on account of258 supposed rigidness of my Calvinism; some, a dislike more directly personal in consequence of my disapproving of their settlement; & some in consequence of the late religious disputes which have arisen respecting the late religious excitement throughout New England. These dislikes I do not wish to revive & establish by being obliged to object against them by name as improper members of a Council in my case. Still such things may most reasonably to be considered in the present affair.

With regard to the objection that this Church 35 years ago voted to be subject to a Council of the churches of the County, -- if it has any seeming force, I desire that it may be brought to the test of an exact inquiry in order to determine whether it is now obligatory on the church of the present generation or whether it is null & void. It is certainly one or the other. It is either in force for the existing pastor & members or it is not. It is either alive or dead. If it be alive & in force, then the whole of it is alive; but if it be not alive then it is dead as to every part of it; & we are obliged to observe none of its provisions, except as the laws of God & nature independently of that vote bind us to observe them. Now the question whether that vote is dead or alive ? is [109] easily resolved & depends on the resolution of another question viz. Whether, on Protestant principles, the determination of Ancestors as to matters of religion & the worship of God binds future generations, without their consent either express or implied ? The present members of this Church at least nine out of ten are a new generation. As to any express confirmation of that vote it is notorious that there has been no such thing. As to any implied confirmation of it, if there has been any, it must have been by conforming to it in practice. But this to my knowledge has not been done by the present church of Northampton. Since I have been their pastor they have uniformly in their practice implicitly rejected & annulled it & that not merely in circumstantials, but in the substance & main scope of it. The thing mentioned in the vote is a Council of Churches & not of ministers. The first association of ministers in this county was formed259 after my settlement. Yet this church never convoked a Council of Churches in this generation untill now. The main design of that vote too was to have a stated judicatory & not an elective council according to the presbyterian principles of Mr. Stoddard; yet this main design has never been regarded but wholly counteracted. So that it is evident to a demonstration that this church now has no such constitution, & that the ancient Church vote is fact dead. The neighboring ministers & churches also have customarily neglected & counteracted that old argument of some of our forefathers. - If it is now held to bind this church it will be the first time during the present generation. It will be revived out of the dust purely for my sake, after it has been long dead & buried; which will be tantamount to making a new260 constitution to meet my individual case. If that vote [110] be really no establishment for us, then I am bound by no part of it; &, if so, no argument ought to be drawn from it with regard to any thing either less or more pertaining to the present case.

It is objected that the present Council can have no supposed261 reference to any supposed business of the future Council except that mentioned in the votes of the church to judge whether pastor & people shall be separated; & that they are simply to determine what sort of Council is proper to do that business. To this I answer,

1. The business expressly mentioned in the Vote will not depend on judging the correctness of my opinion respecting262 the qualifications for communion, but on a judgment of the rights of the people as to liberty of conscience, & of the rights of the pastor as to what he can fairly demand of the people & so will consist in settling points of justice between the parties.

2. The business expressly mentioned in the vote, does certainly imply what is necessarily connected with it, considering the state of the case; & so must therefore be considered by this Council as implied in that business. Considered thus it includes all the three things which were first mentioned. If a surgeon is sent for to judge whether a limb shall be amputated, the message implies that he is to judge whether amputation may not be dispensed with; if not whether it shall take place immediately or be postponed; & if so in what manner it is to be done with the least injury. In the same manner the expression in the vote of the Church cannot be understood to limit the business of the decisive Council, any further than263 their doing what is proper for a Council to do which is called on the business expressed in the vote.[111] It is objected that if diversity of religious sentiment, on account of the prejudices which it usually occasions in the case of those who differ disqualifies men from being judges in an ecclesiastical controversy in the case of those who differ from them, then it also disqualifies them from being judges in a civil controversy; & of course that the judges of our Courts in New England being chiefly congregational its would be improper judges in any cases where either parties was an episcopalian or anabaptist, or quaker & that the justices in England being all of the Church of England would be improper judges of dissenters.

Answer. If there were any stated judicatories in the nation but merely justices commissioned to be convoked for each cause by the election of the parties, & there were as many lawful justices on one side as on the other; it would obviously be most just for one party to be allowed to chose as many on his side as the other on his - especially if the controversy were of a religious nature, & the conduct to be judged of, had reference to that very question in which the judges differed from each other. For example, suppose the Test-Act were taken off in England & half of the justices were dissenters, & in all controversies the common law of the nation gave each party the right of choosing one half of his judges; & suppose that a controversy arises between an Episcopalian & a Dissenter concerning a Dissenter264 & writing in defense of Dissenters & endeavouring to propagate Dissent & that the point to be judged of is the conduct of the two parties in the management of this controversy & the Episcopalian has chosen half of the judges who are of his side; would it not be [112] equitable that the Dissenter should choose the other half of his side: especially if a part of the business of the judges who are of his side was to act the part of mediators --. I readily admit that in deciding religious controversies, the judges actually employed have in fact point of fact been usually all on one side & the well known effect has been the grossest unrighteousness & the most violent persecution. This has been true in Romish & Lutheran Countries as well as in England & Scotland & almost every where as every one acknowledges.

Two or three of the objections touched upon at the former sitting of the Council deserve some additional remarks265.

It was objected that, on this plan there can be no defense against error because some a minister can find some who will justify his opinions; as I am said to have asserted in my Remarks on the Springfield controversy.

To this I reply

1. In this objection the actual state of the present case is wholly forgotten; for, as has been often observed the Council will not meet either to justify, or condemn my opinion, whether it be truth or error.

2. What I assert in my remarks on that Controversy is merely this - that, if one party is allowed to choose all his own judges there can be no defence against error. And I say the same now; & it is very true, that if one party has all the judges on his side, there can be no defense for the other party, against error, or injustice or any thing else that is bad because such a Council must of course be partial. What I there assert therefore, instead of being inconsistent with [113] what I now urge, establishes the266 grand point & overthrows the very position it is brought to prove viz. that all the council ought to be on one side.

3. All the force in the objection, if it has any, lies - not against my going out of the county for a choice, but against my having any choice at all, that is, against any elective Councils whatever. For if a minister has any advantage in his choice to defend himself in error it arises from his having liberty to choose half his judges & not from the place where they are chosen whether in or out of the County. The rule of confining him & the church to the County where they live will no more defend the truth than expose it. It will do one or the other just as the county is. If the churches of this county had happened to be of my side, this rule would have defended me in my error & would have disabled the Church from defending themselves just as much as going out of the County will now. Indeed the rule would more generally expose orthodox churches than defend them for there are more erroneous vicinities in the world world than orthodox ones.

It may be objected that by thus insisting on the liberty of going out of the county, I cast a reflexion on the neighboring ministers as though they had not honesty enough to do me justice. To this I answer that, if any individual is in circumstances, which powerfully tend to prejudice his mind in favor of, or against either of the parties in a given case, it is no reflexion on him to object against as improper to be employed as a judge in that case. Thus, if a man is nearly related to one of the parties; or where a town is a party & he is an inhabitant of that town; he cannot sit as judge or juror in such a case. But there is far more reason to object against all the judges being wholly on [114] one side to judge of the conduct of two parties in a religious controversy than to object against near relations; as will appear if we consider two things:

1. Men are as prone, in religious differences to favor their own party as they are267 their near relations. This is true all the world over, in every age with both learned & unlearned, that men are friends to their own party, & commend their persons and justify their conduct. How widely different are the opinions of two opposite parties of the same points of conduct. Thus, how different are the opinions of Presbyterians from those of the Episcopalians concerning the conduct of the Presbyterians in the time of the Long Parliament; & so I might adduce innumerable other instances.

2. Difference in religious sentiments excites one kind of prejudice which nearness of relation does not; for though the latter prejudices us in favor of those who are related to us yet it does not prejudice us against those who are not related to us; whereas we are not only prejudiced in favor of those who agree with us in sentiment, but we are strongly prejudiced against those who differ from us. And the strongest prejudices ever known in the world have arisen from this cause. Hence the question with regard to the neighboring ministers & churches is not whether they are just & upright men, but whether these circumstances naturally tend to bias them in this controversy. If they do, then it is obviously against the plain rights of mankind to oblige me to be judged only by them. It was objected that to allow me to go out of the county will be a bad precedent, & greatly expose the peace & good order of the county in its ecclesiastical affairs. I desire this objection to be thoroughly examined; & we shall be able to determine whether it has any force by resolving these two questions[115]

1. Whether the rule to confine Councils to a vicinity, be so universal & unalterable, that it is absolutely without exceptions & ought never to be departed from in any case, ordinary or extraordinary; & if not; then, 2. Whether this case be so far an extraordinary as to require an exception from the general rule.

As to the first of these questions, I should affront this Rev. Council by suggesting that any one of them would affirm it; for it would contradict their own practice & that of all our churches; as well as the sentiments which they have advanced respecting the Consociations in Connecticut.

The only remaining question is Whether this case be so far extraordinary as to require an exception from the general rule. And if the Council will reflect on what has been said it will I think be plain to a demonstration that the most absolute268 rules of justice do absolutely require it. We are two controverting parties, & we want a council because we want judges to judge between us; - & for what unless to do justice between us; - We want a balance to weigh both parties & is it not essential that the scales be even.269 Why then oblige me to be weighed against the other party when their scale is so much the heaviest before we are put into the balance. It is the law of God, Thou shalt have a just balance; & his strict injunction, That which is altogether just shalt thou follow. The prudence & justice of this Council teaches them in other respects not to give one side an advantage above the other - particularly, not to hear one side in the absence of the other; —— particularly, not to hear the one side in the absence of the other, to have the same rules respecting evidence for both & to give each equal opportunity to plead his own cause. Now if justice requires that such an exact equality be maintained in circumstantials, how much more does it require that in a point so essential as the choice of the tribunal [116] which is to decide the main controversy, there be a perfect equality, & that neither party be allowed to have all the judges on his own side. How highly my people estimate the advantage of having all the judges on their own side is abundantly evident from their strenuous & persevering efforts to secure it; & if it be so great an advantage, why should we not be placed on the same footing. My people have ever pretended that I ought not to choose half of the Council; nor have we submitted the question to this Council whether this our case shall be referred to the whole County without any choice in the case. I say we have never270 submitted this question because it was never once mentioned, & I suppose never entered into our hearts. I am sure it did not into mine. This point was never controverted between us; & it is absurd to suppose, that we summoned a Council of Churches271 which we had never even once disputed, but had always been agreed in. That I should have a choice in the Council was fully admitted by the Church & the point debated between us was, whether I should go out of the County in my choice. But if I be allowed a choice as well as they, why should I not be allowed choose those who are on my side as well as the Church those who are on their side. To deny me this, is utterly inconsistent with272 allowing me a choice, for the pretence is an equality of advantage or an equal chance for justice. To allow me an equal choice is an implicit acknowledgement that I am in justice entitled to an equal advantage; but to tie me up to judges who are of their opinion is grossly inconsistent with this acknowledgement. To tell me with a great show of fairness, you shall stand as good a chance for justice as we, you shall choose one half of the Council, & we will choose [117] the other; --- but then we will choose those who are on our side, & you also shall chose those who are on our side, & opposed to yourself on the main point of controversy is only only mocking and insulting me. This Council in their former result intimate, that a part of the business of the future council will be273 to reconcile us; &, in the very nature the Council which comes with power to separate must have power to decide whether the separation shall be immediate or whether it is not their duty to act as mediators & attempt a reconciliation. And what is more obvious to common sense274 that a mediator should be impartial one in whom both parties confide & have an equal interest. If it be lawful to compare little things with great ones, did not the most High himself, when he was pleased to appoint a mediator between God & man, take care that he should partake of both natures to qualify him for that office.

And besides the grounds already mentioned, requiring an exemption from the ordinary rules of proceeding this case is in other respects very275 extraordinary, not merely in some of its circumstances but in its very nature; so much so that no such case I apprehend has occurred in New England or even in the christian church. The proposed council will convene to decide on a new question - a question which I suppose was never before submitted to an ecclesiastical tribunal. And certainly, in the decision of such a case, it cannot but be allowed, that a number of the senior ministers of the Country should be engaged.

The circumstances of this Country ought also to be considered [118] we having no appeal from one Council to another. Such a state of things makes a great alteration, as to what is reasonable in many particular cases, & requires some things to be allowed which need not be allowed, if here, as in Scotland, we could appeal from the Churches of the vicinity to those of the Province & ultimately to the assembly of the whole nation. It is a strange way of arguing that, because there ought to be a regular establishment in the country, we ought therefore to bind ministers & churches to the same measures as if there were one; & yet not allow them the same276 privileges which an establishment secures, & which alone can render those measures tolerable. If we had a regular establishment, there would be here as in Scotland no elective Councils. There would have been none of late in Hatfield nor in this town in the case of Mr. Hawley. Yet, as we have no establishment, every one allows the necessity of such Councils in many cases; & the same point277 involves us in the necessity of going out of the vicinity because we have not the right of appeal.

On the whole admitting it to be a good general rule that Councils should be selected from the neighborhood no reason can be assigned why this case should not be exempted, unless we take the broad ground that no exemptions from general rules shall ever be allowed, however just expedient & necessary in themselves for fear of a bad precedent. But was this broad ground ever taken, nor can it ever be observed in human society. No such uniform observance of general rules was ever known in our ecclesiastical concerns. It is a general rule that the alleged delinquency of a private brother ought first to be tried278; yet [119] the Church may refer it directly to a council as was lately done in Hatfield with the approbation of the churches & with no fear of a bad precedent. And with regard to this very point, to go from the vicinity in the choice of Councils, so far from being a new thing, has been customary, where the peculiar circumstances of the case required it, & not only in this province but even in Connecticut. The aggrieved party at Goshen, in their controversy with Mr. Heaton, passed by the consociation to which Goshen belonged279 & summoned a council from the remote parts of Connecticut by the advice of Col. Williams of Wethersfield & of the Rev. Mr. Williams of Lebanon. Mr. Searl also neglecting the consociation with which Sharon is connected recommended by the advice of the best judges summoned a Council to settle him at that place. But what I desire is not to go from the vicinity in a neglect of churches of the vicinity, but to select half the Council from abroad first asking the leave & approbation of these churches.

As to the danger of this case being pleaded as a precedent for the same liberty in ordinary cases; the danger is less than perhaps in any other case because all the world regard this case as wholly new & extraordinary.

And it is a great mistake, that an exemption from a general rule, in a singular case which imperiously demands it, tends to weaken that general rule. The very contrary is true that to grant the exemption strengthens, & to refuse it weakens the rule; for the injustice which men suffer by an over exact observance of the rule is ascribed to280 the rule itself & thus we are inclined to renounce it. The ecclesiastical constitution of Connecticut has of late been evidently weakened by thus overstraining the general rules & to refuse reasonable exemptions where plain justice requires them, will tend to deter the [120] country from ever coming into a regular establishment. If then the whole matter be duly weighed by the Rev.281 Council, I cannot but think they will judge that no ill consequences will follow from granting me this equal liberty of choice, which I claim as a matter of obvious justice; and that there will be many good consequences as there will be opportunity to obtain a just & impartial Council by bringing the whole of the consistory to a proper balance, as we shall be able to employ a number of the senior ministers & gentlemen of chief note in the Country in determining this new & extraordinary affair, as there will be a proper consistory to attempt a reconciliation between the parties, & as the proceedings will be better stated abroad282 more to the satisfaction of the world. I beseech the Rev.283 Council not suddenly to pass over these things, but to weigh them with thorough deliberation. I trust to their justice that they will use great care & diligence that there will be no unequal & hard dealing as to the terms I am bound to, in the hearing & determining this affair of such vast importance.

And if, upon the whole of what they see & find in our circumstances, they [128] judge it best that Pastor & people be immediately separated that they proceed to dissolve the relation between them."

There was much discourse concerning this draught315. It was read publicly & distinctly, three or four times, & it was desired that each particular passage of it might be considered & scanned.

It was offered to such as desired to view & examine it & handed from one to another. Some amendments were proposed but the amendments did not seemed to be liked as well as the first draught316. It was then put to vote & it was questioned whether it was a vote. Then it was desired that all might sit down & hold up their hands for some considerable time, & then it appeared plainly to be a vote, & was generally acknowledged to be so by such as had appeared most to oppose it & was not questioned by more than one or two. And one of them viz. Major Pomeroy said it was generally allowed to be a vote & therefore intimated it to be his mind that it should pass as such & that we should317 proceed to other business.

Then I declared to the Church what I should insist on with respect to the Council that should be chosen viz. To choose half of the members of the Council; & to choose three ministers or churches without the bounds of the County, & should not insist on more than three unless the whole Council consisted of more than 14. fourteen. I also told them that I should insist on a provisional choice of some others out of the County to come in case any of the three first pitched upon should fail so that I might indeed have three from other parts of the land.

It was then strongly insisted on by the Church that I that I ought not to allowed to choose any out of the County; [129] so that the consideration of the number to be chosen out of the County was for present laid aside. And the matter debated was, Whether I should be allowed to bring any at all from without the bounds of the County. After much talk it was put to vote in the following words: Whether or not you do consent that in choosing the Council now under consideration, I, in my choice should go out of this County for any part of the Council? Let those who do consent, manifest it by holding up their hands." --- It was not voted. -- Upon which I told the Church that the business of the meeting was come to an end; as it was apparent that Pastor & people could act in nothing together in calling a Council. After some discourse I told them that I stood ready to yield to have but two of the Council from abroad unless the Council consisted of more than ten; but should insist on three if the whole was more than ten & on more if the Council was above fourteen. It being moved by some of the brethren that the meeting should be adjourned318 for further consideration; It was accordingly adjourned to the next day at one oclock.

Accordingly the next day we met again, & the last subject of consideration concerning my going out of the County for any part of the Council was proposed again; &, supposing they319 now had sufficient consideration of the matter I was about to put it to vote again. But then it was desired by some that there might be a reconsideration of of the first vote which320 had passed the day before concerning the business of the Council, & suggested that if some alteration were made in that vote there was a probability that the latter vote concerning my going [130] out of the County would pass without difficulty. Then Major Pomeroy declared that a number of brethren had met together & drawn up something determining the business of the Council: which draught he read which was to this purpose viz. —— That the Council should come & propose terms of accommodation between Pastor & People; to which, if the people did not consent, the Council should proceed immediately to dissolve the relation between Pastor & people." I objected much against setting aside what had been already voted to make way for any new projections as only tending to open a door for new contests & difficulties & greatly to entangle & lengthen out our affairs & also because there was nothing in what had been already voted but what was essential, what the nature of things & the state of our affairs did necessarily require, & therefore what I must insist upon. And I particularly321 to what Major Pomeroy proposed as limiting the Council without referring any thing to their judgment or advice, & not so much a suffering them to act their own judgment in determining whether it was best immediately to separate between Pastor & People. The same things were strongly objected by some others; & on the whole I manifested that I should wholly decline putting it to vote; on which, after much earnest talk I put the other matter to vote viz. whether I should be allowed to go out of the County for any members of the Council -- which again passed in the negative. Upon this I informed the church that I was not against their having farther time for consideration if they desired it, & farther conference with them or with [131] a committee they might choose; but it would not be worth the while to make any farther attempt to act in concert if they were fixed & resolved in these two things --- Not to leave it to the discretion of the Council whether to separate; & not to allow me to choose any members of the Council out of the County. - Some of them declared they were fixed in these things. Upon which I asked, if any had any thing to object against my dissolving the meeting seeing we were come at present to a stop as to acting in any thing together; & told them withal that, if afterwards on further consideration a church meeting was desired to reconsider these matters, I would not refuse warning one. On this occasion there was much earnest talk about the power of the Church to act without me & to call a Council themselves.

Finally some of the brethren thought that I had best dissolve the meeting & accordingly I dissolved it; & immediately after which, as the people began to move in order to go out of the meeting house, some individuals called out very earnestly for them to stay, & proceed to act without me & see if they could not have Maj. Pomeroy’s draught (aforementioned) put to vote. I then came away; & the people that stayed behind differing among themselves gradually dispersed & did nothing.
