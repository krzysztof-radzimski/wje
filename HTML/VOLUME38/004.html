"Reflections on the Remonstrance Sent to Mr. Clark and Mr. Edwards"

This is a copy of a document, drawn up for Edwards sometime in late 1751 (certainly after the council of May 15) by Ebenezer Pomeroy, Jr., clerk of the Northampton church. It contains retractions by the Northampton Church Committee from statements made in its original "Remonstrance" against Edwards and his followers upon the effort to found a second church in town. The address apparently was made to an individual (probably Rev. Thomas Prince of Boston, moderator of the council) or a council; the "Reflections" refers to the head negotiator as "Sir" and one acquainted with "those breathren by whose procuremt. we Suppose you are here," that is, Edwards’ adherents or, more specifically, Edwards and Col. Timothy Dwight, leader of the dissidents. While we do not have the original Remonstrance, we can deduce its content from this and other documents (e.g. the Petition of the Minority and the Northampton Church Committee’s Report on it). Among other things, the committee charged Dwight with threatening to block efforts to settle a new pastor of the first church if Edwards was not allowed to be installed over a second church, and it charged Edwards with complicity. The Church Committee does moderate some of its statements here, but they do not back down on the essential points of their opposition and criticism of Edwards and Dwight.

*********

The manuscript (BRBL, f. 1351) is three leaves, octavo, with the last page blank, except for a notation by Edwards.

Retractations of Northampton People marginal

marginal

The First Church in Northampton for a considerable time past having had and still having many things in the situation and Circumstances which a sovereign, holy and just God has seen fit to bring them into, which ought to excite not only each particular member thereof to a most solemn, serious and impartial examination of his past and present temper and conduct, and to sincere and deep repentance and humiliation of and for what he shall, upon such examination, find amiss and sinful in either; but also the whole church as a body impartially to canvass their public acts and proceedings relating to the melancholy affair of the di[s]mission of their late, reverend and worthy pastor Mr. Jonathan Edwards, and other matters which have ensued and been consequent thereon, which have been attended with4 much temptation and exceedingly exposed the church, as well as particular persons, to do that which might be offensive to God and a transgression of the perfect and holy laws of Christianity; and if, on such examination, they should find any of their public acts truly unjustifiable and such as they might reasonably judge provoking to God, to make such retractions and reflections as the rules of the Gospel require; the said church, under such apprehensions, having taken the letter exhibited to the Rev. Mr. Peter Clark,5 a member of the ecclesiastic council which sat in Northampton in May last, commonly Called "The Church’s Remonstrance," under particular and deliberate examination think it their duty to make the following reflections and remarks thereon:

Particularly in the first pages of said Remonstrance, the church used these words, viz. "and some things unaccountable in the conduct of Mr. Edwards"; where they apprehend they ought not to have used so strong an expression, but ought rather to have expressed themselves in these or the like words: "and some things very difficult for us to account for in the conduct of Mr. Edwards . . ."

In the third page these words are used, viz. "if we had had mediating councils here from generation to generation, they never could have altered the case or found out a way," etc. Although we believe the thing to be true as expressed, yet we apprehend we ought instead of these terms to have used terms more mild, and that the words used manifested too great an earnestness and engagedness of spirit. And a little onward in the same page we said, "There was no more prospect of the latter than of the moon’s falling," meaning by the word "latter" what immediately preceded, viz. Mr. Edwards’ relinquishing some of his before-mentioned sentiments. We own that the form of speech was too ludicrous and merry and did not savor enough of solemnity and gravity of mind, and apprehend instead thereof we ought to have said, "And there was no prospect of the latter."

In the beginning of the sixth page is a paragraph in these words, viz.

And here, sir, we can’t but observe to you something we take to be very unjustifiable in the conduct of those brethren by whose procurement we suppose you are here, as well as some other things in Mr. Edwards his conduct to us unaccountable, both savoring very much of the reverse of that Christian meekness, gentleness, sincerity and Charity which they make so high pretensions to; particularly the violent efforts they, and especially the primum mobile of them6 (though it is somewhat difficult to say who is the briskest spring[)], have made against our obtaining any other minister than Mr. Edwards, and the indefatigable pains used to resettle Mr. Edwards after a fair dismission, per fas et ne fas.

We freely acknowledge that several expressions in this paragraph are too harsh, severe and sarcastical and do not comport with that gentleness, mildness and meekness which the rules of the gospel require, more particularly the phrase per fas et ne fas; for although we apprehend some of the methods used to resettle Mr. Edwards were not justifiable, yet we are sensible that the terms per fas et ne fas are too strong and carry too high a charge in them. And whereas the said paragraph contains a charge upon the aggrieved brethren collectively of violent efforts made to prevent our obtaining any other minister than Mr. Edwards, we acknowledge that we have not suffi[ci]ent reason to conclude that any others of them, besides the gentleman intended by the words primum mobile, did directly use any means to prevent our obtaining a minister other than Mr. Edwards. Though we apprehend their indefatigable pains to resettle him was a great impediment in the way of our obtaining another, but we confess we have not sufficient reason to conclude that they had that in direct view. We therefore freely ask their pardon of that charge made on them without sufficient grounds.

And inasmuch as in the before-mentioned sixth page the church said, "How little does it savor of Christian moderation, meekness and charity for a particular man to say that we--viz. the First Church and Parish in Northampton--should not have any minister till they had settled Mr. Edwards, . . . that it signified nothing for us to try," etc.: we confess that we apprehend we were guilty of some small mistake in reciting the words spoken by a certain gentleman, though we don’t apprehend that we mi[s]represented the same. We rather think that that part of the paragraph to which the above-recited words belong ought to have been as follows, viz. "How little does it savor of Christian moderation, meekness and humility for a particular man to say, ’If you’ (meaning the First Church and Parish of Northampton) ’will be willing that we’ (meaning Mr. Edwards’ adherents) ’should have Mr. Edwards, I’ll do what I can to help you get another minister as good as you can; I can get you a minister or I can keep you from having one--you won’t have a minister till Mr. Edwards is settled,’" etc., as in the Remonstrance.

And whereas onward in the sixth page, after complaining of very wrong representations made of some things by a certain gentleman and some others, and giving an instance thereof, we said that such representations were no other than downright falsehoods, and we should be glad if we had reason to judge they were not willfully such: we freely acknowledge that the terms "downright falsehoods" were too harsh and savored too much of unchristian heat, severity and bitterness of spirit, and that we ought not to have gone into any stronger expressions in that particular than that such representations were very wrong.

And whereas the above-recited sentence implies that we apprehended that we had not sufficient reason to judge that the said misrepresentation complained of by us was not a willful falsehood, we confess that the abo[v]esaid expression manifested that we had too great jealousy of that particular brother and some others, and were too ready to construe their conduct uncharitably; and that if we had then had a proper exercise of charity towards them, we should have then hoped--and we declare that we do now hope--that said misrepresentations proceeded from a mistake in judgment only. We therefore freely ask7 their forgiveness thereof.

And where in the same page we said in these words, "and there are some things in Mr. Edwards’ conduct so unaccountable that, had such things been predicted of him before his dismission, nothing could have been more shocking to us": we declare that we are sensible that the language is too strong and naturally tended to raise an idea in the minds of the hearers more formidable than the particulars after-enumerated would warrant; for which we freely ask Mr. Edwards his pardon, and think that the sentence ought to have run thus: " . . . and there are some particulars in Mr. Edwards’ conduct that are to us so unaccountable that, if such things had been predicted of him before his dismission, they would have been surprizing to us," etc.

And whereas there is a little further on in the seventh page an express averment that, when Mr. Edwards set out on a journey with a certain gentleman to procure a council, he knew that we were about to send for a candidate: we acknowledge that that part of the charge is made in stronger terms than the evidence would warrant, we having no other ground to conclude that he knew it than that, as near as we can remember, it was a thing commonly known and talked of at [that] time; but inasmuch as we are credibly informed that Mr. Edwards peremptorily declares he did not know it, we are bound in charity to believe him: and therefore declare that that strong expression ought not to have been inserted.

And whereas there is an implied averment that Mr. Edwards’ end in sending for and being active in procuring said council was that they might install him here, we own that we had not sufficient evidence to determine that that was his direct aim therein. We therefore humbly ask his pardon in the abovesaid particulars, for making said charges beyond what the ground or evidence we had would well support.

And whereas in the eighth page the form of speech we made use of plainly declares that Mr. Edwards had a great inclination to continue at Northampton as a minister, although, as we apprehended he had reason to judge it would be at the expense of the peace and prosperity of the greatest part of the town: although we apprehend the terms we used can’t be interpreted into a charge of being so inclined to continue at Northampton as a minister as that he desired it at the same time, believing and being convinced in himself that it would be at the expense of the peace and prosperity of the greatest part of the town (as it is construed by some), yet we freely own that the expressions were more peremptory than the evidence we had would warrant, and that we discovered a censoriousness that was inconsistent with the proper exercise of that true evangelical charity which believeth all things, and hopeth all things. And since we have heard that he labored abundantly before the council which was here about that time to exhibit to them the great difficulties and objections there was in the way of his resettling here, we can’t but hope that some things which induced us to believe that he was much inclined to resettle here proceeded from a solicitude and great desire to know his duty. We therefore freely and humbly ask Mr. Edwards to forgive our censoriousness and rashness in this particular.

And finally, we humbly and earnestly pray God, of his great grace and mercy in Christ Jesus, to pardon what has been provoking and offensive to him in the foregoing instances, wherein we have made reflections on ourselves, and make us fully sensible of in everything wherein we have provoked and offended him, and give us true repentance thereof.

By a vote desired Mr. Edwards’ prayers for this church, and that a copy of the reflections on the Remonstrance be sent to Mr. Clark and Mr. Edwards.

A true copy. Attest: Ebenezer Pomeroy, Jr., Church Clerk
