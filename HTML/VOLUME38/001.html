"Sacrament Book I" Sac. Bk. I Jonathan Edwards

"Sacrament Book 1"

"Sacrament Book 1"

The only extant notebook from a series Edwards kept in preparing An Humble Inquiry , on the communion controversy, this was originally Bk. II, re-assembled and re-numbered as Bk. I. At one point in this manuscript, Edwards refers to "Bk. IV." With the exception of a few scattered entries, the entire book is struck through with use lines. This notebook provides a good example of Edwards’ process of working towards a finished treatise.

*****

The manuscript (BRBL, f. 1247) is eighteen leaves, with the first and last leaves glued to insides of the front and back covers, which is constructed from a ream wrapper and inscribed "Sac. B. II I." Stitch marks show that the notebook was disbound at some point by Edwards and reconstructed with sheets from other books or with blank sheets tipped in, during which he renumbered several of the existing pages: pp. 2-3 were originally numbered pp. 20-21, pp. 3-4 were originally pp. 68-69, and the last page was originally p. 70. The leaves that would have comprised pp. 8-9, 14-19, 26-27, and 36-37 are missing, most likely removed by Edwards.

Ans . In the original it is, "Unto Zion it shall be said, This and [that] man is born in her." The prefix lamed signifies "to," or the dative case, but it never signifies de , or "concerning."

"To Zion it shall be said," agreeable to the words, v. 4, "I will make mention of Rahab and Babylon to them that know me."

And if it be interpreted, "Of Zion it shall be said, This and that man was born in her," the meaning can be no other than that they were born into her, of parents that belonged to her, and by means [not] used in hell, not that those that were born were inhabitants of Zion before they were born. Their Father is in Zion; Zion is their mother. Ministers, that are their spiritual parents, are in Zion; the means used are in Zion, and the [new] birth brings ’em into Zion, to be inhabitants or citizens of that city. Zion has them as it were in her womb before they are born, and travails in birth with them. Ministers and godly friends travail in birth with them who are inhabitants of Zion.

How can they own it, that pretend to do no other than renounce it? To own is at least to pretend not to renounce.

BAPTISM. Positive charity alone operates to persons’ being admitted to any new positive privileges; but the baptizing of a child is a new positive privilege as much as the admission of a person to the Lord’s Supper. When an adult person that has heretofore been one of the church is received in by baptism, he is received to a new positive privilege, and positive charity is requisite. And therefore, when a child is born, in order to our doing anything whereby we positively and solemnly testify that we positively receive that child as a saint, one regenerated and whose sins are washed away in the blood of Christ, we must have positive charity for the child. But a positive charity for the child is founded on a positive charity for the parent; the parent was one whom God had a favor for as one of his beloved people, that gave up himself and [his] offspring to God, and that therefore He will bless his offspring with saving blessings for his sake.

A negative charity is sufficient for negative privileges. Not positively censuring and condemning a person is sufficient for not inflicting any positive punishment on a person as wicked, and therefore may be sufficient to suffer persons that are already in the church without admonition or excommunication.

Verbal PROFESSIONS. If persons that are baptized in infancy are admitted to the Lord’s Table without any verbal profession of godliness, or something positive exhibited to afford ground of positive charity, it utterly defeats the design which required ground of positive charity in admission of adult persons into the Christian church by baptism. The design aimed at that makes this requisite, is that ordinarily those who are united communicants in the church should have ground of positive charity one for another; but this design is as effectually defeated as if no ground of positive charity were required at first.

Use frequent RECAPITULATIONS.

APOSTLES’ Epistles. The question is whether it was an accidental thing. or a thing belonging to the nature and character of the society.

OBJECTION. We read of none that professed to believe in Christ with a common faith that was ever rejected and not received into the church.

Ans . 1. Nothing else in those days was ordinarily called faith but a saving faith, or an appearance of it.

[ Ans .] 2. It being known that they were bound to profess no other but a saving faith, and that it was the custom to accept none but on such a profession; hence none offered to make any other but what they pretended to be this, and none offered to be received into the church but on a pretense of this. In that, there was seldom occasion to reject any.

[ Ans .] 3. We have instance in the history of some that had a common faith and moral seriousness, and conviction of conscience as the fruit of it, that yet were not admitted to baptism, but something further waited for. Acts 2; compare Acts 13:48 and 52.

The words "believers" and "disciples" are evidently used to signify the same thing as in the beginning of the next chapter, where it signifies that faith that those that joined themselves to the Company [of] Christians had, or seemed to have; and vv. 22-23 of that chapter.

The people that did not join themselves to them had a common faith, evenXX zeal and admiration and religious affection. Acts 4:21, Acts 5:13, Acts 5:26.

4. Not being admitted among Christians as of their company, to join with them in their religious priviledges, or to bear their name, together with other circumstances, viz. the sufferings they exposed to, tended to prevent men’s long continuing to follow ’em, to hear the word preached, or to have anything to do with them.

EPISTLES. Objection . It might so happen through the peculiar state of the church.

Ans . It was not so universally. There were times when they were high in reputation, and were in peace. They were in great reputation with the multitude, when persecuted by the rulers, Acts 4:21, Acts 5:13, Acts 5:26. They had times of great rest and freedom from persecution, Acts 9:31. These were the Hebrews whom the Apostle writes to.

WEDDING GARMENT. Infidels might come into Christian congregations to hear the word preached, as appears by what the Apostle says, and by many places in the Acts. They might come to hear the word preached. But will it ever be said to them, "How camest thou in hither, in a state of sin?"

METHOD. See "Moral Agency," Bk. IV, p. 90.XX

IX. OBJECTION. The strength of every good argument to prove any proposition not self-evident, and so needs proof, lies in some more evident proposition. And this argument, if it has any strength, lies in this proposition, that men must have have a known right to the Lord’s Supper in order to their coming regularly to the Lord’s Supper.

One objection against my opinion, is that it would occasion a very USTEADY attendance on the Lord’s Supper, because that as often as persons have prevailing doubts, they must stay away. But according to their doctrine of fitness for the Lord’s Supper, persons must not come when not fit. With their qualification, it would cause a vastly greater unsteadiness; their communicating must be as unsteady and uncertain as the moral sincerity of natural men, which is like a lamp without oil, etc.

(* P. 37.)XX How unreasonable is it to take persons into a state for life on qualifications that, from their nature, they have reason to look upon as not being for life and but for a very short time, under a notion of a fitness for what they are admitted to. What they are admitted to is a durable privilege, and therefore nothing can be a fitness for it but a durable qualification. To suppose otherwise implies a contradiction: it supposes it a fitness far more than it is a fitness. How unreasonable for a man that is going a journey in the night to take a lamp to enlighten him through the journey that he knows is nothing but a flaming wick without oil.

It can’t be a fitness in any respect for that which he is admitted, for he is admitted to a long privilege. ’Tis a long qualification only that fits a suit, a long privilege. A short qualification can no more [. . .] (** Bk. II, p. 116.)XX

[. . .] the king and parliament should be pleased to introduce a new constitution of things in one of the plantations by a new charter, or a new body of instituions and statutes. In those things wherein their new charter is abundantly clear and full, that is the rule, though it may be hard for us to account for some other time, or find out the reasons that might influence the wisdom of the king and parliament. And though we can’t tell how to reconcile the difference that appears to wisdom and justice, that is none of our business in order to determine what our present rights and obligations are by the charter we are under. Not that the Old Testament is out of date, but I say that the Old Testament dispensation XX is out of date, and consequently the constitution that established it.

Last p., col. 2]XX [. . .] pretended to be the same sort of persons with the foolish virgins, and never were received by the wise under any such notion.

To say that persons may PROFESS Christianity, and not profess real XX Christianity but only profess visible Christianity, is just the same absurdity as if we should say that they profess that they are professors; and if it be inquired what they profess, that they do profess, or that they make visibility visible.
