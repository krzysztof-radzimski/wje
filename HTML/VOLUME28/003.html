"PAPER ON FREE WILL"

The first part of this manuscript (BRBL, Box 21, f. 1256) is probably contemporaneous with the "Paper on Justification" ("Notes on Imputation and Free Grace"), that is, 1734 or later. In the second signature of this book, Edwards is writing on free will and efficacious grace. The manuscript is composed of four folio leaves in two signatures: the first, pp. 1-4, with an English Arms/Crowned GR watermark, the second, pp. 5-8, London Arms/Crowned GR(L).

"PAPER ON FREE WILL"

OF FREE WILL. A promise, the condition of which is not capable of being understood, is no promise at all. A promise is a signification of some some future good to the persons to whom the promise is made; if there be no signification thereof, there is no promise. For God merely to intend to show good within his own breast is no promise. 'Tis no promise till that design is signified or made known. And therefore words, the meaning of which are not capable of being understood, can be no promise: for they are no signification of anything; they make nothing known. The design of the supposed promiser still remains as much within his own breast as they did before. His design is no way signified: for though there are words spoken or letters written, yet they are no promise anymore than any other sounds or letters; and that for this reason: because they ben't capable of being understood or of having any intelligible meaning; or, which is the same thing, they are words without signification, and therefore can be no promise. They bring God under no obligation to the creature, for these sounds and letters are not spoken to man: for that which is in its own nature uncapable of being understood by man, is not spoken to man, because what is spoken to man is spoken to man's understanding. It is not mere sounds of a supposed promise, or marks upon paper, as such, that binds him that causes them; but 'tis the meaning of those marks or sounds that obliges, or what is signified by them to him to whom they are directed that binds and obliges him that causes them; and therefore, if they are not capable of signifying anything to his understanding, then to him they have no signification or meaning at all, and therefore can't bring the supposed promiser under any obligation to him

A16 promise is a signifier of some future good to the person to whom the promise is made; and therefore, a conditional promise is a signification of some future good to be bestowed by the promiser, together with the signification of the condition of the promise. And therefore if there be no signification of the promises, if the condition of the promise is hid, the promise is not revealed; or, which is the same thing, is not made. If there be a design of bestowing good upon condition, yet there is no promise till that design is revealed. But if there be sounds or marks that ben't capable of being understood, [there is no promise].

If a man should meet another alone, and through mistake should speak to him in a language that he did not understand, and knew not a word of, intentionally making some promise to him: such a promise brings the promiser under no obligation, because 'tis no17 signification of his mind to the other any more than if he had held his peace; and his design, notwithstanding such unknown words, is still kept to himself as much as before. But especially if his words were in themselves uncapable of an intelligible meaning.

But 'tis absurd to suppose that God would make a promise that was in its own nature uncapable of being understood. The end of all speech is to signify something. 'Tis therefore blasphemous to suppose that God would direct himself to us in that speech that was18 not capable of signifying any[thing], or that could have no intelligible meaning.

Obj. That text, "Ask, [and it shall be given you]," and "[every one that asks] receives," and will he not "give the Holy Spirit to them that ask him?" [Luke 11:9-13].

Ans. 1. That it evidently intends the prayers of children by Christ's argument: which of you, "if his son ask [of] him bread, will give a stone? or if he ask a fish, will he for a fish give him a serpent?" [Luke 11:11].

Prayers without faith and sincerity are no prayers at all indeed. They don't pray to God. Show how [they are] no prayers. And 'tis unreasonable therefore to suppose that in Scripture account there are no prayers [without faith and sincerity].

Again, 'tis unreasonable to suppose absolute promises made to faithless prayers: for to what purpose are promises made to such who, in their prayers, have no faith to depend on those promises?

'Tis plain that it is intended the prayer of faith by many places. "But let him ask in faith, nothing wavering" [Jas. 1:6].

When it is said, ask and receive, they therefore will allow that it is not asking. A man may ask in words in a very careless manner, yea, in a very profane and impudent manner: nobody will suppose the promise made to such asking as this. So that they themselves are forced not to stick to what is implied in the literal meaning of the words; and therefore, 'tis very unfair in them to argue from their literal meaning. Yea, they must allow that is not meant such asking as any wicked man whatsoever is capable of, or else the forementioned absurdities will follow.

'Tis unreasonable to think that promises are made to those who have faith to depend on those promises. Why should promises be made to those who are in such a state that don't allow of any believing use of the promise? Promises are made to faith: for God to make such promises to them that are in such a state, is incompatible with any faith. To believe and depend on the promises, or hope in the promises, is as absurd as to speak to them that have no ears. What is speech for, but that those that are spoken to might hear it? and what are promises for, but that those to whom the promises are made might believe them, depend on them, hope in them?

Again, if absolute promises of saving blessings are made to men that have no godliness, then either men may know that those promises belong to them while they are yet ungodly, or not. If not—if they can't know that they are made to them while ungodly—to what purpose are they made to them, if they can't know it? To what purpose do promises belong to them that, while they remain in that state, can't know it?

But if they can and sometimes do know that absolute promises belong to them while yet ungodly, see what consequences will follow.

A great argument of theirs to prove that God has put the condition of salvation within our reach, is that it would be unjust in God to require that of us, under pain of his wrath, that is not within our present power, or the power that we have in our present state since the fall. But this argument they themselves confute and overthrow in their own scheme: for they say that we in our present state ben't able to perfect performance, or that we can perform no other than an imperfect obedience; and yet that Christ died to satisfy for the imperfections of our obedience, that so our imperfect obedience may be accepted. Now how can these things hang together? If we can't perform any other than an imperfect obedience in our present state, and it ben't just in God to punish us for want of performing such an obedience as we can't perform in our present state, then what need of Christ's dying to satisfy for the imperfections of our obedience? What need of Christ's dying to satisfy for that which don't deserve to be punished? If our failing of perfect obedience don't deserve to be punished, or that God can't justly punish, then there is no need of Christ's suffering to satisfy for it. What need Christ suffer to satisy for that that is no fault, or that in its own nature deserves no suffering?

It may be they will answer that Christ died to satisfy the first law for us, which law required death, that so we might be at liberty from it, to be put under a new law that requires only sincere obedience.

I answer: how could Christ die to satisfy the first law for us, when, according to their scheme, we never broke that law? For according to them, we never broke that law in any sense. We never broke that law under Adam, for they say Adam was not our representative, and the guilt of his breaking of the law don't belong to us. And we never broke it in our persons, for according to their scheme we never were under it: and how can we break a law that we were never under? And therefore, how could Christ die to satisfy the law for us, who never broke it, nor even were under it?

It may be they will answer, Christ died to satisfy the law for us that we might not be under the law, and that we might be delivered from it. But what need of Christ's dying that we might not be under the law, when, according to their scheme, 'twould in itself be unjust that we should be under that law, because in our present state we are not able to keep it? What need therefore of Christ's dying, that we might not be under a law that it would be unjust that we should be under, whether Christ died or no?

To show upon how many accounts 'tis fit that absolute promises should not be made to the ungodly.

They say, it would be unjust for God to require that of us as the condition of salvation that is not within our present power, and that it is not just with God to punish that that we now, with our present power, cannot avoid.

And they allow that man in his fallen state cannot avoid sin, for they allow that a man now cannot perform a perfect sinless obedience. And yet they say that Christ died to satisfy for that sin, or that sinful imperfection of obedience. Now how do these things consist? What need of Christ's dying to satisfy for that which deserves no punishment, or that it would be in itself unjust for God to punish?

SCRIPTURES:

Rom. 9:16, "'Tis not of him that willeth nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy."

So 'tis said, "Who maketh thee to differ" [I Cor. 4:7]; whereas the difference has its original with man himself.

How is it possible that anything should be more evident than that the foundation of that distinction that there is between one man and another, whereby one is godly and another not godly, is with God, and that it is owing to his pleasure? Matt. 11:25-27, "At that time Jesus rejoiced in spirit and said, I thank thee, O Father, Lord of heaven and earth, because thou hast hid these things from the wise and prudent, and hast revealed them unto babes." Here the distinction is spoken of, viz. that some know those things and others know them not. Here, first, this distinction is attributed to God, as to him that caused it to be: for those that know it, know it because God revealed it; and the others know it, not because God hid it from them. 'Tis spoken of as what God caused.24 Second, God is spoken of as the cause. There was this distinction between such persons, as there was why the wise and prudent had it not and on the contrary that babes had it. Third, this is more evident still, because Christ thanks the Father for this. Fourth, 'tis evident from the next words that this is owing to the mere pleasure of God: "Even so, Father: for so it seemed good in thy sight" [Matt. 11:26]. Fifth, 'tis evident from the words that follow,25 that this distinction here spoken [of] is to be attributed to God as it relates to particular persons and not only to kinds or sorts of men: "No man knoweth the Son, but the Father; neither knoweth any man [the] Father, but the Son, and he to whomsoever the Son will reveal him."

The Scripture certainly seems to hold forth that the grace of God, or kindness of God to us, makes us to differ from others. But if God be obliged to bestow it upon our performance of what we do of ourselves, there is no particular kindness exercised to him that receives grace and is godly, no kindness in his receiving saving grace but what is common to all and to those that don't receive.

If we obtain the saving grace of God by virtue of an obligation that God has laid himself under to us, in common with the rest of mankind, his grace ceases to be of the nature of a present. By a "present" we mean something given in token of particular kindness to the person to whom it is given. But if this be the case, there is no kindness to him that actually receives God's grace but what is common to the whole race of mankind; 'tis no token of any particular benevolence to me. The kindness is in coming under obligation on such conditions, if anywhere; but that kindness is to all equally. There is no more of any gift or present in the case to a godly man than to an ungodly. But neither is this any kindness, according to their notion.

That persons entertain an opinion that God has obliged himself by absolute promises to what unconverted men can do, to bestow his saving grace upon them, without doubt generally arises from an apprehension in their own minds that it would be hard in God ever to refuse it; or, which is the same thing, that God is obliged to bestow it. And if so, what sort of promises are these? They ben't free promises; they are not promises of any free grace, but only God promises that which he is obliged to do, and which we could charge him with doing wrong if he should fail of. And therefore, what obligations to praise and thankfulness are [persons] under to him for those promises? And if this be so, there is no free grace in the case, either in the giving the promise or giving the thing promised: for if God ben't obliged to give the thing promised, neither is he obliged to make the promise. The whole gospel scheme of free grace is hereby overthrown, for by "free grace" in the gospel is surely meant that which God is not obliged to, which he might, without dealing any way unfairly or hardly, or in any respect wrongly, refuse. Debt and grace are opposed in Scripture as destroying one another.

And though perhaps they may say that though God ben't obliged to make any offer of salvation, yet seeing he does make men offers, it would be but an illusion to offer it when at the same time they can't attain it nor do anything whereby God is obliged to bestow it, and that the offer seems to oblige God to give it when they do what they can: I answer, that God offers it no otherwise than he does. If he offers it on what it is in their power to do, then God is obliged; but if he only reveals that they shall have it on such conditions which he directs them to seek, but at the same time reveals himself to be the sovereign bestower of it, then his offer don't oblige .

And whatever persons pretend, without doubt 'tis a secret apprehension of some goodness or worthiness in what natural men can do; that that is the thing that secretly prejudices and inclines them (though perhaps insensibly) to receive this doctrine, or at least a want of a sense of the miserable and sinful vile pollution of all that they do. If this were seen, it would not seem hard that God should make no absolute promises to it; yea, it would convince that it would not be suitable that God should oblige himself to it. And this doctrine is very dangerous on that account, because it tends greatly to confirm persons in an opinion of the worthiness of what they do, which they are so naturally inclined to.

Concerning26 the NECESSITY of the mighty power of God to convert the soul and the IRRESISTABLENESS, or rather "victorious efficacy," of that grace in opposition to the effect's being suspended on men's free will, see the matter excellently cleared in Mr. Clark of Salem Village his sermon on that subject, in a sermon at the public lecture at Boston that I borrowed of Mr. Bull, beginning p. 15 to p. 22, of which some of the words are these:27

First. There is a necessity of a divine power and grace in the conversion of a sinner which appears,

1. From the nature of man in his fallen degenerate state.... Our Savior proves the necessity of the regenerating grace of the Spirit, in order to our being restored to the spiritual life, and to an inheritance in the kingdom of God, from the universal corruption and carnality of man's nature. John 3:6-7, "That which is born of the flesh is flesh; and that which is born of the Spirit is spirit. Marvel not" (therefore adds he) "that I said unto thee, Ye must be born again."... Without the restoring grace of the Redeemer, man fallen is not only impotent, but full of enmity against holiness and the duties of the spiritual life. [...] Rom. 8:7, "The carnal mind is enmity against God: for it is not subject to the law of God, neither indeed can be." [...] And how can such a degenerate creature, renew himself by repentance, or convert himself to God, without the special influences of divine grace?

2. It appears by the nature of that work of grace, by which this change is effected. It is called the workmanship of God, and ascribed to his creating power [...]. Eph. 2:10, "For we are his workmanship, created in Christ Jesus unto good works." Ch. 4:24, "That ye put on the new man, which after God is created in righteousness and true holiness." 'Tis also termed regeneration, or a begetting us again. Jas. 1:18, "Of his own will begat he us by the word of truth." I Pet. 1:3, "Blessed be the God and Father of our Lord Jesus Christ, who according to his abundant mercy hath begotten us again."... This is ascribed entirely to God, exclusive of all other causes. John. 1:13, "Which were born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of men, but of God." And if such a change as amounts to a new birth, is necessary to the conversion of a sinner, surely man cannot be the author of it himself, no more than he may be said to beget himself at first; but the power and grace of God is necessary to the production of it. [...]

Secondly. There is a victorious efficacy in the grace of God, which being exerted shall infallibly convert a sinner, even the most obstinate and hardened sinner. "Turn thou me," and the effect shall answer the virtue of the cause, "and I shall be turned" [Jer. 31:18]. So Jer. 17:14, "Heal me, O Lord, and I shall be healed; save me, and I shall be saved.... As for God, his work is perfect; if he undertake any saving work, he can and will bring it to effect. Though the sinner may resist for a while, yet he shall not finally do so, but shall be made to yield at last in the day of God's power with him.... Yet some there are, who suppose, that when God has done all that he has promised, and furnished a sinner with sufficient aids of grace, whereby he may be converted if he will, it is at last left on the free will of man, whether he will be converted or no, and that he may finally resist the operations of divine grace. If so, then instead of saying, "Turn me, and I shall be turned," it may be said, "Though thou turnest me, I may never be effectually turned." But the contrary of this is manifest from Scripture.

1. Because the regeneration and conversion of a sinner is expressly ascribed to the will of God, and as expressly denied to the will of man. In the forecited, John 1:13, believers in Christ are said to be "born, not of blood, nor of the will of the flesh, nor of the will of man, but of God." The new birth is not the product of the will of man, but of the will of God. Jas. 1:18, "Of his own will begat he us." Again, Rom. 9:15-16, "He saith to Moses, I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and I will have compassion on whom I will have compassion." His inference hence is, so then, it is not of him that willeth, nor of him that runneth, but of God that showeth mercy, and that according to his own sovereign will. The phrase, "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy," as it speaks the absolute freedom of the divine will, so it imports the irresistable efficacy of it. If God will have mercy on any soul, nothing in the world shall hinder it; for there is a powerful efficacy in the will of God, he works all things according to "the counsel of his own will" (Eph. 1:11). He works them effectually (as the word signfies) with a potent, irresistable energy. If any are converted and saved, it is not of man that willeth originally, but of God that willeth and worketh according to his will.

2. It is ascribed to the mighty power of God, even to that power to which all things are possible. When our Savior had laid open the difficulties of salvation, or of entering into the kingdom of God, Mark. 10:26-27, the disciples were exceedingly surprized, and being "astonished out of measure," saith the Evangelist, said among themselves, "Who then can be saved? Jesus looking upon them said, With men it is impossible, but not with God: for with God all things are possible." He speaks it indeed, with reference to the Conversion and salvation of rich men, but the same is true of all sinners wedded to their lusts. The young man that came to Christ with that question, "Good Master, what shall I do that I may inherit eternal life?" [Mark 10:17] (on which occasion those words were spoken by our Savior), showed as good a disposition to the kingdom of God, as any mere natural man may ordinarily be supposed to do, yet it required the almighty power of divine grace to bring him into it, and to subdue his heart and will to the grace and government of Christ; and when this power is put forth, the most obstinate shall be made to yield to it. 'Tis not the preaching of an apostle, but the power of God accompanying it, that works faith (I Cor. 2:5), and captivates the souls of men to an obedient subjection to Christ (II Cor. 10:4-5). And this power of God to which all things are possible, is infinitely greater than that which is in the devil, or the corrupt nature of man to make resistance.

3. God has promised by his grace, to take away the principle of enmity and resistance in the carnal will; and herein lies the work of his grace principally, in subduing the obstinacy of men's hearts, in curing the will and affections of their carnality. Deut. 30:6, "The Lord thy God will circumcise thine heart, and the heart of thy seed, to love the Lord thy God with all thine heart," etc. Ezek. 36:26, "I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh & I will give you an heart of flesh. now what is it that may be supposed to resist, or frustrate the Operations of grace but the heart of stone or the perverse obstinacy of the will? but this God has promised shall be taken away by the prevailing efficacy of his grace.

4. Because in the work of grace, the will is renewed and powerfully inclined towards God; as the Scriptures clearly testify. Ps. 110. 3, "Thy people shall be willing" (or become thy willing subjects) "in the day of thy power. Philip. 2:13, "For it is God that worketh in you both to will and to do of his own good pleasure." John 6:44, "No man can come unto me, except the Father which hath sent me drew him" (saith Christ), which shows the necessity of divine grace; and our Savior's inference (v. 45, "Every man therefore that hath heard, and learned of the Father, cometh unto me), shows the victorious efficacy. If the Father draws, by the word and Spirit, the sinner infallibly comes to Christ.

5. Because of the absurd consequences of the contrary opinion, that leaves the work of conversion at last to the self-determining power of man's free will, for then the great and glorious designs God had in view, in the work of man's redemption by his Son Jesus Christ, would appear to have been built on a precarious bottom; then the decrees of God might be frustrated, and his intentions defeated, contrary to that declaration, Is. 46:10, "My counsel shall stand, and I will fulfill all my pleasure"; then God's promise to Christ of a numerous seed to serve him, is grounded on an uncertainty, [...] the fruits of his death and undertaking depending on the slippery and inconstant free will of man; then the elect have no more special grace than the reprobate, the saved than the damned; then the regenerate man may share in the glory of his salvation, if it be the good use of free will, and not the good work of free grace, that makes him to differ from those that perish.

That28 duty which is not done in sincerity, is not done at all in the sight of God, who looks at the heart and not at bodily motions or external sounds. If there be no sincere prayer, or that prayer which is the voice of faith, there is no prayer to God at all in God's sight: for 'tis the voice of the heart and the voice of faith that God looks at, and 'tis unreasonable to suppose that the promises made to prayer are made to any other prayer than Christian prayer, the prayer of the heart, prayer as the act of an intelligent creature and not of a machine. 'Tis real prayer, and not a mere pretense of prayer. See sermon on Ps. 27:4, in the answer to the objection under the second inference.29

To handle that argument from our prayers to God, that he would grant that we may do thus or thus. How absurd this is: if the thing that determines whether we shall have or do such things or no be our own free will independently, and not to be dependent on any determination of God's, our very praying that such a thing may be, supposes that it depends on God's determination.

To deny a disposing or governing providence over the wills and so the acts of the intelligent world, is to deny the chief part of providence, the main of God's disposing government of the world, and to take away the glory and crown of providence: for what is his disposal of the actions and motions of brute creatures and the inanimate creation in comparison of this? 'Tis in a great measure to dethrone God. Indeed, it leaves him his moral government of the world, but it takes away the chief of his disposing government. Yea, in effect it takes away all absolute decrees whatsoever with respect to future events: for the main events, those that concern the acts of intelligent creatures, being undetermined and so yet remaining contingent, not determined by anything that is as yet, but remaining yet to receive their first determination from men themselves, this makes it necessary that an absolute determination about lesser events should be suspended. 'Tis those main events are determined, because 'tis needfull that the lesser events should be ordered one way or another according as the greater events shall be. The main events must govern the lesser, and if the lesser should be absolutely determined before the greater are, God may be disappointed: for those greater events still remaining contingent, they may come out so as to make it necessary that the lesser events should be ordered otherwise, that this may be accommodated one to another and that the compages and series of things may be consistent with itself. Without doubt, the habitation is made for its inhabitants, as the inferior, inanimate and irrational part of the universe is made for the intelligent part that inhabits it; and therefore 'tis necessary that events in the parts of the habitation should be accommodated to the state of the inhabitants. If God has any design by the events and changes in the various parts of the habitation, it must be something relating to intelligent inhabitants, for no end can be obtained any other way. If the universe had no inhabitatants, and there were nothing but unperceiving beings, it is easy to see that no end could be obtained by any motions or events on it: no end could be obtained, for nothing capable of good could in any respect be the better or worse for them in any respect, nor any glory of God manifested, no good shown, nor any perceived. And therefore it will follow that all events in the habitation could [not] have any end but with respect to the inhabitants, and therefore alterations and changes can't be wisely ordered but with respect to that end, or as accommodated to the state of the inhabitants. Therefore, if those alterations are absolutely predetermined, 'tis necessary that that state of intelligent creatures should also be predetermined. There is no one event in the inferior, brutal or inanimate world that we read of in the Scriptures or any other history that can be pitched upon as a predetermined event but what some way or other so concerns the state of intelligent inhabitants, that it was absolutely necessary that the state of those inhabitants should be predetermined in order to its being absolutely predetermined with wisdom; and but that if the state of intelligent beings that were concerned in it was undetermined, it would be evidently most wise to suspend the determination of that event. Especially is it so with respect to all important events: and if we deny that any important events are predetermined of God, then surely there is nothing left that it is important to contend about; if we deny that ever [God] absolutely decrees any event of any importance, 'tis of no importance to contend that he ever decreed any event at all: thus do they that deny God's absolute decrees and disposing providence with respect to the will and free actions of rational creatures in effect deny all absolute decrees whatsoever.

We have much more evidence of God's absolutely foreordaining the free action of men than anything thing else whatsoever: for most of the things that are mentioned in Scripture as the objects of God's decrees are acts and events depending on the wills of men, and there is no one event that can be mentioned that ever was foretold of God but what was either dependent on men's free will, or did so concern the acts and state of mankind depending on their will, so as that it is most easy to see it would be either impossible to predetermine it or unwise till the acts of men's wills were determined.

FOREKNOWLEDGE proves predetermination. 'Tis plainly impossible that an event should be foreknown that it shall certainly be, while it yet remains a thing merely contingent whether it should be or no. It is a contradiction; 'tis as much as to say that a thing is certainly to be, that is as yet absolutely contingent and uncertain whether it is to be or no. If the event be foreknown certainly that 'tis to be, then it is certainly to be; and if it be certainly to be, then something has already determined its being, either a divine decree or something else. Something has rendered a thing fixed and settled, and not vague and merely contingent, whether it is to be or no. If the future being of anything be already a thing fixed and settled, and not remaining vague and uncertain, then something has made it so. If the futurition of the event be fixed and limited, 'tis fixed and limited by something. If an event certainly hereafter be, then 'tis something more than merely possible. But30 things that ben't necessarily existent in themselves are no more than merely possibly. And therefore if there be something more31 besides possibility, there be certain futurition; there must be some cause of it, i.e. there must be something that predetermines its being, or brings it out of a state of possibility into a state of futurition. The futurition of the event is fixed and limited, so that the mark will certainly not be missed; otherwise it must remain in itself an uncertain thing whether it will be or no. If there be no certainty or fixation of the event, then it can't be discerned or known; 'tis impossible that more should be discerned than is. If the futurition of the event be contingent, then it is discerned to be contingent, and that is quite inconsistent with its being discerned to be certain.

And will the meer Powers of Nature ever carry any any man so far, as to be constant, fervent, and persevering in all the Ways of known Duty? —- None can pretend, that these Promises are made to an hypocritical Performance of Duty; to those that flatter God with their Lips, and lie to him with their Tongues, while their Hearts are far from him. And can any unsanctify'd Person be hearty and sincere, in all his religious Performances? —- None can pretend, that these Promises are made to such who bring their Lusts with them to God, in all their Approaches to him." In pp. 159-65, Dickinson answers objections that "it is vain for unconverted Sinners to seek for Grace, since they have no Claim to it when they have done all they can" (159) and "That it takes from Man all Freedom of Will, and renders him a meer Machine, uncapable of all moral Good, even as if he were a Tree or a Beast." In the "Discourse of the Decree of Election," on Eph. 1:4-5, Dickinson (pp. 31-39) again answers the objection that election "takes away the Liberty of the Creature." His formulation is similar to JE's "greatest apparent good": "We could not be free Agents, if we had not a Power to will what appears to us from our present View of things most fit to be chosen; or if we had a Power to will what appears to us from our present View of Things unfit to be chosen. For either of these supposes the Will it self to be the Effect of some Constraint from something without us; and not the Result of our own Understanding, Affections, and Appetites, as it always is in every free Agent.... If Freedom consists in a Power to will or not to will what appears to the Mind in its present View of things the fittest Object of Choice, then the holy Angels and glorified Saints are not free: for they cannot help but will the Glory of God, and their own Holiness and Happiness" (pp. 34-35). ↩

The passage reads: "Their scheme is self-contradictory, for it makes a man justified where he is: for it supposes a man [to] have a right to salvation before he is justified. For, first, they suppose that a man is not justified till he is become godly, or has special and saving grace; but, secondly, they hold that he has a right to salvation before he has special grace, viz. by a good improvement of common grace. For they say he has a right to special grace, which implies salvation; but he that has a right to that which imples a right to salvation, has already a right to salvation, and can challenge it. He can challenge it already; as, for instance, he that has a right to be looked upon as the king's eldest son, he therein has a right to the crown because, being the king's eldest son implies the other.
"So that, according to this scheme, a man is justified not by evangelical obedience, but by the good improvement of common grace. The absurdities, viz.: first, that he is justified before he is; second, justified by that which is no godliness, which in itself is an absurdity; and thirdly, that yet he is justified only by his godliness.
"They say that God has promised special grace to a good improvement of common grace, and salvation to special grace. Therefore, I say, that he has in effect promised salvation to only a good improvement of common grace, so that he can then immediately challenge it without further delay. Thus, for instance, if one man promises another that if he will do such a thing, and at the same promises, that if he has the possession of another thing, and that if he has that other thing, he will give him another, and when he has that, another: I say, that in such a case, as soon as ever he has performed the first condition, he has a right to all the other things that follow one another, and can challenge 'em then without further delay; because as soon as that first is performed, that other stands bound to bestow all the rest. So that the proper condition of salvation is the good improvement of common grace, which they themselves allow is no godliness.

29. The sermon on Ps. 27:4 is dated May 1738; it is written in a medium brown ink that is very similar to that in this entry. The doctrine is, "God never begrutches his people anything they desire or are capable of as being too good for 'em." In the second use of the application, JE states that if the doctrine is true, then "here is matter of great encouragement for God's people in their prayers." He then goes on to pose an objection regarding unanswered prayers, in which individuals "have often prayed to God, and God don't seem to hear them." He answers that, as far as prayers for temporal blessings are concerned, the things we ask for God sees "are not best for you." Thus not receiving wealth, goods, etc. is "no argument that God begrutches you anything." In regard to prayers for spiritual light, JE states that God bestows this "in his own time." Also, those who have prayed for spiritual comfort may not have done so in sincerity; this is not a real prayer, because a prayer not done "'with a humble sense of their unworthiness of what is prayed for" is not sincere. A real prayer is "the voice of faith." ↩
