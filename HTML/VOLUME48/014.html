283. Cant. 2:3(a) 283.Cant. 2'3a(5_33) edited Jonathan Edwards
TRUE BELIEVER HATH REST IN CHRIST Scripture TRUE BELIEVER HATH REST IN CHRIST1 Canticles 2:3 As the apple tree among the trees of the wood, so is my beloved among the sons. I sat down under his shadow with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste.

Christ and his church are here in this and the foregoing verses setting forth each other's excellencies as compared with others. Christ says of his church in the preceding verse: "As the lily among thorns, so is my love among the daughters"; and here in the text the church is declaring the sense she has of, and delight in, the glory and excellency of Christ2.

And we may observe in the words,

1. How far Christ excels others as the apple tree among {the trees of the wood}. Whether by "the sons" here we understand the sons of God or the sons of men, including both, the comparison is just. Christ became one of the sons of men by his incarnation; he commonly calls himself the Son of Man, but is the most excellent of all the sons of men; Psalms 45:2, "Thou art fairer than the children of men." Or if we understand it of the sons of God: though Christ be the only begotten Son of God, i. e. the only natural Son of God, yet there are others that in Scriptures are called the sons of God. So true believers are called; I John 3:2, "Beloved, now we are the sons of God"; but Christ is among these sons also as the apple {tree among the trees of the wood}. He has taken the saints to an union with himself; he calls them his brethren. But as he is the elder brother, so he is infinitely the most excellent in this family; others are mean and vile in comparison of him, and that whether we understand it of saints on earth, or saints in heaven.

The angels are also called sons of God, Job 38:7, but Christ far excels these also. He is the head and king of the angels, "that in all things he might have the preeminence" [Colossians 1:18]; Hebrews 1:4, "Being made so much better than the angels, as he hath by inheritance obtained a more excellent name than they."

2. The spouse declares her own happiness in him: "I sat down under {his shadow with great delight, and his fruit was sweet to my taste}." Here in these words is eloquently set forth, (1) the rest she had in him, expressed in that clause that I would now especially insist upon, "I sat down under his shadow"; (2) the joy she had in him. She says it was with great delight that she sat under his shadow, and that his fruit was sweet to her taste.

His shadow was most refreshing to her after she had been wearied under the hot sunbeams beating on her head and had been wandering about, seeking rest and finding none. It was most refreshing to her when she came to sit down under this cool shadow; she found that rest and refreshment there that [were] most delightful to her. And as the3 shadow of this tree was refreshing, so the fruit was sweet. While she sat under the shadow, she was not only protected from the hot scorching sunbeams and had quiet rest there, but she had opportunity to eat the fruit of the tree for her further refreshment and strengthening. Persons when weary and beat out with labor or travailing,4 they not only need a place to sit down and rest, but they need refreshment inwardly, they need food to strengthen them. The spouse, she finds both in Christ, under his shadow; she eats his fruit which is most sweet and pleasant to her taste.

Doctrine

The true believer hath rest in Christ.

This is very emphatically signified in those words of the spouse, "I sat down under this shadow." Her rest is implied both in her action or posture: "I sat down" - sitting down is a posture of rest; "after I had been long fatigued and wearied, I reposed myself" - and also in that it is under a shadow. A cool shadow is a place of rest, where persons are protected from tedious heat and are wont to rest themselves when weary.5

Prop. I. Persons before they come to Christ are in a restless condition. 'Tis thus with all natural men whatsoever, but especially with those whose consciences God has awakened.

First. All natural men whatsoever are in a restless condition. Many natural men seem to be at ease; they seem to go along through the world without much to disturb them. But there is no man living that enjoys true rest, that is out of Christ, whatever plenty of earthly things he enjoys. If he fares sumptuously and has never so many of the delights of this life, yet if he be out of Christ, he is in a restless state. It is impossible that man, whilst he remains separate from God, the author of his being and fountain of all good, should enjoy true rest: for he is then absent from his true happiness, from his proper resting place.

A natural man is in a restless condition two ways, viz.

1. As he enjoys no true peace of conscience. 'Tis impossible that he should, for there is no foundation for it. A natural man may have his conscience seared and his mind dreadfully stupefied and benumbed, but yet he has no true peace of conscience. Man has those principles in his nature that render it impossible for him to be at rest whilst in such a state as all natural men are in, for God has endowed man with a natural conscience and a faculty of reason which, as long as they remain, will disturb and disrest a natural man. Natural men are all under guilt, and where guilt and natural conscience meet together and are in the same subject, there will not, and cannot, be peace, but unquietness and disturbance. Though man by habits and customs of sinning may greatly benumb natural conscience, yet every man has such a principle6 which, so far as it exerts itself, let it be less or more, will disturb the soul.

As it is in the outward man, there is difference between stupidity and rest. There is a great deal of difference between that benumbedness and insensibility a man may be under in an apoplexy or by a blow on the head, and sweet and quiet repose. So it is in the inward man.

God has endowed man with a faculty of reason, and where reason and a declaration of the wrath of God and evil and continual danger of eternal misery meet, there can't be true peace. Though man may do much towards blinding this reason, yet so far as a natural man exercises his reason, so far he must ever more be disrested and his peace excluded. That can't be true rest that is built upon no other foundation than reason's blindness. And however free some men may appear from all disturbance from reason or conscience, yet God only, and their own heart only, know what they meet with. Again,

2. A natural man enjoys no true contentment. He is not satisfied; none of the enjoyments that he possesses give him rest. A natural man is restlessly pursuing after something to fill the cravings of his soul; Psalms 4:6, "Who will shew us any good?" And let him have what he will of worldly enjoyments, still he will remain unsatisfied, as 'tis not in the power of riches, or honor, or sensual gratification to satisfy, to give rest to the soul; they are but shadows, and those that depend upon them are "as when a hungry man dreameth, and, behold, he eateth; but he awaketh, and his soul is empty" [Isaiah 29:8].

All men are seeking rest daily; 'tis their continual work. The mind of a natural man will be restless, for it has nothing to fix it but its own fleeting variable imagination, whereby he vainly imagines that there is happiness in this thing and the other, wherein it is not, and so is perpetually wandering from one thing to another in a restless manner. Sin is of such a nature that it won't suffer the soul to be at rest while it reigns in the heart. It puts all things out of order and keeps the soul in a tumult; Isaiah 57:20, "the wicked are like the troubled sea that cannot rest, whose waters cast up mire and dirt."

Second. Especially is it so with natural men when God awakens conscience by his Spirit. Then those principles of reason and natural conscience do their proper work and make7 'em sensible of their guilt and danger, which, if it be seen, can't but exceeding disrest the mind. When God awakens the soul, then is even that false rest and stupid peace taken away; the threatenings of God's law and the declaration of divine anger in God's holy Word, are like arrows that stick in the heart and cause continual pain and restlessness. The hearts of such are wounded with a wound that they carry about with them day and night. If a man has a thorn or any such thing remaining in his flesh, how restless will it make him, till he is freed from it! How much more will the sharp arrows of conviction, when sticking in the heart! Such an one is heavy laden, he has a weight lying on his mind that he is restless till he gets rid of. Such an one travails,8 as it were, under the scorching beams of the sun beating on his head. The soul in such a case earnestly seeks rest: it wanders here and there and flies from one thing to another, seeking rest; it seeks rest, it seeks rest in its own righteousness, it seeks rest in the creature,9 it seeks rest in the absolute mercy of God; it wanders about from mount to hill,10 seeking rest and finding none.

Sinners under awakening may trust in their own righteousness and in other carnal confidence, but they have no true rest or acquiescence of soul in anything. And when God gives persons thorough convictions, he disrests them in all their refuge; he won't suffer them to rest in any of them, but he brings them to see that they are utterly unsafe and undone, and that all their confidences are as a spider's web.

Prop. II. When persons have closed with Christ, they have rest in him. When the poor, restless, wandering soul has Christ in his glory and fullness discovered to it and comes to him, then it hath found its proper resting place. It finds that rest in him that it never could find in anything else; here it finds what in vain it hath sought for in worldly enjoyments and in his own righteousness.11 In him doth he find ease from his burden; he assuages the pain and anguish of his wound and applies that healing balm and pours in that oil of comfort that restores the soul.12 The believing soul enjoys in Christ a sweet calm and peace that passes understanding.13 He may then look back on past dangers and disturbances he was in in a Christless state, as the mariner reflects on the storms that he has been in after he is got to a safe harbor, or at home and under a safe roof. The true believer is like Noah's dove, who, while absent from the ark, found no rest for the sole of her foot till she returned to the ark;14 there she had a quiet, secure resting place.

The true believer hath a twofold rest in Christ: the rest of reliance, and the rest of contentment and satisfaction.

First. The true believer rests in Christ as he relies upon him; he rests in him as a Savior. The believer's soul hath a sweet acquiescence in Christ as a Savior as the gospel reveals and offers him; he hath rest in Christ as a mediator between God and him, to deliver him from wrath and to make peace between God and him; he rests in Him as the author and means of all happiness. Herein a true trust differs from a feigned false trust in Him. Herein does that reliance upon Christ that is from the powerful saving influence of the Spirit of God, differ from that which is forced and that men themselves are authors of. He that truly relies on Christ as a Savior has a satisfying rest and sweet acquiescence of soul in him as a real, a sufficient, a willing and faithful Savior. The hypocrite, however he may seemingly force a dependence on Christ and may flatter himself that Christ is his Savior, has not this real, entire rest of soul in him.15

1. The true believer rests in it that there is such a Savior. A person in a natural condition, that is under a sense of guilt and danger of eternal wrath is sensible of his need of salvation, but he don't rest in it that there is a savior. But he that believes is convinced of it that there is indeed a savior, and that that Savior is Jesus Christ. He rests in it that the Scripture, which declares that Christ is the Savior, is the Word of God and that the gospel is true; he sees that in the gospel that convinces him that it is of God and that it is the truth. Another hears that Christ is a Savior often enough, but hearing will [not] satisfy a soul that has a sense of his guilt. A natural person may read or hear many probable rational arguments in favor of it, but neither will these cause the awakened soul to rest in it that it is so. The soul that has a sense of the wrath of God won't be satisfied without seeing. Let it never so often be declared that 'tis so, and never so many plausible arguments be offered, that soul will not acquiesce in it that the gospel indeed is true, unless it sees that the gospel is true, i. e. unless it sees that in the gospel that is a plain evidence of the truth of it. But he that truly believes is convinced of, and doth acquiesce in, the truth of it.

Not but that a true believer may have temptations concerning the truth of the gospel, as he mayn't have the satisfying evidence of the truth of the gospel always in view. He may not have faith always in exercise; sometimes remaining unbelief is uppermost. Yet when faith has its proper act and the believer acts himself, 'tis then with him.

2. The believer acquiesces in the suffering of Christ as a Savior. He sees that glory in Christ that convinces him that he is equal to the work that he has undertaken, that there is enough in Christ for him to answer his necessities. The apprehension he has of the glory of Christ suffices him as to this matter, sets him at rest concerning it, that Christ is a sufficient Savior.

This is a great difficulty with a natural man that is sensible of guilt and wrath. He is not satisfied of Christ's sufficiency to save him; he can't fully rest in it that there16 is enough in Christ for one that has committed so much wickedness and made God so angry. But he that believes in Christ has a satisfying conviction that there is worthiness and merit enough in Christ for him, however guilty he has been and however dreadfully he has displeased God. He rests in it that Christ's blood is sufficient to atone for all his sins and to procure him peace with God, however angry He hath been with him. He rests in it that Christ hath wrought out a righteousness sufficient to commend him to God and purchase heaven, and that 'tis safe appearing in his righteousness.17 He sees enough in Christ to set his heart at rest as to these things, and [that] convinces him that there is no need of his seeking further, no need of any other means of salvation, that here is all that justice demands and all that this case requires.

The believer also rests in it that Christ is able to bestow the salvation purchased, that he is able to bring to the actual possession of eternal life. Though there appear many difficulties in the way, yet the believer acquiesces in that, that Christ is able to save him to the uttermost, overcome all enemies and remove all difficulties. We may see the language of faith in Matthew 8:2, "Lord, if thou wilt, thou canst make me clean"; and Matthew 9:21, "If I may but touch the hem of his garment, I shall be whole,"18 and the twenty-eighth verse, "Jesus said unto them, Believe ye that I am able to do this? They said unto him, Yea, Lord."

3. The believer rests in it that Christ is willing to save. This is what unbelievers under convictions of their sin and danger do not acquiesce in; they fear that Christ won't be willing to accept of 'em if they come to him, and therefore do not realize it that the calls and invitations of Christ are directed to them. They are told often that Christ is willing to {save}, but they don't rest in it that there is such love and grace in Christ as to make him willing to receive such guilty creatures as they. But the true believer has that discovery of the wonderful riches of the grace and love of Jesus Christ, of the fullness and freeness of it, that it satisfies him that He has grace enough even to accept of such a vile unworthy creature as he; sees so much as encourages him to venture.

4. The believer rests in the faithfulness of Christ that he will fulfill his promises. Christ not only is able to save and has mercy enough to save, but he has promised to save all that come to him. Unbelievers dare not trust those promises, but believers rest in Christ's faithfulness. They can venture their souls upon him and quietly repose themselves upon this confidence, that he is faithful that hath promised.19 The confidence that the believer hath of these things encourages him to rely upon Christ and gives him a sweet repose and calm of mind.20

Thus the believer rests in Christ with a rest of reliance.

Second. He rests in him with a rest of contentment and satisfaction. This kind of rest is not to be found in any other but a divine person. 'Tis in Christ only, and in God through him, that any of the children of men have any true rest and satisfaction. The believer has this sort of rest in Christ two ways:

1. The believer is entirely pleased with, and acquiesces in, the way of salvation by Christ. He don't only trust in Christ for salvation, depends on salvation in no other way, but he chooses it. The will, as well as the other faculty, doth entirely acquiesce in it. Of all ways of salvation, if other ways were possible, this would be by far the most pleasing and acceptable to a believer, and if it were left to him in what way he would be saved, he would with abundant alacrity and cheerfulness choose this way; there would be no hesitation which way to choose, he would choose it before being saved by his own righteousness, or any other way whatsoever. 'Tis a way that the believer delights in. There is a sweet harmony between the soul of the believer and this way of salvation by Christ; it entirely accords and consents to it. The soul of a believer is cast into the mold of the gospel. The believer has had it discovered to him what a wise and glorious way this is, how suitable a way, that he is wholly contented with it.21 The way of salvation by Christ is so excellent and glorious a way, that everyone that understands it must rest in it, having found so glorious a way and having an understanding of it given him by the Spirit of God. He rests here, here he sits down, he seeks no farther for a way of salvation, and desires no other.

Natural men under convictions, they are restlessly seeking some way of salvation; they han't found, don't understand the true way, and therefore they are seeking other ways. They are devising ways of their own and [are] restlessly wandering from one thing to another. But the believer, having had a discovery of the right way and of the suitableness and excellency of it, is fixed, he has done wandering here and there, he has made his fixed and final choice of this way of life.

Man is naturally prejudiced against this way of salvation. It don't at all suit the disposition of the heart as it is by nature; there is a great enmity against [it]. There are many things in it that are directly contrary to those principles, that do naturally prevail and reign in men's hearts, and therefore natural men are far from acquiescing in this way. On the contrary, he is finding fault with it, and is offended at it, and is full of objections against it. Man naturally don't like to be saved by Christ; he is disposed to set himself. But a believer delights in this way of salvation and chooses it chiefly for those very things upon the account of which an unbeliever dislikes it and is offended at it. A believer chooses to renounce himself and chooses to ascribe all the glory of salvation to Christ; he chooses rather that Christ should have the glory of his salvation than himself.

A believer hath rest both in the way of procuring salvation and also in the way of bestowing it.

(1) In the way of procuring. The way of procuring salvation is by the worthiness of Jesus Christ. This a believer is well-pleased with, and acquiesces in, because it humbles and abases man, and exalts Christ.

(2) In the way of bestowing it. The way of bestowing salvation is by the government of Christ. Christ procures salvation in the exercise of his priestly office, but he bestows it in the exercise of his kingly office. The believer accepts of Christ for his king and chooses that he should govern him and rule over him. The consideration of the necessity of being subject to Christ's government causes no unquietness in the mind of the believer; 'tis what he [is] entirely quiet and at rest about, and rejoices in it. He rests in Christ's commandments, he loves, and delights in, obedience unto them.

2. The believer rests, and is satisfied in, Christ as a portion. Man necessarily seeks happiness, and this is what the world is restlessly in pursuit of, viz., something for a satisfying portion, a portion in which they may be happy, or wherein their craving souls may have satisfaction. The believer, having found this pearl,22 seeks no further; he has done with that inquiry, "Who will show me any good,23 where shall I find a satisfying portion?" Being come to Christ, he sits down here and ceases his wandering from mount to hill.

When men come to Christ and come to experience something of the happiness that is to be had in him, they find another kind of pleasure than ever they found in anything else. 'Tis not like a mere vanishing flash or shadow, 'tis a substantial delight, such as is found to be suitable to the spiritual and rational nature of the soul, and such as has power to content and satisfy it. John 4:13-14, "Jesus answered and said unto her, Whosoever drinketh of this water shall thirst again: But he that drinketh of the water that I shall give shall never thirst; but the water that I shall give him shall be in him a well of water springing up into everlasting life."

The pleasure and joy that is to be had in Christ differs from temporal and carnal delights in this respect, that it is of such a nature that those that have tasted it desire no other kind of enjoyment; not but that they desire more of the same, for 'tis the character of the godly man, that he thirsts for God. He that has tasted and seen that the Lord is gracious24 longs after Christ. But they are satisfied and at rest in this respect, as they desire no change. It is not so with temporal enjoyment: after a man has had one temporal enjoyment a little while, he can't be satisfied with it, he wants something new. But the soul is never cloyed or weary in perpetually exerting itself with all its might for the enjoyment of that spiritual delight that is to be had in Christ, so as to desire any change.

There is difference between the believer being satisfied in Christ and a being satisfied in what he already enjoys of him. His not being satisfied with that that he already enjoys of him, is no sign that he is not satisfied in Christ. But on25 the contrary, if the believer was satisfied with that little that he already enjoys of Christ without longing for more, it would be a sign that he was not satisfied in Christ, and had not a very great esteem of him. But if he [is] satisfied in Christ, i. e. if that kind of happiness that is in Christ be what he finds to be of a truly satisfying nature, he will necessarily long for a full enjoyment of him. The believer, though he desires more of Christ, yet by what he hath tasted is convinced that there is enough in Christ to make him perfectly happy; and therefore, having found in him all that he wants, he rests in him and seeks no further.

Every faculty of the believer is satisfied and at rest in Christ. The understanding rests here, it desires no other object to be its portion, to entertain in26 contemplation; the glory of Christ is object enough for the entertainment of that extensive faculty.27 The will and affections are at rest in the beauty and love of Christ; here they are immovably fixed and need no other object to entertain and fill them, and be their everlasting food. The soul of the believer is at rest in Christ, as it desires not a more glorious object, a more sweet and delightful good than Jesus Christ.28

Application

I. Use of Instruction. Hence those that believe in Christ are the happiest men in the world. If we would know who are happy, we must inquire who they are that do truly enjoy rest. Many make a pretence and show of happiness, but 'tis in vain to pretend to it, as long as they still continue, yet restlessly seeking something new, craving still as much as those that are poor. Many ungodly men think themselves happier than the godly. The sensual man, he that wallows himself in carnal pleasures, he thinks himself much happier than those that fear God, and deny themselves, and live a spiritual life. The worldly rich man despises the poor godly man and looks upon himself [as] far more happy than he. The proud man, in his honor and advancement, looks with contempt on the humble and meek and lowly of heart that are in mean outward circumstances.

But let it be inquired who have the truest rest, who have the most inward quietness and satisfaction of mind. The mind of man has an appetite after happiness that is essential and necessary to it, and it is impossible it should be separated from it without destroying its being. This appetite in man is violent and impetuous, and 'tis therefore impossible that the mind of man should be at rest till it has found some object wherein true happiness is to be enjoyed. They, therefore, that have true rest, we may well conclude that they are the happy persons.

They that are true believers in Christ, therefore, only, are they that have found true happiness, for they only have found rest for their souls. The kings and princes of the world that are out of Christ, however pompous and glittering a show they make, they han't found rest; their earthly crowns won't afford rest to their souls. They have obtained great wealth, and they have obtained great honor, and they have opportunity to swim in sensual delight; they have obtained earthly power, they have the gratification to behold whole nations subject to, and some of them have obtained an absolute dominion over many nations, over vast regions of the earth: but they han't obtained that that [the] meanest, poorest Christian that dwells in a cottage has obtained, viz. rest for his soul. Notwithstanding all this glorious outward show, yet inward peace and satisfaction is wanting. The good Christian on a dunghill has more in his mind than they have in all their treasures, more than their stately palaces with all the ornaments and delights that they are filled with can boast of, more than they have within the compass of their vast dominions.

The godly man's enjoyments are not so much sought after as other men's; there are other things that do more dazzle the eyes of men, and that are more coveted, and that men are more ambitious of, and that the possessors of are more eminent for, than the godly man's happiness in his Savior. The comfort and happiness of a godly man is a hidden happiness, his life is a hidden life. A stranger intermeddles not with his joy; 'tis not a thing that makes a great show and appearance outwardly, as carnal men's enjoyments do; ;tis inward, and in that part that is most capable of exquisite delight and satisfaction. 'Tis a happiness in the enjoyment of which the believer is not much concerned with other men or with earthly things, and happiness that he is not dependent on the creature for, and that he don't need the creature to uphold or contribute to.

First. 'Tis between God and his own soul. 'Tis a happiness that he has immediately from Christ, without much intermeddling with other things; and therefore it is, that it is so secret and hidden from the observation of the world. The carnal man's happiness is outward, and therefore visible. It consists in the enjoyment of outward things. He is concerned with the creature and is dependent on visible objects, and therefore it makes a show and appearance to man's bodily eyes. The man that has rest in Christ is not ostentatious of his happiness, he don't need, nor desire, to make a show of it; 'tis enough with him in itself, 'tis satisfying in its own nature, and he don't need any such additions as the honor of it. Worldly happiness consists very much in the thoughts of the world, in being thought to be happy by others, which makes those that enjoy worldly things ambitious of its being taken notice of by others; but 'tis a sign of the emptiness of it that it needs any such thing to add to it, that is but a miserable, empty sort of happiness that is so much dependent on the thoughts and imaginations of the world.

A man can't be happy without inward peace. What will it profit a man, and how will it help, to clothe him with scarlet and deck him with pearls, and heap silver and gold upon him, and to bow the knee before [him], as long as he is destitute of peace in his own mind, is inwardly disquieted, has a worm inwardly gnawing and tormenting him? But there is none that is not in Christ that has true inward peace. Natural man may have a benumbedness and stupidity of mind, a brutal senselessness and carelessness,29 but he can't enjoy the peace of a rational creature. 'Tis the believer alone that has the experience of this, and therefore alone is truly happy.

None but believers in Christ can have true peace of conscience. 'Tis not in the power of any other offering or sacrifice, but that of Christ, to give peace of conscience; Hebrews 9:9, "Which was a figure for the time then present, in which were offered both gifts and sacrifices, which cannot make the comers thereunto perfect as pertaining to the conscience."30 Men may flatter themselves in other things; a man may flatter himself in his own righteousness, but he never can have true peace of conscience in his own righteousness. He that hath a disquieted conscience, this will embitter all other enjoyment. It will be an alloy in all a man's pleasures, it will be a canker on his silver and gold, it will not suffer him quietly to enjoy anything; but he that has peace of conscience, it tends on the contrary to sweeten all other enjoyment. It adds a sweetness to his bread and his meat, and enables him to relish the blessings of God's common providence. A poor man's mean fare, his small substance, is better and more desirable with peace of conscience than the richest delicacies and sumptuous fare of the wealthiest and greatest; Ecclesiastes 4:6, "Better is an handful with quietness, than both the hands full with travail and vexation of spirit."

Here I would mention some propagations of this rest that the believer hath in Christ, above all other seeming rest that men have in carnal things.

1. 'Tis not a rest that depends on blindness and mistake, but on the contrary, on light and truth. That quietness that men seem to have that are out of Christ in worldly enjoyments, or in their own righteousness, depends on blindness; there could be no quietness if men's eyes were open. A carnal man could not have that security and ease in his own mind, that he has, were he not deluded and blindfold; if it were not for that, he could not please himself as he does in his sensual possessions and delights. If it were not for this, he could not flatter himself in his own righteousness.

A wicked man, even he that is in the greatest prosperity, has no ease or quietness any otherwise than a blind man may in a house that is on fire over his head, or walking carelessly on the brink of a dreadful pit, or before the mouths of loaden cannons with matches lighted. The security of a blind man in such a case is because he is blind, his quietness depends on his blindness; so does all that ease and all that seeming peace that carnal men enjoy in carnal things. The very foundation of the carnal man's ease and seeming happiness is a mistake, a mistaken imagination. Take away that mistake and set the man right in his mind, and all his rest fails, all his happiness falls to the ground.

But 'tis not so with the believer's rest that he hath in Christ. His rest is not dependent on his blindness, but on the contrary, his sight and discerning of the truth that is the foundation of it. 'Tis a rational ease and rest; it don't depend on darkness. 'Tis not because the believer is in darkness, and don't see what circumstances he is in, that he is at rest; but it depends on light. 'Tis because he does see, and the more bright and clear the light is, the more does it confirm and establish his peace and rest. The more fully he knows things to be as they are, the more undisturbed is his rest in Christ. The believer's rest in Christ is a well-grounded rest; he has reason to be at rest and quietness, there is good foundation for it: for in Christ Jesus there is indeed safety; he that is in Christ is safe from evil, he is built on a foundation that it is impossible should fail. In Christ Jesus there is sufficiency, and the more the believer sees of Christ, sees his glory and faithfulness and fullness, the more established and the more undisturbed will his rest in him be.

2. This rest is no shortlived rest. That seeming rest that carnal men have in earthly enjoyment is shortlived: the things that they settle upon as the foundation of their rest are a running sand, the things they choose for a happiness are fleeting shadows; they please the fancy of the beholder for a moment, and are gone; they soon leave men in a most miserable condition, in a state wherein they have no rest, day or night. How often does it come to pass, that while men are sleeping securely and dream nothing of danger being nigh, they are suddenly hurled out of their place, a tempest stealeth them away in the night, Job 27:20-21! Then what becomes of his rest, what becomes of his boasted happiness? Terrors then take hold on his waters, and those terrors never leave him; he is never suffered any more to enjoy one moment's ease or rest.

What rest can a man have that is burning in a furnace of fire, and has the wrath of the Almighty continually poured out upon his soul to disquiet him? What rest can a man have that has the peals of the thunder of the Almighty perpetually coming down his head, and the sharp arrows of his wrath continually piercing his heart? What rest can a man have that is full of quick sense, and yet is all swallowed up in a lake of fire and brimstone, and is full of fire in every part within and without, full of the fierceness of the wrath of [the] Almighty?

But this is what all other rest will soon turn to but that rest that is in Jesus Christ.31 'Tis this that the hypocrite's rest, that is not in Christ but in himself, will soon turn to; Job 20:5, "the joy of the hypocrite is but for a moment."

But the believer's rest in Christ is a continuing, lasting rest. He that rests in Christ, rests where he never shall be disturbed; he that is built upon this foundation, he is built where he may quietly remain forever; the greatest floods and most violent storms never will be able to unsettle him. He that is got into this ark may hear the storms beat violently without, but may repose himself within, without fear of disturbance; he may look abroad and see the waves of the deluge toss themselves and rage, without any power to reach or molest him. He that is set down under the shadow of this tree, there he may forever enjoy his sweet repose without any danger of the heat or sun's smiting him, Isaiah 49:10. He that has rest in Christ, no change can deprive him of it. If he passes through never so many and so great changes, and whatever assaults are upon him, yea, death itself can't rob a man of this rest; when his body is dissolved and turns to putrefaction and the worms devour [it], yet the saint's rest in Christ remains. This rest is an eternal rest. When the believer rests by faith in Christ in this world, 'tis the beginning of his heavenly and eternal rest.

The durableness of this rest that believers have in Christ has many times been remarkably evidenced in the great trials that believers have had, and when their rest has been assaulted by their most powerful enemies. It is a thing that has often been tried not only by Satan, but by men, by kings and the most powerful monarchs upon earth, who have used their utmost power to move believers from their reliance upon, and rest in, Christ, [who] have used to that end the greatest torments and cruelties that they could invent and inflict, but yet have been conquered, have not been able to overthrow Christians' rest in Christ.

3. Another property of this rest that believers have in Christ is that it can make a man happy alone and don't need the help of other things in order to it. A man in resting in Christ may be happy and enjoy a quiet repose of mind, a sweet and joyful habit; he may be full of contentment and may relish a complacential delight, trusting in Christ being his glory and enjoying communion with him in the greatest poverty, in the most indigent circumstances, and when stripped of all other things. A dungeon or a desert may become happy, and often has been turned into a paradise by believers' acquiescence and complacence in Jesus Christ.

4. This rest can render a man happy, whatever opposition be made against it. If God is pleased to enable a man to rely upon Christ, and if Christ discovers his glory and love, that will be sufficient to uphold a brightness and pleasure in the soul, let who will set themselves to oppose it, and let them use their utmost strength to that end.

This is evident by the trials that believers have sometimes had. The most powerful monarchs have endeavored and done their utmost to destroy the peace of a believer. Often times [they] have been unable, by all that they could do, to disturb the comfort and sweet joy and delight that [believers] have in Christ; but believers have given evidences that they have indeed enjoyed their rest, a true sweet rest in Christ, in the flames, on gridirons, and on the rack, and under the extremest outward tortures.

Before I proceed to another use, I would briefly answer an objection that may [be] made against this inference of believers being the happiest men in the world, viz.,

Obj. Don't we often see that those that are accounted believers are very much dejected, and that they sometimes live a very afflicted life? Don't they seem evidently to live a much more melancholy life, to have more things to distress them and disquiet them than many natural men?

Ans. 1. Those Christians that meet with much darkness and sorrow, yet what comfort they enjoy may be better than all that carnal men enjoy. We will suppose that he be a person that meets with a great deal of spiritual darkness, which indeed is oftentimes very distressing to the soul, and his spiritual darkness be accompanied with outward affliction, and he may have far less light than many other Christians; yet that little spiritual light and comfort that he sometimes has, may be far better than all that the richest and merriest of natural men enjoy.32 I don't mean only better morally, but the comfort and pleasure may be worth more; the happiness he enjoys in it, if laid in the scales, may weigh down all the carnal man's mirth.

It is an ill way of judging of others' happiness to judge by the outward appearance of mirth and gaiety, to determine that the wicked man lives most happily because he has the most merry countenance and gives the greatest external signs of ease and merriment. Some such who in the outward appearance seem to be so gay and cheerful, yet if [we] could look into the inward recesses of their hearts and know all their secret uneasiness and the inward pain they endure, we should change our minds concerning their happiness. That mirth that they enjoy that makes such a show is very superficial, it is like the crackling of thorns {under a pot}, Ecclesiastes 7:6, and does 'em but very little good; and a little of the light of God's countenance, and of holy rest and joy in Christ, is worth a whole age of it.

2. If it be so as is said in the objection, their having so much trouble is not because they are believers, but because they are no more of believers. This objection, if what it asserts be allowed, is not to the discredit of faith, nor of a rest in Christ. It argues nothing against the tendency of faith to make man happy, that some believers have so much trouble and distress and live such a melancholy life, because this is not from their faith that they do so, but from the want of faith; 'tis not because they are believers, but that they are no more of believers.

'Tis because faith is so imperfect that believers, while in this world, are so liable to spiritual darkness; 'tis because there is33 so great remainder of unbelief in them. 'Tis for want of a due sense of Christ's sufficiency, for want of a sense of the wonderful greatness and sufficiency of his love and grace, and so full a reliance upon him, as ought to be. The tendency of faith is to rest and joy; and the perfect exercise of faith, if such there were, would be utterly inconsistent with spiritual darkness. And so a being overborne with melancholy and distress at any outward affliction, is from the imperfection of faith. Faith, when in lively exercise, tends to carry the mind above the storms of the world and out of the reach of them, and to bring down comfort in affliction and light in the midst of darkness.

This objection argues nothing against this kind of happiness that believers have in rest in Christ: for the trouble that they have is not from the defect of the kind of happiness, but 'tis for want of it, because they have so little of it. And this ordinarily is the believer's greatest affliction, that he is no more of a believer and that he has no more of that kind of rest and pleasure that he has tasted; and this surely can be no disparagement to this happiness, that the believer mourns that he has so little of it.

II. [Second]Use is of Exh. to unbelievers, earnestly to seek after faith in Jesus Christ, that you may be one of those that has rest in him. Your own experience can testify how destitute you have been of any true rest or satisfaction hitherto. Therefore don't go on seeking rest where it is not to be found. Hear what Christ says to you in the fifty-fifth [chapter] of Isaiah, first and second verse: "Ho, every one that thirsteth, come ye to the waters, and he that hath no money; come ye, buy, and eat; yea, come, buy wine and milk without money and without price. Wherefore do ye spend money for that which is not bread? and your labour for that which satisfieth not? hearken diligently unto me, and eat ye that which is good, and let your soul delight itself in fatness." You are one that thirsts, though your thirst ben't well directed; yet you are thirsting, as well as the rest of your fellow creatures, for happiness. Christ here invites those that thirst to come to him and promises that he will satisfy 'em.

Here, for Direction:

1. In order to your having rest in Christ, it is needful that you should be heavy laden with sin. 'Tis God's manner, before he brings persons to true rest, to cause that they should be disrested; as the children of Israel, before God brought them to their rest, the land of Canaan, they first wandered in a dreadful wilderness. A being disrested with sin and in a natural state, prepares the heart for its true rest; they hereby learn the need of a resting place and are taught how to prize rest. Before persons are brought to sit under Christ's shadow, 'tis needful that they should first feel the hot and wearisome beams of the sun; this prepares [them] to be the more sensible of how refreshing Christ's shadow is, where they are protected from those sunbeams. A being a while exposed to the storm, prepares them to be brought to him who is an hiding place from the wind and a covert {from the tempest}; by travailing in a dry place, they are prepared to come to him who is as rivers {of water}; by travailing34 in a weary land, they are prepared to come to him who is as the shadow {of a great rock}, Isaiah 32:2.

The wisdom of God appears in first making persons sensible of their guilt and of the miserable state and condition that they are in, before he saves 'em from their miserable state. Persons do become heavy laden with sin by the conviction of God's Spirit; God in a measure opens their eyes to see the circumstances they are in. As I have observed already, 'tis only from blindness that any man can have quietness in a natural state; God doth but in a degree enlighten men, and this is sufficient to disrest 'em. Therefore earnestly seek that you may have those enlightenings and convictions of the Spirit of God.

2. In order to your having rest in Christ, it is needful you should be wholly brought off from resting in other things. You can't have true rest in other things, but you must be brought off from all dependence in anything else; you will be brought off from seeking rest in other things, or expecting it from [them]. You must wholly be brought off from resting in your own righteousness or in your own strength. When persons are disrested by the convictions of the Spirit of God and are heavy laden with sin, they are apt to be seeking rest in many other things; but you must be brought off from all, you must be sensible that you can have rest nowhere else.

III. [Third Use] is of Encouragement to the weary and heavy laden, to come to Christ for rest. You are earnestly longing for rest and are seeking it day and night, your soul is grievously burdened; but be not discouraged, there is rest to be had. It may be true that you have been seeking of it a long while in vain; none of those things that you have hitherto sought rest in have been able to yield it to you; however, you have no cause to be discouraged, for there is rest to be had. There is rest in Jesus Christ; if you will come to him, you will surely find rest in him. Hear how graciously he invites you, Matthew 11:28, "Come unto me, all ye that labour and are heavy laden, and I will give you rest." If you are one of them that are heavy laden, that are longing for rest, you are one that Christ particularly directs that invitation to. And Christ never failed anyone yet; neither will he fail you.35

If you will come to Christ, he will as readily and willingly receive [you] as ever he has any other, as ever he did Moses or David or the apostle Paul, or any saint whatsoever. If you will go to him, he'll receive you into the same union to himself that he did them; he'll bestow upon you the same benefits, you shall be a partaker with them of the very same rest. You shall experience the very same kind of refreshments of soul and shall be as safe from all that may reasonably disturb and disquiet your mind as they.

If you come to Christ, you shall be freed from whatsoever now disrests you. Now you are disrested with the fears of the wrath of God. If you come to him, you shall be safe from the wrath of God: Christ will stand between you and wrath, he will safely succor and defend [you], and your sins that you have committed, how dreadful soever, they shall not bring the wrath of God on you; they shall all be blotted out, you shall never see the deserved fruit of those sins; God will be at peace with you, as much as if you never had committed any sin. Yea, not only so, but God will receive you as a favorite and will be your friend and your Father, and you shall believe the testimonies of his favor and love. And God will reveal himself to you as a God merciful to you, and as your God, and will receive you near to himself for his Son's sake, and you shall dwell everlastingly in his glorious presence.

You are not at rest, because you find in you a thirst after some good that may make you happy and answer the enlarged desires and faculties of your souls. If you come to Christ and choose him for your portion, you shall find it, he'll satisfy every craving of human nature. You shall have honors and riches and pleasures that are true, that are of the right kind, that are substance, and not shadow, and you shall have a fullness of 'em, you shall have an ocean of good in your possession. Be persuaded to taste and see that Christ is gracious. If you once come to him, you will find36 the doctrine you have [been] taught concerning the rest and satisfaction that is to be found in him, to be true; you'll readily give in your testimony that 'tis all true.
