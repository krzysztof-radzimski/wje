Documents Relating to the "Bad Book" Case Bad Book Case Jonathan Edwards

THE BAD BOOK CONTROVERSY

In the early months of 1744, Edwards learned that a number of young people of the parish had been circulating an illustrated book on midwivery amongst themselves, which, as Samuel Hopkins relates, "they improved to promote lascivious and obscene Discourse" amongst themselves. Specifically, young men who circulated the book "made sport" of young women in the vicinity, claiming to know about their anatomy, menstruation, and childbirth. In March, Edwards preached a sermon on Heb. 12:15-16 as a way of introducing the scandal to the church. At a meeting after the service, he laid the situation before the members and obtained permission to investigate the matter. A committee, consisting of some of the most prestigious men in town, was appointed to conduct the inquiry. Then Edwards reportedly read a list of suspects and witnesses that were to appear before the committee to testify. Hopkins states that the zeal with which the congregation approved the investigation turned to resentment against Edwards because of his failure to distinguish between the accused and the innocent; the sons and daughters of prominent families were thereby associated with those of low repute. By the time that the committee was to meet, many objected to the manner in which the case was being managed. "The Town," Hopkins writes, "was suddenly all on a Blaze."

The first document presented here is the infamous list that Edwards purportedly read from the pulpit. It is reproduced with the shorthand marks that he made beside each name. Edward almost surely used these marks to distinguish in his own mind the degree to which the individuals were either involved as suspects or important as witnesses. The names at the top half of the list, as appears by the subsequent testimonies, are those of suspects, while those at the bottom half are witnesses. The suspects are all males, and the witnesses, with the exception of Dr. Hutchinson, females. Between the two groups of names is that of Dr. Samuel Mather, who, along with Hutchinson, was probably called in as a local expert on anatomy or because the book may have been taken from his library.

Edwards was active in collecting evidence. A letter draft to "Eleazar Hannam's wife," Dorothy Danks Hannam, of adjoining Southampton, dated March 26, 1744, is found, with its left margin trimmed, as a page in a sermon on Micah 5:7. In the letter, Edwards requests--practically orders--her to provide testimony to the committee on who had read the "unclean" book.

The next document is the collected statements against one of the main culprits, Oliver Warner. The handwriting and telegraphic composition of the document indicates that Edwards took down the testimonies as they were being given before the committee. Here the origin of the mischief is revealed. Rebeccah Strong, the first witness, states that she saw two young men, Charles Wright and Timothy Root, looking at the book as long before as five years, in 1739. The antics of the young men, all in their early twenties, are enumerated; at a time when many of them are coming to the age of marriage and sexual intimacy (indeed, one of the suspects, Noah Baker, was married in 1743), they use their knowledge to disparage and harass young women. In this series of testimonies, too, the title of the book is revealed. Two young women, Elizabeth Pomeroy and Katherine Wright, identify it as "The Midwife Rightly Instructed," which Thomas H. Johnson has identified as an edition of The Problems of Aristotle, first printed under that title in 1679.

But the nature of the case, and the offenses of the suspects, changed as the proceedings started. Testimonies revealed that Timothy and Simeon Root, the other two principal culprits, had behaved disrespectfully in church when the affair was initially "under consideration" in March. Furthermore, on the day that the committee first sat at the parsonage, "those two Roots," Warner, and other suspects who were expected to wait to be called, voiced their protest at having to wait, played games in the yard, and even went to the tavern for a mug of "flip." Timothy Root especially distinguished himself for his earthy, irreverent remarks, telling his comrades that they were "devilish cowards" for waiting on the committee, and declaring that he would not "worship a wig" and that he did not "care a turd" for any of the members of the committee. In the face of these revelations, the matter turned from one of lasciviousness to one of insolence, and Edwards was determined to maintain the traditional hierarchy of deference.

In June 1744, Timothy and Simeon Root made their confessions before the congregation; Oliver Warner's was probably not far separated in time from theirs. The confessions, written by Edwards, are remarkably brief and general, considering the great deal of evidence compiled against the subjects. In the Roots' there is no mention of, and scarcely an allusion to, the controversy over the midwivery book. Instead, their confessions focus on their sin of disrespect for authority. Warner's confession does account for his "very unclean and lascivious expressions," but even then, he is allowed to state that he did not remember using them.

The superficial nature of the confessions may be a reflection of the growing discontent over Edwards' efforts to assert his authority, which had been brewing at least since the covenant renewal of 1742. The final document in this series cannot categorically be assigned to the "bad book" controversy, but its content strongly suggests that Edwards wrote it in response to criticisms from the church over his proceedings in this discipline case. In addition, the dating of the piece is unknown. It could be located earlier in the controversy, before the confessions of the culprits, and even before the committee meeting. In any case, the first part of the document consists of notes, apparently for a public address, justifying his course of action. In them, Edwards denies the need for a formal complaint for the church to proceed against an individual, especially since the offense is not private. Also, he justifies his barring the suspects from the Lord's Supper because of the scriptural injunction to "put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean." The second part of the document is a short series of questions that Edwards apparently posed to himself as liable to public debate. Which part of the document came first is uncertain; perhaps the short series of questions prompted the notes defending his actions, or perhaps the series of questions was something Edwards contemplated separately.

* * * * *

The manuscripts related to the bad book controversy consist of mixed sizes of paper because they are mostly notes and drafts. The list of suspects and witnesses is a single rectangular scrap, and the letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam is now part of the sermon on Micah 5:7. Edwards trimmed the left side of the letter to conform to the size of the sermon notebook, causing some lacunae. The "Testimonies Against Oliver Warner" and "Papers Concerning Young Men's Reading Midwives Books," consisting of the notes and subsequent drafts, are on eight quarto leaves. Edwards made his initial notes at the actual hearings; he then wrote a fuller draft of the testimonies, and finally a fair copy of the draft. To avoid unnecessary replication of texts, the notes, which contain information not in subsequent drafts, and the final fair copy (with the exception of the first two statements, which are from the previous draft), are included here. The confessions are on irregular sizes of paper. The first, that of Oliver Warner, is on a rectangular piece of coarse, gray paper, like the type Edwards used on occasion to make notebook covers. The second, on a smaller piece of regular paper, is actually two confessions in one. One sentence is bracketed, which Timothy Root read but Simeon Root did not, to allow for the fact that the latter was not a full member of the church. Finally, Edwards' defense of his actions are on three pieces of fan paper, each folded in half, and the list of questions is on a single piece of fan paper.

Transcriptions of the list of suspects and witnesses, the letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam, the notes on Oliver Warner, the fair copy of the testimonies, and Oliver Warner's confession were previously published in Thomas H. Johnson, "Jonathan Edwards and the Young Folks' Bible," New England Quarterly 5 (1932), 41-51.

Letter to Mrs. Eleazar Hannam
Northampton March 26, [1744]
 To Eleazar Hannam's wife:

It has been testified by one before [the] church, that she was once at the [widow] Macklin's house, where were Oliver Warner and Medad Lyman reading in [a book] about womenkind, that which was [very] unclean to read; and that they made sport of what they read before some womenkind; and that Thankful Parsons [said] you were there. This therefore is [to] desire you, as you are a member of the Christian church and would maintain communion of churches, whereby they [are] obliged to assist one another as occasion [arises], to send an exact and full testimony [of all] that you know relating to this matter particularly as near as you can, how [unclean] it was; which of these two young [men] read in the book, whether one or [both] of them; what the book was about, whether it was about womenkind; [whether] it was about women's having children; whether they seemed to make sport [and] diversion of what was read; whether [they] took occasion from it to run upon the [girls]; and the like. In thus giving your testimony, I think you will do what [you] are obliged to do as a Christian.
Jonathan Edwards.

Testimony in Bad Book Case, in BRBL, Sermon Fragments (f. 1157). In JE's hand.

Bathsheba Negro Testifieth that not very far from the Time that Noah baker was married she was at david Burts where were present Noah Baker Timothy Root & Elkanah Burt reading in a Book that they called the Bible in a laughing way what was read out of the Book was about women and about womens having Children & it was exceeding unclean and Bawdy to the highest degree when they read they would laugh exceedingly & catch hold of the Girls & shake 'em Timothy Root read most and he in particular called it the young folks Bible she said that Naomi Warner was there at that Time & heard these things.

"HERE ARE INCLUDED THE TESTIMONIES AGAINST OLIVER WARNER, GIVEN IN IN THE SPRING, IN THE YEAR 1744"

Rebeccah Strong testifieth that near five years ago the next May, Charles Wright and Timothy Root came into the shop, and [she] found 'em with a book that they were provoked about. Moses Sheldon asked whether we had seen such a book, mentioning the name. She had at Dr. Mather's, concluding by the pictures that seemed to her to be these parts of a woman's body.

Timothy Root and Simeon [Root] and Moses Sheldon, often talking in a private way about a book, said something about a bible in a laughing way, that she concluded was that. When talking about the book, they talked about women and girls, and turn about and looked upon me, and said, "You had not need to be scared; we know as much about ye as you, and more too." When he (Moses Sheldon) inquired about the book, he asked me if it was not such an one, and I told him it was. "Well," says he, "that was the book that we had here, that you used to look after, and I have read it out. Uncle Moses said Noah Baker had the book, and kept it between his coat and the lining. Sarah Baker found it, and gave it to her mother. Moses Sheldon and those two Roots and Charles Wright. Timothy Root and Charles Wright chiefly used to talk much about such things as I suppose such books relate to.

Rachel Clap. Oliver Warner, Deacon Parsons' son, Medad Lyman wanted Aristotle, intended to find it. Simeon Root noised that he was sorry so that he had not read more. He told what was pictured out in the book. There the child is, lies all pictured out.

John Miller's wife. Delivered by younger sister. Isaac Parsons promises reformation. Never thought of the harm. He would totally and finally have done with it.

Mindwell. Moses Sheldon: "I know a great deal more than you can think about the works of nature." Not above two months ago. Turned away and laughed.

Martha Clark. Ebenezer Bartlet: "What if I should say I sat 'till midnight reading one of them books." When he was talking about such books. "Don't you think I would sit up again if I had opportunity?"

Bathsheba. At David Burt's, Noah Baker, Timothy Root and Elkanah Burt, reading in a book that they called "the bible" in a laughing way. All read in it. Timothy Root read most. (About the time that Noah Baker was married.) Read it before her [and] Naomi Warner. Laugh. Ready to kiss them, and catch hold of the girls and shook 'em. Timothy Root in particular. Called it "young folks' bible." The book was exceeding unclean to the top of baseness. The book what they read was about women. Samuel Burt's book, they said. It [was] about women's having children.

Had seen the book at Samuel Burt's once before. Timothy Root said at that time at Burt's, after the child is born they burn guts and garbages.

Hannah Clark with her in the street, and Oliver Warner met them. Said, "When will the moon change girls? I believe you can tell. I believe you have circles 'round your eyes. I believe it runs."

Medad Lyman and Oliver Warner knew what the girls was, what nasty creatures they was.

At another time, Oliver Warner and Medad Lyman reading in a base book, nasty book, about womenkind. At Moll Macklin's talk. John Macklin was there; he was worse than others that was there. Thankful Parsons was there. Dorothy Danks was there.

Joanna Clark. Oliver Warner, about a year ago: "When does the moon change girls? Come, I'll look at your [face] and see whether there be a blue circle 'round your eyes." Bathsheba Negro was with her. "I believe it runs."

Ebenezer Pomeroy, last lecture day night, when she groaned, said, "I believe you need the old granny. I have read in a book about that." John Lancton was with him.

Shadrach Bedartha. Oliver Warner asked me if I did not want a book that there had been talk about. I asked him who there be had such a book. He answered, "That is no matter to you." He offered to let me have it; offered it for ten shillings in money. He understood Oliver, and Oliver talked as though he supposed I understood him.

Elizabeth Pomeroy. Found a book in their house up the chimney, on the backside of the chimney on the press. No reason to think it was mother brought it in. The title, "The Midwife Rightly Instructed." Katherine Wright was with her. A new book.

Katherine Wright to the same. See Betty took it out. "Midwife Rightly Instructed," etc.

Experience Strong. A year or year and one half [ago], Ebenezer Bartlet at Jonathan Strong's house: "It might be necessary for young men to know such things." He owned that if he had had such a sense as he once had, he should have had no inclination to read such books.

Isaac Searl. Ephraim Wright talked to him and Oliver Warner too, that told him they would not have him tell more than he need, that they would not have him enlarge much upon it. Ephraim Wright told him he need not say a word, if he had not a mind to.

Mary Downing. Two years ago this summer at her mother's. Oliver Warner there. There was reading amongst them in a book. They made sport of what they read in the book. One or two more there; she could not certainly say who they was. They all did so. What they laughed and made sport of was about girls, things concerning girls that it is unclean to speak of. They seemed to boast as if they knew about girls, knew what belonged to girls as well as girls themselves. She took it that the book was about the same things that they talked of. They seemed by their talk to apprehend that they got what they knew about girls out of that book. They run upon the girls at that time, boasting how much they knew about them. Many other times here, talking after the same manner about what he knew about girls. He has talked to me himself about what he knew.

John Lancton, a fortnight ago last Friday, was at the farm where I was and was talking of such things, and he boasted that he had read Aristotle. He talked about his reading that book more than once; talked about the things that was in that book in a most unclean manner a long time. Betty Danks and Moll Waters there. He spoke of the book as a granny book. When I checked him, he laughed. He talked exceeding uncleanly and lasciviously, so that I never heard any fellow go so far. After he was gone, we--the young women that were there--agreed that we never heard any such talk come out of any man's mouth whatsoever. It seemed to me to be almost as bad as tongue could express.

"PAPERS CONCERNING YOUNG MEN'S READING MIDWIVES' BOOKS, THEIR CONTEMPT OF THE CHURCH, ETC."

Caleb Sheen. Came in just after, he is ready to think. 'Tis I said not all gone. Col. Stoddard. Moses Hannam. . . . Col. Stoddard said, Great affront to the church. Moses see 'em going. Tim Root, according to the best of his knowledge. Perhaps about the middle of the afternoon, he thinks before Medad Lyman went down. Medad Lyman, Timothy Root, Simeon Root, Oliver [Warner]. According to the best of your memory, Medad Lyman said they had drunk a mug of flip there.

Caleb Sheldon. Medad Lyman, Oliver Warner, Tim Root and Simeon Root came from that way, and went that way. When they came back, some of them had been to the tavern.

Joseph Lyman. There was there, he thought, between three and four of [them]. Timothy Root, Simeon Root, Oliver Warner, Medad Lyman. Called for a mug of flip. Said they came without liberty. Had a mug of flip.

Mrs. Lyman. The same persons. Clearly. Called for a mug of flip. About two hours high, as near as can be remembered.

Rachel Clap. One of [them] called for a mug of flip. The same persons. In the day time, sun about two hours high,

Maj. Pumeroy. Timothy Root and Simeon Root. Whisper together diverse times, and laugh in the time when the church, while I was speaking and the matter was in hand.

Rachel Clap says the same thing. Continued in it a pretty while together. She thinks they continued on the most of the time.

Naomi Strong. The biggest part of the time. Turned their backs part of the time, and whispered and laughed. And then sat down, and whispered and laughed.

Sarah [and] Jerusha Edwards. Talking aloud when the doors were shut and windows were shut.

Simeon Root could vindicate himself, but he did not incline.

Col. Stoddard. "What do we here?" In a loud [voice]. "We won't stay here all day long." Seen him and knew his voice.

Capt. Clap heard him say [it] at that time, in a loud and earnest manner. I thought with an air of contempt.

Deac. Pomeroy judged it was the same.

Sarah Edwards. "What are we kept here all for, and hindered from our work?" Said, "I wonder what is afoot?" Timothy Root made answer, "I desire to know what is afoot in the first place." My wife. Timothy Root said that he was asked, when they were called in, they could not exercise themselves agreeable to themselves. Terrified with fine

clothes. And so implicitly owned it.

Moses Sheldon lying down his full length on the bed.

Jerusha [Edwards]. Zadok Lyman, Caleb Sheldon, Oliver Warner, Ebenezer Bartlet, Samuel Barnard, Moses Sheldon, jumping over one anothers' heads.

Shadrach Bedartha. Says Medad Lyman came by the consent of several of them. Said there needed; said the gentlemen here did not allow any such thing. And Col. Stoddard in particular said it was insulting in the church.

He thinks Timothy Root and Simeon [Root] and Oliver Warner in the dooryard when he came out and said this. Was sensible by what Medad said that it would be to the displeasure of the church for them to go away. Moses Sheldon, Samuel Barnard, Oliver Warner dancing in the lot.

Isaac Searl says it was afterward. After Medad had come, Moses Sheldon, Timothy Root and Simeon [Root] put Medad upon coming; they all said he would bear 'em out in it, in coming in. He said that that committee was very much displeased with him for coming in. See Oliver Warner and Timothy Root going to the tavern. He was called out. He heard [them] answering, they had been to the tavern. I see Moses Sheldon, Zadok Lyman, Samuel Barnard.

Caleb Sheldon. Samuel Barnard dancing on the steps. Sung, danced to himself. They soon broke off because some said it was not right. Oliver Warner says the same thing.

Zadok Lyman, Ebenezer Bartlet, Samuel Barnard, Medad Lyman, Oliver Warner and Caleb Sheldon in the dance. Moses Hannam testifies to the same.

Moses Sheldon, Timothy Root, Ephraim Wright, Caleb Sheldon played leap frog. Timothy Root and Moses Sheldon first moved it.

Katherine Wright. Said Moses Sheldon, if I did say anything, he would have her up in the broad alley.

Fair copies of foregoing testimonies

Joanna Clark testifies and says that about a year ago, she being in the street with Bathsheba, Maj. Pumeroy's servant, Oliver Warner met them and said to them, "When will the moon change girls? Come, I'll look on you and see whether there be a blue circle round your eyes. I believe it runs!"

Bathsheba testifies and says that about a year ago, she was with Joanna Clark in the street, and Oliver Warner came to them and said, "When will the moon change girls? I believe you can tell. I believe you have a circle round your eyes. I believe it runs!"

Major Pomroy testified and said that in the time when the affair lately considered in this church concerning the young men's using books written on the business of midwives was first proposed to the church on the sabbath, and the said affair was under consideration, he saw Timothy Root and Simeon Root, diverse times, whispering and laughing together in the open face of the church.

Rachel Clap testifies and says that when the affair lately considered in this church was first proposed to the church, the church being stayed on that occasion, she saw Timothy Root and Simeon Root, whispering and laughing together the bigger part of the time that the church stayed in the meeting house after the congregation was dismissed.

Naomi Strong testifies that at the time forementioned, she saw Timothy Root and Simeon Root whispering and laughing together the biggest part of the time that the church stayed in the meeting house, and had the matter aforesaid under consideration.

Moses Hannam testifies that in the time of the first sitting of the committee of this church, one of the committee, viz. Lieut. Parsons, going out of doors, Simeon Root with some others asked him what the committee were about, and told him that if they did not do something quickly, they would go away, they would not be kept here for nothing; and that when Lieut. Parsons returned into the room where the committee were sitting, he followed him near to the door, and when the door was opened, he stood near the door and spake out in a pretty loud and powerful manner, saying, "What do we here? We won't stay here all day long," or to that purpose.

Col. Stoddard testifies that when the committee of this church, chosen to inquire into the forementioned affair, were first sitting on that business at the house of the pastor of this church, he saw Simeon Root near the door of the room where the committee were sitting, and heard him say in a loud and earnest manner, "What do we hear? We won't stay here all day long."

Capt. Clap, being one of the Committee, testifies that he heard the same thing, and that he spake it in a loud and earnest manner and, as appeared to him, with an air of contempt.

Deac. Pomeroy testified that he heard words to the same purpose, spoken, as he judged, by the same person.

Sarah Edwards, Jr. testifies that she heard the same Simeon Root say, while the committee were sitting, "What are we kept here for all day and hindered from our work? I wonder what is afoot?" And Timothy Root made answer, "Afoot? I desire to know what is afoot in the first place!"

Joseph Lyman and his wife, and Rachel Clap, testified that Timothy Root and Simeon Root came to the tavern that day that the committee of this church sat the first time, and were of a company that called for a mug of flip and drank it. This, as Mrs. Lyman and Rachel Clap said, was, as near as they could judge, when the sun was about two hours high. Joseph Lyman says that the company said they came without liberty.

Caleb Sheldon said that he saw Timothy Root and Simeon Root going that way, and afterwards coming from that way; and that some of them said they had been to the tavern.

Isaac Searl testifies that when Medad Lyman went in to the committee to ask leave to go away and get some refreshment, Timothy Root and Simeon Root put him upon it, and said they would bear him out in it; and that when Medad came back, he said the committee was very much displeased.

Sarah Edwards testified that at the time of the first sitting of the committee of this church, she heard Timothy Root say, "I won't worship a wig"; and on somebody's making answer that some respect was due to some persons, he said again, "I won't worship a wig," repeating, "A wig," over many times, as appeared, with much of an air of disdain. Timothy Root implicitly owned before the pastor and brethren that were with him in the hearing of these things, that he said so, and that therein he had respect to some of the committee, by saying that the reason he said so was that he was afraid that the witnesses, when called in, could not exercise themselves agreeable to themselves, being terrified with the fine clothes of some of the committee, or to that purpose; although he denied that it was with any disdain or contempt.

Caleb Sheldon says that Timothy Root, with another, were the first in moving the young men to play leap frog in the time of the sitting of the committee.

Moses Hannam testifies that while the committee of this church were sitting the first time at the pastor's house, Timothy Root moved to others to go away, and said to 'em, in the presence of a considerable number of persons, "Come, we'll go away; do you think I'll be kept here for nothing?" And speaking of the committee, said, "They are nothing but men molded up of a little dirt. I don't care a turd, or I don't care a fart, for any of them."

John Birge testifies and says as follows. At the time of the first sitting of the committee of this church, I, going along in the street on the backside of Mr. Edwards' lot, was surprized to see a considerable number of young men, that I supposed were called to attend the committee, at play in the lot: I there saw and heard one of the young men, whom I knew to be Timothy Root—as well as one could be known by his appearance and voice at such a distance—and heard him say to others, "I will go away, and if you were not devilish cowards, you would have gone away some time ago." And speaking of the committee or church, as I supposed, he said, "If they have any business with me, they may come to me; I ben't obliged to wait any longer on their arses, as I have done." He said thus, or to this purpose: "As to those foul expressions, I have a full remembrance of them."

[JE's Notes Defending His Actions]

Complaint.

To deny powers to a church to meddle without a complaint is to deny a power of self-preservation common to all societies.

Shall the master of a ship not inquire when he fears the ship is running on rocks.

All societies have this power.

The Scripture directs to making a complaint only in the two instances of personal injury and private offenses.

To deny any power without a complaint is to deny any power of authoritative inquiry. To deny this is to deny the power of self-preservation common to all societies.

Watchmen are set chiefly for preservation of the body, as watchmen in the time of war are for the preservation of a town or land. Ezek. 33.

Mr. Durham is express that no particular formal accusation necessary.

Obj. I. The rule in the eighteenth [chapter] of Matthew.

1. The rule don't require it, because it was not a private offense. 'Tis the way that offenses used to become public, viz. by the fame of them, not by being a great many witnesses.

2. It could not be done privately for,
First, search must have been made to know who they were.
Secondly, witnesses and they must be heard face to face.
Thirdly, they must be dealt with, all of them.
Fourthly, if they would not hear one or two more, taken with each one.
Whether or no such a usage or practice amongst young people in a town is not scandalous.

Whether this [is] a scandalous offense. Mention the scriptures that show it.

Matt. 18:7-8. Rom. 14:13, "But judge this rather, that no man put a stumblingblock or an occasion to fall in his brother's way." V. 15, "But if thy brother be grieved with thy meat, now walkest thou not charitably." [Vv.] 20-21, "It is evil for that man who eateth with offense. It is good neither to eat flesh, nor to drink wine, nor any thing whereby thy brother stumbleth, or is offended, or is made weak."

I Cor. 8:7-13.

'Tis uncovering and viewing women's nakedness....For they do [it] in their imagination. Do it in their hearts.

'Tis a deliberate act.

One thing reckoned gross. Excludes out of the kingdom of heaven. Eph. 5:3-5.

That it is a breach of rule. Hot coals (Prov. 6:27-28).

Job. 31. David by looking on a woman. II Pet. 2:14, "Eyes full of adultery."

Forbidden in the seventh commandment. Prov. 4:23, "Keep thy heart with all diligence."

I Cor. 15:33, "Evil communications corrupt good manners." Prov. 5:8, "Remove thy way far from her, come not nigh the door of her house."

For a person to talk of these things needlessly--young men especially to dwell [on]--would be unclean. Col. 3:8. Put away all filthy communication out of your mouth. And therefore designedly needlessly to hear.

No express rule against castrating.

Give reasons why I think it was not a private offense.

Why I can't give these persons the ordinance of the Lord's Supper. Lev. 10:10, "Put difference between holy and unholy, and between unclean and clean." Jer. 15:19, "If thou take forth the precious from the vile, thou shalt be as my mouth." Ezek 22:26, "They have put no difference between the holy and profane." And [ch.] 44:23.

Whether the church did anything contrary to the rules of the Word of God in determining to search into this matter as they did? Whether therefore, seeing they did so determine to make thorough search, the committee should not go on till they think they have made a thorough search? Whether if it be, I say, that these things were the things that were reported, whether these things were of a scandalous nature? Whether it was to be looked upon as a private offense? Whether I ought to have done anything about it as pastor of the church? Whether I ought to have gone and talked privately with them? [Confession of Timothy and Simeon Root] I do acknowledge that I have been guilty of scandalously contemptuous behavior towards the authority of this church in their late proceeding in the affair, to inquire into which the church lately chose a committee. Herein I am sensible, I have greatly sinned against God, and injured the authority of this church [and violated the obligations I am under as a member of this church, engaged by solemn covenant to be subject to the authority of this church.] For this my sinful and foolish behavior, I now appear publicly to humble myself and take shame to myself, and do ask forgiveness of God and of this church, desiring their prayers that God would forgive me through Christ; and do now declare that 'tis my resolution, by the help of God, for the time to come, to behave myself more humbly, meekly and decently, treating those that are over me in the Lord with due honor and respect, as becomes a Christian. The above is the confession of Timothy Root, and also of Simeon Root, excepting that which is enclosed in brackets thus, [ ]; owned June 1744. [The Confession of Oliver Warner] Whereas two persons, viz. Joanna Clark and Bathsheba, Maj. Pumeroy's servant, have testified that they heard me utter certain very unclean and lascivious expressions, in what I said to them some time the last year--although I do not remember my using those expressions--yet because I might use them or some other unclean expressions like them, and forget it in so long a time, and inasmuch as they do both of them so positively and constantly declare that I did utter those expressions, I do therefore give them credit and suspect myself; and accordingly do now appear publicly to humble myself for that which is so unbecoming of a Christian, and of a very scandalous nature; and ask forgiveness of God and his people, and promise to be more watchful for the future, to avoid all lascivious, vain and light conversation, and in both word and deed to behave myself with that sobriety and purity that becomes the gospel.
