Editor's Introduction

-- 1 --

The Account of the Life of David Brainerd deserves close attention, for it reveals a side of colonial life that is often overlooked. Long confined to the libraries of rare book rooms and missionary societies, it is not now widely read, yet it speaks as directly to the issues of its day as do Edwards' other works. It has been reprinted more often than has any other Edwards work. The text, to be sure, is largely Brainerd's, with editorial comments distributed throughout, but the volume as Edwards conceived it belongs to him. It belongs, moreover, to the period of the Great Awakening, when in the third and fourth decades of the eighteenth century religious enthusiasm swept the colonial frontier.

Because Brainerd had been active during the Awakening, Edwards looked to him for an example of the good rather than the excessive "effects" of experimental religion. Indeed, it has been suggested that Edwards actually exploited Brainerd's life in order to distinguish for the times the genuine "New Birth" from enthusiasm.1 What should be said, however, is that Edwards for the most part let the author speak for himself, and that Brainerd described at length what proved to be an arduous task. More than that, Brainerd questioned at times his qualifications for the missionary life: there are hints throughout the diary that his calling may not have been true, that he had taken upon himself (for the wrong reasons) a job for which he was not properly fit. He carried on, to be sure, out of a strong sense of duty, but his joyless determination to persevere set the tone of the text.

Brainerd firmly believed, nevertheless, in the mission task. Like Edwards, he held that contact with the Indians was a means by which the glory of the church might be spread. Missions, both for Brainerd as author and for Edwards as editor, were a major device by which to bring about the millennium: and when Edwards published his account of Brainerd's life in 1749, these millennial

-- 2 --

expectations were firmly linked to missionary zeal.2 When the heathen are converted, it was held, Indians shall become divines and all nations shall be one. Although Brainerd's personal struggles may be more evident in the diary than his aspirations for the kingdom of God among the Indians, these aspirations were significant—giving Edwards ample reason for editing the text.

For four years, from 1743 to 1747, Brainerd carried out his solitary task: on the western border of Massachusetts, in eastern New York, and throughout the region known as the Forks of the Delaware. In addition, he founded a mission in central New Jersey, where in 1745, he met with his greatest success. But the reader should not expect to find romance. One looks in vain for an heroic frontiersman, a noble sachem, or an inspired woodland scene. The settlers, mainly Scots-Irish, are depicted as slovenly and mean. The Indians are resentful, hounded by the Dutch, and the forest is a "howling wilderness" greatly to be feared. There is nothing here of the forest that James Fenimore Cooper later described—nothing to suggest that the eighteenth-century wilderness might be a breeding ground of heroism and wisdom. Brainerd disliked the wild because he hated the discomforts that living in the woods entailed. When his horse broke a leg, he had immediately to kill it, leaving himself stranded. Indeed, he spent much of his time miles from any settlement, and as skilled as he was in following the woodland path, he lived in fear that when next he lost his way he might not be able to survive. Plagued by disease, riding thousands of miles on horseback, he persevered until the end when, on October 9, 1747, he died in Edwards' house.

He had not at any time led a happy life; he was prone to extreme moods of temperament, the blackest of these brought on by exposure to Indian life. Most frustrating of all, his converts were inclined to die. Indeed, the greatest number of Indian victims of disease were always to be found among those who had been civilized. (Brainerd's converts, of course, were thoroughly exposed to the tuberculosis from which he eventually died.) Finally, although historians over the years have made every effort to shape Brainerd into the foremost missionary of his times, his achievement in the field was slight; he made relatively few converts along the way.

-- 3 --

What should be said, moreover, is that Brainerd's failure in the field has rarely been stressed. Edwards skirted the issue. His Life of Brainerd soon became a spiritual classic and a model for missionary histories. In the words of Thomas H. Johnson, it was "the first biography written in America that achieved wide notice abroad as well as at home."3 Gideon Hawley, another missionary protégé of Edwards, carried in his saddlebag a copy of the Life, to which he referred when unable to stand the strain of another day. "I need, greatly need something more than humane to support me," he wrote in 1753. "I read my Bible and Mr. Brainerd's Life, the only books I brought with me, and from them have a little support."4 John Wesley was so impressed that he published a condensed version (1768). "Let every preacher read carefully over the 'Life of David Brainerd,'" he instructed in the handbook of the Methodist ministry. Although Wesley could be critical of Brainerd for "applauding himself and magnifying his own work," his example was upheld. "Find preachers of David Brainerd's spirit," he wrote in his journal (1767), "and nothing can stand before them."5

Francis Asbury (1745–1816), who in 1771 became the first missionary of Methodism to America, later referred to Brainerd as "that model of meekness, moderation, temptation and labor, and self-denial." Still another Methodist, Thomas Coke (1747–1814), founder of missions throughout the world, wrote of Brainerd on coming to America in 1784: "His humility, his self-denial, his perseverence and his flaming zeal for God, are exemplary indeed."6 Among those who were inspired by the Life of Brainerd actually to become missionaries, the most notable were the Baptist William Carey (1761–1834) and the Anglican Henry Martyn (1781–1812). Carey, one of the first missionaries to India and translator of the Scriptures into numerous languages and dialects, considered the Life to be a sacred text. Martyn, equally famous as a translator, first read the Life while at Cambridge in 1802.

-- 4 --

"I long to be like him," he wrote of Brainerd, and like Brainerd, died of tuberculosis (while serving in India with Carey).7 Others of this era who testified to the importance of Brainerd in their lives were Samuel Marsden, missionary to Australia and New Zealand, Robert Morrison, Scottish missionary to China, Samuel John Mills, American missionary to India, and Christian Frederick Schwartz, German missionary to India.8

Most of these figures flourished at the turn of the century, but Brainerd's influence did not end there. Robert McCheyne, who organized missions for the Church of Scotland, wrote of Brainerd in 1832: "Most wonderful man! What conflicts, what depressions, desertions, strength, advancement, victories, within thy torn bosom! … Tonight more set upon missionary enterprise than ever."9 And David Livingston, famous in Africa, was also inspired by Brainerd,1 as was John Wilson in Western India,2 Andrew Murry in South Africa,3 and Sheldon Jackson in Alaska.4 Indeed, it has been said that "David Brainerd dead" more greatly influenced the missionary cause than did "David Brainerd alive."5 It has also been said that to the twentieth-century mind Brainerd is no longer the saintly example of the ideal missionary but "an example of the exact opposite."6 Yet in 1956 the missionary Jim Elliot, inspired by Brainerd, flew with four companions to the Auca Indians in South America.7 "Confession of pride—suggested by David Brainerd's Diary yesterday—must become an hourly thing with me," Elliot wrote shortly before he died.8 Elliot had admired Brainerd's courage; but in the jungles of Ecuador he faced a tribe far more hostile than any that Brainerd had met—even in the upper reaches of the Susquehanna.9

1. The Place of the Diary in Edwards' Life and Thought

The major works that Edwards published between 1736 and 1746 were efforts to describe the revivals and to define the religious life. His Life of Brainerd should be grouped with these. It stands, in 1749, as a sequel to his earlier revival writings. It is, moreover, one of the most important of these writings, mainly because it concentrates upon the religious faith of one man. In no other work does Edwards confront the spiritual life with such an abundance of concrete evidence so close at hand. In no other work is the reader made so vividly aware of Edwards' personal response to the events of the time. As Perry Miller has said, Edwards interpreted Brainerd "as a rebuke both to enthusiasts and to Arminians."1 The enthusiasts, who assumed that God already loved them and that his love would descend in a moment of direct revelation, could not point to one example of perseverence in religion such as Brainerd displayed. The Arminians, who emphasized moral behavior as the true sign of a man's spiritual state, could not give one example of their doctrine's having brought about such a moral alteration as that which occurred in Brainerd. The voice that spoke from the diary both condemned the excesses of the Awakening and challenged Arminianism, which Edwards now feared was the greater danger to New England spiritual life.

Revival Writings

The diary, above all, allowed Edwards to present "case histories." He had used case histories in A Faithful Narrative of the Surprising Work of God (1736). Later, in The Distinguishing Marks of a Work of the Spirit of God (1741), he had described in religious experience what he considered to be valid, sorting out the false from the true. Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival of Religion in New England (1742) allowed him once again to concentrate on case histories, and in A Treatise Concerning Religious Affections (1746), he went on to describe in detail the nature and theory of the religious life. But in none of these works had he anything like the bulk of raw material that Brainerd had left behind. In none had he Brainerd's example on which to draw, for the diary revealed

-- 6 --

a perfect example of authentic spirituality, namely Brainerd himself. What is more, Brainerd's saga revealed the thoughts of one who was not an "idle spectator" but who had experienced the Awakening at close quarters and had observed the "extraordinary effects and unusual appearances of that day."2

Brainerd, of course, had described in his diary numerous case histories, namely those of his own converts. It would seem, furthermore, that he had judged these cases by Edwards' rules, which he found in The Distinguishing Marks. Although Brainerd does not mention this volume, we find under Further Remains (III) "The distinguishing marks of a true Christian, taken from one of my old manuscripts." He clearly knew the signs of faith—both positive and negative—that Edwards had defined. The first edition of The Distinguishing Marks (1741), the London edition of 1742, or one of the two Scottish editions of that year, had undoubtedly come his way. Moreover, he had listened to the commencement address (September 10, 1741) from which the printed text was taken.

Indeed, the influence of Edwards' works has always to be taken into account. We know that Brainerd read the Affections (Letter No. 10), but he must have read the earlier works as well. Certain notions that appear in the diary could only have come from Edwards. More than that, one senses on Brainerd's part an attempt to parrot his mentor. He conformed, with certain exceptions, to Edwards' rules.

Edwards, in The Distinguishing Marks, had allowed for spiritual effects of "the most extraordinary kind," such as "throwing the body into great agonies and extorting great outcries." "That persons have many impressions on their imaginations," he wrote, "don't prove that they have nothing else."3 And Brainerd, like Edwards, felt frequently the need to show that "ecstasy" and the true work of the Spirit can go together. Brainerd, to be sure, held that sinners responded best to "the tender and melting invitation of the Gospel, when there was not a word of terror spoken to them"; but the results were still chaotic. On one occasion, we are told, many were so affected that "some few could neither go nor stand, but lay flat on the ground, as if pierced at heart, crying

-- 7 --

incessantly for mercy."4 Of a woman who had suffered "agony in seeking after Christ," he wrote: "I had never seen such an appearance of ecstasy and humility meeting in any one person in all my life before."5 In short, ecstasy was not necessarily enthusiasm, which was the main point that Edwards had made in The Distinguishing Marks.

By enthusiasm, needless to say, Brainerd meant antinomianism, which he greatly feared. Although few revivalists had gone so far as to embrace antinomian doctrine, their enthusiasm and zeal had led to similar concepts—to the notion of conversion as a sudden flash: and for this reason Brainerd was careful to distinguish between "ecstasy" and instantaneous conversion. But of greatest interest to Edwards was Brainerd's own conversion. As the Preface to the diary reveals, when Edwards set aside time to examine Brainerd's papers, he was still disturbed by revivalist preaching that had stressed instantaneous conversion, so that Brainerd's account served a useful purpose indeed. Brainerd's conversion, with which the diary begins, was as useful to Edwards as that of Sarah Pierrepont Edwards had been earlier. In Some Thoughts her conversion had served as a model.

In Some Thoughts, however, Edwards had not informed the reader that it was Sarah, his wife, whose firsthand account he had transcribed. She is referred to throughout as "the person"; her identity and sex are never disclosed, for by rendering her anonymous and sexless Edwards could stress the experiences themselves. He could show, as C. C. Goen has observed, that "in none of her experiences were there any impulses, revelations, superstitions, antinomian delusions, or melancholic seizures." Goen has remarked as well that "her testimony became the final case history that Edwards could set before the world to authenticate the revival":6 and in a sense, of course, this remark is true. When Edwards published Some Thoughts, he had no idea that Brainerd's papers would one day fall into his hands. But when they did, he had additional case histories with which to "authenticate the revival" and none that need be disguised. Sarah's case had served Edwards well in 1742, but with Brainerd's text he could do more. He could include in his Life of Brainerd numerous case histories—

-- 8 --

some reported in detail. In addition, because Brainerd had frequently reviewed the process of his own conversion, hoping that repeated experiences of contrition and humiliation would restore his original sense of the Spirit's presence, Edwards had an expanded text with which to work. "I greatly dreaded the loss of convictions, and returning back to a state of carnal security, and to my former insensibility of impending wrath," Brainerd wrote. The most that he could hope for, however, was a "divine temper, whereby the soul exhalts God and treads itself in the dust."7 Like other Puritan saints, both before and after his time, he could never be sure that his spiritual state was secure.

Brainerd, like Sarah Edwards, had known "legal terrors," or contrition and humiliation. No sudden illumination had transformed him. He was then made aware that "faith alone was the condition of salvation," that he could do nothing to earn it: he must be willing to be damned. When finally overcome by grace, "unspeakable glory" opened to his soul, but that glory he carefully defined: "I don't mean any external brightness, for I saw no such thing, nor do I intend any imagination of a body of light … or anything of that nature; but it was a new inward apprehension or view that I had of God."8 It was, moreover, an experience of conversion that directly contravened the antinomian stance.

Of Brainerd's conversion, which resembled Sarah's to a remarkable degree, Edwards wrote that the young man had not been excited by "strong and lively images formed in his imagination."9 Of Sarah's conversion Edwards had said: "Now if such things are enthusiasm, and the fruits of a distempered mind, let my brain be evermore possessed of that happy distemper."1 He had also observed of Sarah that she had "formerly in lower degrees of grace, been subject to unsteadiness, and many ups and downs, in the frame of mind; the mind being under great disadvantages, through a vapory habit of body, and often subject to melancholy, and at times almost overborne with it, it having been so even from early youth."2 There were, in short, striking similarities between the two cases; as spiritual biography the Life of Brainerd carried on where Edwards had earlier left off.

-- 9 --

Edwards, of course, looked to Brainerd's converts as well. In April of 1743, at Kaunaumeek, Brainerd was convinced that the Indians could not be converted. "Feared," he wrote, "that nothing would ever be done for them to any happy effect." "To an eye of reason," he shortly afterwards remarked, "everything that respects the conversion of the heathen is as dark as midnight."3 Although he preached at frequent intervals, it was not until June of 1745 that he was able to record a first genuine success, reporting that numerous persons were brought under "great concern for their souls" and "shed many tears." Throughout the summer he continued to have successes, recording in August that some who listened were caused "to cry out in anguish of soul." But why he suddenly gained these triumphs is not clear, for his method and doctrine remained unchanged. Nothing in the diary suggests that he altered his approach. "I spoke not a word of terror," wrote Brainerd, "but on the contrary, set before them the … all-sufficiency of Christ's merits."4 Unlike some, he did not use "harangues of terror." He did, however, preach the law as well as the Gospel. The "Spirit of bondage," he maintained, must precede the "Spirit of adoption," otherwise the soul cannot come to Christ. Still, his emphasis on Gospel invitation not only contradicted critics of revival preaching but also fitted in with Edwards' reaction against the extremes of Whitefield and other early revival preachers. Like most later revivalists, Brainerd wanted to reach men's hearts, not terrify them. The "anguished" become distressed, he declared, not because they are subject to the "terrors of hell" but because they cannot gain the "happiness of the godly."5

It should be stressed in this regard that Brainerd, unlike Edwards, emphasized the nature of conversion, or the process by which the "godly" achieved their end. Where Edwards assumed that true piety may be determined "without entering into any controversies about the nature of conversion," and that only "the more essential things which belong to it ought to be professed,"6 Brainerd demanded a close relation of "beginnings," or "preparatory work." In keeping with Eliot and other first-generation

-- 10 --

Puritan divines, Brainerd focused upon a process leading toward conversion and possible assurance of election, while Edwards looked upon conversion as one of the many "signs" of election. But Edwards did not make an issue of the matter, though he may have been tempted to do so, for many of Brainerd's charges did bog down in "preparatory work." Conviction of sin at times led only to "anguish" and "distress." The convicted saw no "safety" in preparation, wrote Brainerd, "unless they could do something towards saving themselves."7

Although Brainerd saw the danger of his method, he ordinarily claimed that nothing "boisterous" or "unseemly" happened. Of his preaching he wrote: "It produced no boisterous commotions of the passions, but seemed deeply to affect the heart."8 In one section of his Public Journal he is careful to report "no visionary notions, trances, and imaginations intermixed with those rational convictions of sin."9 Edwards, of course, did not wish to present his protégé as faulty in doctrine or method. The missionary had done his job. He had, moreover, described at first hand the spiritual effects of the time. Had Edwards deliberately sought out an epilogue to his revival writings, he could not have done better. Nor could he have chosen a better prologue to his later works.

Other Publications

During the summer of 1747, as Brainerd lay dying in the Northampton house, Edwards wrote to the Reverend John Erskine, a Church of Scotland minister, that he was thinking of writing "something particularly and largely on the Arminian controversy." A year later, on August 31, 1748, he again wrote to Erskine: "I have for the present, been diverted from the design I hinted to you, of publishing something against some of the Arminian Tenets, by something else that Divine Providence unexpectedly laid in my way, and seemed to render unavoidable, viz. publishing Mr. Brainerd's Life, of which the enclosed papers of proposals gives some account."1 In order to edit the diary, Edwards

-- 11 --

had put aside the treatise that would take the Arminians to task, his Freedom of the Will (1754). But in his next treatise, The Doctrine of Original Sin Defended (1758), he again took the Arminians to task, and this time he appears to have drawn on the diary, for missions—both in America and abroad—are used to give the argument strength.

If natural man is able of his own accord to follow God's will, asked Edwards, how can it be explained that "savages" who have not known saving grace persist in their ways? "What appearances was there, when the Europeans first came hither, of their being recovered, or recovering, in any degree from the grossest ignorance, delusions, and most stupid paganism? And how is it at this day, in those parts of Africa and Asia, into which the light of the Gospel has not penetrated?" Edwards, of course, had found the Mohawks who came to Stockbridge from the interior to be far less manageable than the Housatonics. Heathens who lived where "the light of the Gospel" had not come, he affirmed, showed a "natural stupidity" that condemned them to "worship and trust in idols." "This strong and universally prevalent disposition of mankind to idolatry," Edwards observed, "is a most glaring evidence of the exceeding depravity of human nature." One encounters in the heathen world, he declared, "brutish stupidity," and here he may have drawn on Brainerd's accounts; for Brainerd too had been appalled by the "brutish" nature of the heathen, especially on the Susquehanna. Among contemporary observers, Brainerd had most vividly described "the idolatry of the heathen world" and the transformation of the heathen where "the light of the Gospel" had been spread.2

Edwards, of course, had long been concerned with the missionary's role. In one of a series of sermons written in 1739, he had defined the missionary's task, stressing the importance of the work in America, where Satan had established his rule. Because the American continent had been unknown to Christian nations for many centuries, said Edwards, the devil had those who inhabited this part of the world "secure to himself" and "out of the reach of the light of the Gospel." Here many Indian nations worshipped the devil "from age to age," while the Gospel was confined to the other side of the world. Indeed, Edwards explained, it has been said "that the occasion of the first peopling of America

-- 12 --

was this, that the devil, being alarmed and surprised by the wonderful success of the Gospel which there was the first three hundred years after Christ … and seeing the Gospel spread so fast, and fearing that his heathenish kingdom would be wholly overthrown through the world, led away a people from the other continent into America, that they might be quite out of the reach of the Gospel, that here he might quietly possess them, and reign over them as their god." Whether or not this traditional account is true, Edwards remarked, there can be no doubt that the devil did assert his "dominion" over the Indians for many years. But now, at last, that has changed: "God has sent the Gospel into these parts of the world, and now the Christian church is set up here in New England, and in other parts of America, where before had been nothing but the grossest heathenish darkness."3

Edwards was willing to admit, nevertheless, that there was much to be done. "And however small the propagation of the Gospel among the heathen here in America has been hitherto," he continued, "yet I think we may well look upon the discovery of so great a part of the world as America, and the bringing of the Gospel into it, as one thing by which divine Providence is preparing the way for the future glorious times of the church; when Satan's kingdom shall be overthrown … throughout the whole habitable globe, on every side, and on all its continents." Moreover, when those times come, he maintained, "then doubtless the Gospel, which is already brought over into America, shall have glorious success, and all the inhabitants of this new discovered world, shall become subjects of the kingdom of Christ, as well as all the other ends of the earth."4

These remarks, which anticipate Brainerd's task, Edwards hoped to revise as part of his "great work," A History of the Work of Redemption, but he died before the revisions could be made. The 1739 "rough draft" that Jonathan Edwards, Jr., sent to Scotland for publication in 1774 became the printed text. However, in another posthumously printed work, The Nature of True Virtue, Edwards could incorporate Brainerd's example into the finished draft.

Edwards, in the spring of 1755, had started work on this treatise,

-- 13 --

which was published in 1765, seven years after he died: and though Brainerd is not mentioned in the text, he is clearly there. More than that, the extent to which he provides for Edwards the model of the true saint must be seriously considered; for Edwards in this work identified true sainthood with total commitment to making the condition of mankind as "excellent" or "beautiful" as God had intended. Benevolence toward "some particular persons or private systems," he wrote in True Virtue, is not enough; for "such limited private benevolence, not arising from, nor being subordinate to benevolence to being in general, cannot have the nature of true virtue."5 True spirituality, said Edwards, is not simply the experience of grace, but strenuous exertion directed toward correcting the errors of the world: and in Brainerd he had the example of a man who had strained himself to the point of death.

Edwards, of course, was not so much interested in the young man's mission to the Indians as in his commitment to a holy cause—his willingness to abandon all comfort for the sake of redeeming society. It was Brainerd's benevolence to being in general, not his benevolence to the Indians, that finally mattered; for the mission to the Indians was merely an example of what could be done on a larger scale for all God's people in America. Brainerd's work in this regard prepared the way for promoting God's historical design. If all men imitated Brainerd, they too could help bring about a great and universal benevolence for all mankind. Brainerd had gone into the world and had practiced true virtue at great personal cost. He had suffered for the sake of Christian practice; he had put his faith to the test. By example and deed he had nullified the rationalist, Arminian stance.

It is in this sense, above all, that Brainerd looms as the phantom figure in the text. While the Arminians held that morality is based upon reason and sound judgment, Brainerd considered virtue possible only in the form of acts that flow spontaneously from the indwelling spirit of grace. While the Arminians were inclined to feel that it was not necessary to do anything other than bring reason and judgment to bear on questions of right and wrong, Brainerd held it his duty to fulfill the law of love and to serve his fellow man. This compulsion to serve, this sense of duty,

-- 14 --

is what Edwards so greatly admired in the man. The will gained in faith, Edwards maintained, commands an active life, and he saw Brainerd as a man grasped by faith—the whole man, all his faculties—and willing to trust to the Word and to risk acting upon its commands under all conditions. It is not enough simply to allow that the Word is true, Edwards held; belief in the Word must be followed by meaningful acts. If the act that is willed is not performed, then that act is not really willed.

Brainerd knew that at any time he could associate himself with a settled congregation in a comfortable town, but his sense of duty confined him to the lowest of the social order—to the Indians and poor whites. His mission was to the lowest stratum of society. As much as he longed for a settled community, he seemed to know that the closer one got to comfort, the closer one got to Arminianism, to the rationalism that characterized civilized society: and for this reason Edwards praised his stand. In spite of recurring "melancholy," or extreme states of mental depression, Brainerd personified for Edwards the ethical man. He personified true virtue because he carried out in deeds what he had willed in his heart; his acts of Christian practice were the clearest evidence of his faith.

Communion Controversy

When in 1748 Edwards began to edit Brainerd's diary, he was fully engaged in defending himself against his own parishioners, who for a number of years had taken issue with his stand on communion. Until 1744 Edwards had made no sharp distinction between the converted and the unconverted in administering the Lord's Supper. He had followed the practice of his grandfather, Solomon Stoddard, who had refused to distinguish the regenerate from the unregenerate and had offered "open communion." But Edwards had not been happy with the practice, which departed from that of the founders, and so had demanded a profession of faith from all wishing access to the Supper and full standing in the church. None, however, applied. For four years there were no applicants at all. Then in December of 1748, while Edwards was at work on the Brainerd papers, a candidate applied but refused to offer a profession and was turned down. The following spring a young girl, Mary Hulbert, was about to offer a profession but was frightened out of it, with the result that the church then voted to suspend communion altogether. Toward the

-- 15 --

end of 1749, shortly after the Life of Brainerd had been published, Edwards wrote to Joseph Bellamy: "All that I do and say is watched by the multitude around me with the utmost strictness … my words and actions are represented in dark colors."6 By June of 1750 he had been voted out. The ordeal was at an end.

These events, which surround the editing of the diary, cannot be ignored. Although Edwards never once transcribed a conversation with Brainerd, any connection between the communion controversy and Brainerd's example in the diary ought to be considered; for the principles that Edwards upheld, Brainerd had applied in New Jersey and on the Pennsylvania frontier. Brainerd, as the diary shows, did not immediately baptize and admit to the Supper those whom he converted but waited weeks and even months while he observed the "fruits" of the Spirit. He withheld baptism and communion until he had reason to hope that candidates were "truly religious."7 Indeed, Brainerd was as cautious as Edwards when it came to matters of judgment. "I am far from thinking that every appearance and particular instance of affection that has been among us has been truly genuine, and purely from a divine influence," Brainerd wrote. "I am sensible of the contrary. And I doubt not, but that there has been some corrupt mixtures, some chaff as well as wheat, especially since religious concern became so common and prevalent here" (March 22, 1746).8 For Edwards, of course, the problem was somewhat different. Northampton then endured a "dead" time in religion; the tone had been set by nominal Christians, baptized at birth, who refused to profess their faith for full standing in the church. As opposed to Brainerd, who in 1745 was sifting the wheat from the chaff, Edwards had no harvest at all.

Still, on matters of discipline, Brainerd and Edwards were one. Eliot too had withheld all rites until the "fruits" of the spirit had been observed. But Brainerd, if following Eliot, was also supporting Edwards. More than that, he was transporting the ideology of the Northampton pulpit into the wilderness. Brainerd, it might be argued, had found in Edwards a surrogate father whose notions of discipline so profoundly influenced his own that

-- 16 --

he became—in conscious imitation—a spokesman for Edwards' views. Therefore, Edwards had only to look to the diary for support.

When in 1749 the Life of Brainerd appeared, Edwards published the Humble Inquiry into the Rules of the Word of God Concerning Qualifications Requisite to a Complete Standing and Full Communion in the Visible Christian Church. He also took the position that his congregation should read the book and allow him to expound it from the pulpit. Whether or not he meant to support his case with the diary cannot be known, as the request to expound was denied. However, the Humble Inquiry and the Life of Brainerd were closely linked in Edwards' life.

Stockbridge Mission

Although Edwards was dismissed from the Northampton church in 1750, he remained in the town until October, 1751, when he went to Stockbridge as a missionary—to the same remote place where Brainerd had gone in 1743. Now, with no hope of gaining a popular pulpit—when no settled town close at hand dared to invite him—there can be little doubt that Brainerd's example eased the decision to take up the Stockbridge mission. Indeed, once Edwards had reluctantly moved his family to that distant outpost, he took up the cause of the Indians and defended them against exploitation with a zeal that must have been inspired by Brainerd's work. What is more, throughout the French and Indian War (1755–1763) he stayed on the frontier while others fled, and by his presence and devotion to his job kept the Stockbridge Indians from taking up arms. Although he could not keep the Mohawks at the mission, the Housatonics remained, and he must surely at this time have gained from Brainerd's account the courage and strength that allowed him to retain control. But his health suffered dreadfully. Throughout these mission years, which came to a close in the last year of his life, he endured severe fever and much physical pain. Like Brainerd, he filled up pages of notebooks while the fever raged. Like Brainerd, he was in many ways unfitted for the appointment.

In spite of his devotion to the job, Edwards was by temperament disqualified to minister to "savages" in the wilderness. From John Sergeant, who began the mission, these Indians had heard sermons in their own language, directed to their special concerns;

-- 17 --

from Edwards they heard old sermons composed at Northampton and spoken in a foreign tongue.9 He was unable to master a dialect, either Mohawk, Iroquois, or Housatonic. In keeping with Brainerd's example, he depended upon an interpreter to repeat his words. Moreover, circumstances were against him. When in 1751 he first went to Stockbridge, two years after Sergeant's death, he found a mission that lacked leadership, an Indian population divided against itself, and a settlement of whites (dominated by the Williams family) that worked against him. Unlike Brainerd, who traveled to relatively untouched ground, Edwards took on a mission that had been going for seventeen years. Above all, he had wholly to adjust his pastoral life. At Northampton he had been responsible only to the congregation that he served; now he was responsible to more than one congregation, to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel, and to the Boston commissioners, who held the purse. He was also responsible for a school, with a schoolmaster who did not come directly under his control.1

Because the site of the Stockbridge mission had been neither a trading post nor an Indian town, but was simply a spot between two Housatonic villages, Edwards continuously had to deal with a setting that was artificial. In winter the Indians camped near the meetinghouse and attended the school, but in summer they went back to their farms and traded their produce to the Dutch for rum.2 As a result, Edwards was required to be far more realistic than was Brainerd with respect to religious experience, and much more concerned with drunkeness and crime. He did not make conversion and baptism the main goal of the missionary life, nor did he regard that life as a saintly occupation. Although he suffered from illness, he guarded his health; he did not relish martyrdom in the name of a selfless cause. Nor did he compete with rivals for heathen souls. His conduct as a missionary differed in many ways from the example set in the Life.

Gideon Hawley, who began his career at Stockbridge under Edwards in 1752, has left a record that deserves note.3 In taking up Brainerd's cause he also preserved glimpses of Edwards' life.

-- 18 --

Starting as a schoolmaster for the Iroquois, Hawley went on to set up in 1754 a mission among the Six Nations, near where Brainerd on the Susquehanna had made an unsuccessful attempt eight years before. Because of his knowledge of dialects, Hawley also acted as an interpreter, and on the eve of the French and Indian War he was highly regarded at diplomatic councils. When war broke out, he was obliged to leave his mission and as chaplain joined the expedition to Crown Point. Later he went to Marshpee, on Cape Cod, where he served as missionary from 1758 until he died, in 1807, in his eightieth year.4

Hawley, unlike Brainerd and Edwards, had a single purpose in life, to be a missionary. Like them, he was in many ways unfitted for the job: he wrote uncompromising letters that show a lack of sympathy with Indian character.5 Carrying in his saddlebag a copy of the Life (published the year he was graduated from Yale, 1749), he would have abandoned his work were it not for the self-sacrifice and sainthood he was seeking. He tells of return visits to Stockbridge, of being nursed by Edwards through illness, and of leading a life of sanctity in obvious imitation of the Life. But he was greatly determined where his missionary model was not. Brainerd, who could never gain the colleague he desired, finally had to give the idea up. Hawley, early in his career, wrote, June 13, 1753: "I expect … in due time to have a fellow missionary. I shall insist upon it and make it a term of my continuance in the mission."6 He was also more cheerful about life. "Through the goodness of God I am in health," he wrote, on returning to Stockbridge, June 1, 1754. "I find my friends too. I spent 12 days very agreeably at Mr. Edwards's."7 After a brief trip to Albany, he wrote: "Wednesday July 3rd to Stockbridge, was very much rejoyced to see Mr. Edwards and his family again—where I tarried till Friday near night." Here he even had time for a novel: "Monday July 15th——16th. I was at Mr. Edwards's reading Pamela." Finally, he was always ready to pitch in when his mentor was indisposed: "Lord's Day 25th I preached for Mr. Edwards, he being sick——to the Indians."8

-- 19 --

Of special interest are the contrasts between Hawley's journal and Brainerd's diary as edited by Edwards. (Edwards may have wished that the Brainerd of the unedited diary had been more like the self-acknowledged imitator of the diary.) Hawley could be depressed, but he would quickly recover. Although he complained that he needed "something more than humane" to sustain him, relying upon his Bible and "Mr. Brainerd's Life" for "a little support," he could also report that he was "pleased" with Edwards' sermons and in other respects took genuine delight in the spiritual life.

Spiritual Life

On the matter of depression Edwards was uneasy with the example that Brainerd had set, and with the psychological problems that even the edited diary reveals. He disliked the excessive introspection in which Brainerd engaged. Morbid introversion, he believed, endangered one's state of mind and disrupted the spiritual life. At the age of twenty, in his own diary, he had written: "It is a most evil and pernicious practice, in meditations on afflictions, to sit ruminating on the aggravations of the affliction, and reckoning up the evil, dark circumstances thereof, and dwelling long on the dark side: it doubles and trebles the affliction."9 And in Brainerd's case self-examination clearly had pulled him down, paralyzed him, and hindered his efforts. Yet the heroic quality of Brainerd lay in this very fault, for in spite of the errors of his ways he still had managed to function as a Christian. Therefore, Edwards felt compelled both to blame him and to praise him at the same time. Brainerd is blamed for the melancholy, which slowed down his activity and affected his health, and he is praised for overcoming it as much as he did.

Edwards, of course, did not mean to say that the saint should cease to struggle against the sin that remains within him. The struggle against sin, the doubt of one's state of grace, must be a part of the saintly life. But the problem has to do with what form this concern should take, and for Edwards it should not reach the point of rendering oneself immobile. Because practice is only a sign of faith, not a source, the saint should be able to trust in God's promises when dissatisfied with holy practice; he need not

-- 20 --

fall into despair. Therefore, the issue that Edwards must confront in the diary is whether or not Brainerd can trust in God when he is unable to practice, when he is distressed by "misimprovement of time." Edwards concludes, it is safe to say, that Brainerd did in the end maintain that trust; he did not, in despair, entirely give up hope. He neither moved back to the revivalistic view that the saint should always feel immediately that he is a man of faith, nor did he move out toward Arminian moral conceits, where it is generally assumed that practice, however slight, brings one closer to a state of grace. Because he held to the notion that assurance is a struggle that must be upheld by a hope in the promises and by a trust in God, he gained Edwards' praise, and this in spite of the "clouds" of depression that had plagued his career from the start.

It has earlier been observed that Brainerd in the diary mirrors Edwards' attitudes to the extent that the younger man in his own life—in his exposure to enthusiasm and Arminianism—struggled with powerful forces in conflict at the time. But it should also be recalled that Brainerd, even more than Edwards, was in the midst of things—actually suffering for his views in a way that Edwards did not do until dismissed from his pulpit. What is more, Brainerd spent most of his adult life trying to recover from having made what he considered to be his one grave mistake—that of falling in with the enthusiasts while at Yale. Such an experience Edwards lacked, in that he was always sure of his views and never took a step that he later considered to be a mistake. He devoted his energies to trying to repair the break in the American religious attitude that had caused the uproar around him, and he saw in Brainerd the example of the mended break—the example of a man who had recovered from the disease of enthusiasm to become a saint of sound faith.

It was not, of course, the rationalists alone who needed to be corrected. The rationalists had emphasized morality at the expense of faith, but the enthusiasts had emphasized irresponsible, sterile faith at the expense of Christian practice. Those who during the Great Awakening had taken up the extremist cause and had condemned others for not having known conversion as the enthusiasts understood it, or who had set themselves up as "separatists" more holy than others, were also for Edwards victims of

-- 21 --

bad ethics. He therefore praised Brainerd's piety over theirs because it did not consist in "unaccountable flights and vehement pangs" but was more "like the steady lights of heaven; that are constant principles of light, though sometimes hid with clouds."1 Indeed, after the brief flirtation with enthusiasm Brainerd never again separated faith from practice. As a missionary, he took the precise step of putting his faith into practice. His affections, now rightly ordered, allowed him to reject the excesses of the revivals, to distinguish between true "heart religion" and hysteria.

In this regard Edwards' interest in editing the diary stemmed from a deep concern with showing his contemporaries that he was not defending the passions of the revival at the expense of intellect. There must always be a firm sense of intellect at work, of affections working with understanding, and his gleanings from the diary were intended to support this stand; for Brainerd ordinarily distinguished between the way his converts behaved and the way those overcome by hysteria expressed themselves. Moreover, Brainerd's whole effort—as Edwards presented him—was to move away from the passions that had led to excesses in his own behavior, and to work toward a true knowledge of the affections.

That effort, however, required great strength. Since throughout the years among the Indians Brainerd was plagued by physical illness and by mental depression, he disliked much of his work and was tempted to accept offers of a pastorate in a settled community. Above all, he disliked himself much of the time and once confessed that he was "the meanest, vilest, most helpless, guilty, ignorant, benighted creature living."2 Rarely did he show genuine satisfaction with what he had done, in spite of considerable success toward the end of his career. He frequently questioned the whole enterprise and felt that he was wasting the money of the missionary society that supported him. His loneliness, his sense of failure with the Indians, together with his tendency to pull himself down at every turn, brought on recurring fits of melancholy. He appeared at times to crave death more than he cherished life. Yet when Edwards published the record that Brainerd

-- 22 --

had left, he believed that it set before the reader "a remarkable instance of true and eminent Christian piety in heart and practice … most worthy of imitation."3 In spite of Brainerd's misery, in spite of his faults, Edwards saw in him a virtuous man who exemplified the spiritual life.

Edwards, to be sure, omitted those parts of the diary that he disliked, substituted his own summary of them, and at times changed Brainerd's wording. But the editing did not disguise the melancholy tone of the text, nor did Edwards always try to disguise it. Brainerd, Edwards admits, was "prone to melancholy and dejection of spirit."4 However, the steady and lasting quality of his religious convictions, Edwards believed, should interest the reader. It was Brainerd's experience of religious truths, rather than his personality, that Edwards stressed. Although the story of this missionary's work was dramatic, although the man himself was sick, desperate, and deprived of comforts, Edwards was mainly concerned with the theological example that the diary provides.

Edwards, of course, showed affection for his young friend, giving thanks that in the end Brainerd "should be cast hither to my house, in his last sickness, and should die here";5 but on the whole he presented him not as an individual but as a representative figure. Edwards was concerned with the historical events of Brainerd's life, but he did not want these events to seem significant in themselves. Rather, his use of the diary must be seen as part of a larger effort to answer a basic theological question: "What are the distinguishing qualifications of those that are in favor with God, and entitled to his eternal rewards?"6 This question, said Edwards, had been his main concern ever since he first began the study of divinity. It was a question that Brainerd had also asked, both of himself and of others.

Unlike the diaries of most Puritan divines, Brainerd's was not confined to mere introspection, which for Edwards was its great virtue. Christian practice, in Edwards' scheme, was always to be preferred to that which simply begins and ends in contemplation. Yet the inner life must be there; and Brainerd's diary, although

-- 23 --

it described "external behavior," was also a record of the interior life. It revealed, as Edwards said, "what passed in his own heart." But it served still another use. Brainerd had traveled extensively from place to place. He had been able to observe a great variety of people who were affected by the revivals—"people of many different nations, of different educations, manners, and customs."7 Moreover, he had gone out among these people, Indians and whites alike, and had brought back firsthand information that gave broad factual support to Edwards' stand. But in one respect at least the two could not have differed more. Although Edwards nowhere remarks upon it, Brainerd's response to nature could not have been more different from his own.

Brainerd in this regard was deeply ingrained in the Puritan mold, while Edwards was not. Influenced by the so-called "nature enthusiasts" of the Enlightenment, Edwards gave to nature a mystical quality that is not characteristic of the Puritan. Where Edwards saw mountains and waste places as the setting for divine disclosure, Brainerd saw only a "howling desert." Where Edwards could take spiritual delight "in the sun, moon, and stars; in the clouds, and blue sky; in the grass, flowers, trees,"8 Brainerd never mentioned natural beauty. In contrast to Edwards' joy in summer is Brainerd's fear of winter. But while Brainerd's situation was unusual (having to survive under harsh circumstances), his attitude toward nature was not; for the Puritan, in general, failed to respond to natural beauty. "He did not attack the desire for beauty as such," writes M. M. Knappen of the traditional Puritan divine, "but in his diary he never mentioned an attractive landscape or sunset."9 Nor did Brainerd in his.

On this issue, however, Edwards let Brainerd have his way. The detachment from natural beauty is never criticized; it is simply overlooked. Brainerd had served, after all, as an "instrument" of a great cause. In life he had given his all for "promoting the revival of true religion." In death, through his literary remains, he had become "the instrument of promoting the interest of religion in this world." "He being dead, yet speaketh" (Hebrews 11:14),

-- 24 --

Edwards declared. Indeed, the circumstances of Brainerd's death were fortunate, both for posterity and for his biographer. He might have died "in the wilderness, among the savages," Edwards observed, where the diary would surely have perished. At Boston, in his last illness, he would have suppressed his private papers had his friends not dissuaded him. Moreover, had John Brainerd not brought the diary from New Jersey, without being asked to do so, it might never have been published. And had Edwards not been at Brainerd's side to see his "dying behavior" and hear his "dying prayers," none would have recorded his last days.1 Unlike most biographers, Edwards had distinct advantages: he had known his subject in life, had witnessed his death, and had access to his papers from the start. He could therefore compile a biography that did more than supply the facts. He could include himself as a witness to the spiritual life.2

Because the Life of Brainerd consists of selections from the diary, which in turn are accompanied by Edwards' comments, it does not demand from the reader a trained ability to follow theological subtleties. In addition, the language is confined to narrative prose, although when Edwards discusses Brainerd's sense of perseverance in religion, his style resembles that of the Affections.3 But it was the biographer's intention, for the most part, to allow his subject to speak for himself. Although Edwards took great liberties with Brainerd, altering words and deleting whole passages, the diary provides the bulk of the printed text.

2. Historical Background

Even though the Massachusetts charter had charged the governor and company to win the natives to the Christian cause, John Winthrop feared that "Papists" and Anglicans had met with greater success than had the Puritans.4 Certainly, French Catholics in Canada and Anglicans in the English colonies beyond Massachusetts had brought impressive numbers under their sway. But what the Puritan required of the Indian was far more demanding than

-- 25 --

what the Catholic or Anglican asked, and these requirements remained in Brainerd's time: not mere attendance at services, nor declarations of allegiances, but full church membership. Exposure to Christian habits had always to be followed by conversion, otherwise none could be admitted to communion. Moreover, the conversion experience, which was beyond the ken even of most English immigrants, had also to be based on a sound doctrinal stance.5

At first it had been thought that the Indian should be civilized before he was converted. James Hopkins, in the early period of settlement, had written from England to John Winthrop: "If you can first civil the natives, and then bring some of them to know God … you shall have cause to rejoice."6 But by Brainerd's time the earlier Puritan belief in the use of civilization as a means of conversion had almost vanished. Now conversion took precedence over civilizing. Indians were to be converted not under the influence of civilization, but on the raw frontier, where Brainerd stressed "experimental religion" as his main concern. Another missionary of the time, Charles Beatty, argued that Indians must first be converted before they are civilized, "for they hate civilization and Christianity equally and must be convinced of the misery of their spiritual state before they can realize the misery of their civil state."7

Some American Indians, to be sure, had been willing from the earliest days to listen to the Christian message, but many had not. Certain aspects of the new religion were not appealing, such as kneeling, the lack of dancing, and the English clothes thought proper for a Christian life. Much in Christian doctrine was profoundly strange, if not repellent, to them, for the Indians of North America had never before considered that man was born into a sinful condition and in need of redemption. To them, all nature was good, and man had been given his place in nature's scheme. Mankind, of course, had always to confront ills such as death, disease, and witchcraft, but these had not come through his fault; they were the result of chance or mischief conjured up by some

-- 26 --

primal being. All Indians in North America, furthermore, relied on some person who was supremely fitted to deal with the supernatural—the medicine man, or powwow—and against this formidable figure the missionaries had always to contend (usually in vain) from the first.8 Brainerd, as we shall see, was routed by the powwows on his venture up the Susquehanna in 1745. To the natives of the interior, who were then still untouched by whites, the professionalism of the pulpit and the paid exhorter were distinctly unappealing, unspiritual, and unedifying. Indeed, it was not until the spirit of the red man had been broken and his condition undermined by trade and strong drink that missionaries gained any real hold on him: and even then, in his secret soul, he despised the men who had come to convert him.9

It is not surprising therefore that Brainerd made relatively few converts. Nor is it surprising that he held a view of the red man that had come down from earlier times. John Eliot, the "great apostle" to the Indians, had looked upon his charges as "doleful creatures"—the very ruins of mankind.1

Though Eliot felt obliged to carry on missions, he knew that he had taken on a formidable task, for it was up to him to restore to God a lost Israelite tribe. The Indian, he believed, was a descendant not only of Adam but also of Noah and of Asiatic Tartars who had come to the American continent by way of a land-bridge from Asia.2 Because of endless wanderings, the red man had lost sight of God, but through the Word he could still be restored; he could still be rescued from Satan's embrace. Because Eliot had assumed that the Indian's nature was essentially the same as his own, he wanted to recover for the American native the same spiritual purity which he sought to regain for himself. Others of his time felt the same way.

Little, however, had been done before Eliot's time to encourage missionary efforts. At first it was assumed that the natives would embrace the Gospel; therefore, extensive plans for missionary

-- 27 --

work had not been drawn up. Although Richard Bourne had from 1626 labored at the Cape, and Thomas Mayhew had by 1650 claimed twenty-two converts at Martha's Vineyard, it was not until Eliot began his labors that solid congregations emerged; for Eliot was first to put the Indians into "towns" and to demand strict qualifications for full communion. Between 1651 and 1674 fourteen "praying towns" were established, Natick being chief among them. Still, in the long run, Eliot's achievement was small. Because of the strict admissions procedures in New England, he was not able to gather a church until 1660. What is more, most Indians in the Bay Colony—encouraged by their powwows—remained bitterly hostile to missionary efforts, with the result that few were converted.3 Brainerd found in his own time, of course, that every convert had to face the anger of the powwows, to whom the sachems gave strong support.

The extent of Brainerd's success, then, may be measured against these efforts, which had been carried out by few hands at best. Because the first duty of the Puritan preacher was to his immediate congregation, proselytizing had early been put aside. The number of Indian converts in Massachusetts was higher than elsewhere, owing to the work of Eliot and Mayhew, but in the other colonies little had been done.4 It was not, after all, an easy job. If properly done, it meant teaching converts how to read; in every case it meant exposing them to a complex theology and to a drastic change in life, and for this reason, among others, interest in the work gradually waned. By 1676, with the conclusion of King Philip's war, the English view of the Indian had in any case changed. Because the red man in his fury had wrought havoc on the settlements, his place in the order of redemption quickly fell. The once lost Israelite had confirmed his kinship with the Canaanite, and in the New Jerusalem he was not to be embraced.5 Indeed, King Philip's war had inflicted such a staggering blow on the Indian mission that by the turn of the century missionary work had severely declined: the war had created both indifference to, and hostility toward, the idea of mission.6 Although a new phase

-- 28 --

in evangelical work was about to begin, Solomon Stoddard would later raise the question "whether God is not angry with the country for doing so little toward the conversion of the Indians."7

In 1697, Samuel Sewall had made a vigorous effort to restore the red man to his assigned place. As the new century neared, Sewall had gone back to the early writers and had stressed millennial expectations. Missions, he declared, belonged to the glorious age of the church, and that age was now at hand.8 In challenging the English settlers to renew missions, he had argued that the New Jerusalem could not be realized in America without the conversion of the Indians. The Israelite origins of the natives, he maintained, could still be proved to be true.9

In addition to Sewall, Increase and Cotton Mather had also joined the cry. Increase complained that in the years since Eliot's death "there had been a signal blast of heaven on ye Indian work"; and Cotton in his turn stressed the eschatological role that missions should play.1 But by the turn of the century the natives on the eastern seaboard had all but vanished. What is more, because the colonists feared all aborigines who had been exposed to French Catholic rule, the New England leaders in the first quarter of the new century felt unable to start a mission drive in the west. That situation, however, was soon to change; for the increasing threat of French forces in Canada now brought into being a curious partnership of church, state, and a British society, with the Indians of the Housatonic Valley and the Six Nations, or Iroquois, as its main concern.2

Organized in 1701 and chartered by Queen Anne in 1709, the Society in Scotland for Propagating Christian Knowledge took the initiative in this enterprise, which soon led to the mission work of which Brainerd became a part. There had been throughout the first quarter of the eighteenth century a growing volume of missionary work in Scotland.3 After years of activity in the Highlands,

-- 29 --

where Roman Catholicism and ignorance were the chief foes, the Society in Scotland had in 1730 turned to America, offering sums to Massachusetts for missionary support. With a bequest from Dr. Daniel Williams for the purpose of sending three missionaries to the "infidels," the Society had commissioned Governor Jonathan Belcher and "other gentlemen of character and influence" to form a Boston board of managers and had assigned to them the sum of £56 as the annual income of the Williams bequest.4 To this amount the General Court, in approving the enterprise, had given additional financial support for a period of five years, and each of the three volunteers was sent to a frontier post: Joseph Seccombe to Fort George; Ebenezer Hinsdel to Fort Dummer; and Stephan Parker to Fort Richmond. But because they had gone to military posts, where the Indians came and went, they had not the control that was needed. The Iroquois, moreover, had by then been thoroughly exposed to Jesuits, so that at the end of three years the project had to be stopped. For Brainerd's predecessors, the natives were too much under the influence of the French.5

The next venture of the Society was therefore directed toward the people of Mohegan stock in the Housatonic Valley, and it was to these people that the Boston board, in 1736, sent John Sergeant to found a mission. Sergeant's task, so different from that of the first three appointees, was to establish at Stockbridge an Indian town, and to this mission Brainerd went in 1743 in order to learn the Algonquian tongue.6 But of greatest importance for Brainerd was the New York board, or New Jersey board, as it was also called, which the Society had set up in 1740 with Ebenezer Pemberton as chairman. This Middle Colonies board soon had more to do than the Boston board, mainly because of the Indians' decline in New England. Composed of Presbyterians, it continued to make common cause with New England Congregationalists, so that the missionaries sent out were all New Englanders, including Brainerd, his brother John, and Azariah Horton, who went to Long Island Sound.

-- 30 --

The mission field, however, was not confined to New Englanders. Congregationalists and Presbyterians may have produced the most prominent figures; but other groups were equally dedicated: there were Anglicans and Quakers, along with Moravians and Baptists of several kinds. Brainerd, it is generally agreed, took part in the last phase of missionary activity in the colonies, which was also the most competitive; for rival missions were well under way by the time he got his start.

As early as 1703 an Anglican mission to the Mohawks had been initiated by the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts. Although it was later said that the Anglican missionaries seemed mainly to be concerned with proselytizing among other Christians, Anglicans sent back reports that the rigid doctrines and evangelical excesses of the Dissenters were driving both Indians and whites into the Church of England. Anglicans also claimed baptisms in large numbers, raising hopes at home that the future of the Middle Colonies lay with the National Church.7 But Anglican success was due not so much to the missionaries, who were few in number, as to Sir William Johnson, superintendent of Indian Affairs for New York, and to the war chief Thayendanegea, or Joseph Brandt, who after 1746 led the entire Mohawk Nation into the Church of England. Johnson, who at first had cooperated with the New England mission, came to oppose it, partly because of religious differences, but mainly because he wanted the allegiance of the Indians without imposing a radical change on their culture. In short, he wanted them warlike but on the English side, for in taking command of the New York colonial forces in 1748 he was fully prepared to campaign against the French.8

Of far greater concern for Brainerd, however, was the mission of the Unitas Fratrum, the Moravian Church. Although Anglican missions were effective, the Moravians appear in the diary as Brainerd's principal rivals, having established themselves in the Middle Colonies shortly before he arrived. Indeed, of all the missionary groups they were by far the most passionate in their concern, and for this reason, among others, they challenged the New England cause. They were the first Protestants in America to declare that the conversion of the heathen was the duty of the

-- 31 --

Church as such, apart from colonial policy. They believed, moreover, that they above all others had been chosen to spread the Gospel among the Indians, and to this end taught their children the Mohawk tongue.9

The first Moravian mission had begun in Georgia in 1735 but was abandoned because of popular opposition to the pacifist convictions of the missionaries. In 1740 another venture was made on the New York—Connecticut border but was destroyed by hostile settlers of both colonies. When in 1741 Nicholas Ludwig, Count von Zinzendorf, came to America hoping to unite German Protestants, Moravian prospects improved; for through the evangelist George Whitefield the Count acquired land in the Forks of the Delaware on which the Brethren could settle. Although a plot was made to murder Zinzendorf, he nevertheless prevailed, and in 1742 made several trips in the interest of missions among the Indians:1 to the Minnisinks, the Blue Mountains, the Aquanshicola, and the Upper Schuylkill. Before sailing back to Europe in 1743, just as Brainerd first ventured into the Middle Colonies, the Count had held a parley with the Chiefs of the Six Nations, had organized an Indian congregation at Shekomeko, in New York, and in Pennsylvania had helped to found Moravian congregations at Bethlehem, Nazareth, Hebron, Heidelberg, Lancaster, and York.2

The Moravians' religious views, however, were highly offensive to the strict Protestant mind, and when Brainerd went into Pennsylvania he did so partly to counteract the Moravians' effort. Once there, he discovered to his horror how widely their views differed from his own, and in this regard the Moravians proved formidable rivals indeed.

Unlike Brainerd, for whom salvation was a process of guilt and pain, the Moravians stressed joyful love of the divine. Because they held that Christ had atoned for human sin, they felt free to love God without continuous penitence: they did not embark on a personal struggle for conversion and salvation. As opposed to Brainerd, who demanded convincing relations of the conversion experience, the Moravian missionaries were content with a mere profession of faith. Moreover, where Brainerd strictly catechized

-- 32 --

his charges, the Brethren rejected "the method of teaching them such matters as they can keep in their head, and learn by rote, to say after one."3 Above all, and much to Brainerd's distress, these missionaries greatly stressed the image of Christ on the cross—noting his blood and wounds. But there was little that Brainerd could do, for his rivals had long been determined to triumph in the mission field.

The Moravian Brethren, descendants of a pre-Reformation sect that in 1467 had seceded from the Church of Rome, subscribed to a pietistic theology; because they held that works were essential to the maintenance of grace, they brought a powerful ethical force to the mission field. One formidable Moravian of this time was David Zeisburger, who had immigrated to America in 1738. For over sixty years, from 1745, he lived among red men on the closest possible terms, became a member of an Indian family, and was allowed to move among the Six Nations at will.4 In 1749, two years after Brainerd's death, Parliament recognized the Moravian Unitas Fratrum as an ancient Protestant Episcopal Church, granting it further concessions in the colonies, and of this development Zeisburger took advantage.5

3. Historical Figures

Brainerd's greatest rival, however, had not been Zeisburger but himself. Throughout his mission he was chiefly occupied with thoughts of his pollution and defects in the eyes of infinite purity. He combined anxiety with courage to an extraordinary degree. In no other diary of the time do we have such striking evidence of a man so thoroughly divided against himself. What Brainerd wrote, to be sure, was written in a troubled time; on this point the document is clear. Nevertheless, that it was written by a troubled man must also be taken into account.

Brainerd's emotional experiences, according to one biographer, may have been pathological in origin—especially those which he attributed to a divine agency.6 From the age of seven he had

-- 33 --

been driven to religious duties with unnatural zeal. Much, indeed, might be blamed on his childhood, which had not been emotionally secure. Although his siblings rallied around him, he lacked the love of his father, who before an early death had been remote and severe. When David reached adolescence his mother died, possibly a victim of the same disease that later killed the son. By the time he entered college, and perhaps earlier, he had been infected with that disease, which made him more than ever prone to morbid moods—moods that were deeply rooted in Brainerd's family life.

Family

Brainerd's grandfather, Daniel Brainerd, was an orphan who in about 1649, at the age of eight, had been brought from Essex, England, to Hartford, Connecticut. It is not known why he left home so young, who brought him, or in what vessel he sailed. Until he was twenty-one he lived with a family called Wadsworth, and then in 1662 settled in the town of Haddam, where in time he became the greatest landowner, a commissioner for the General Court, a justice of the peace, and a deacon in the church. His first wife, Hannah Spencer, gave birth over the years to a daughter, Hannah, and seven sons: Daniel, James, Joshua, William, Caleb, Elijah, and Hezekiah. Hezekiah Brainerd, born in 1681, became the father of David and the leading member of the family. According to one descendant, Hezekiah was said to have been a man "of great personal dignity and self-restraint, of rigid notions of parental prerogative and authority, of the strictest puritanical views as to religious ordinances, of unbending integrity as a man and a public officer, and of extreme scrupulousness in his Christian life."7 Having been "early called … by divine grace," wrote another descendant, he "gave himself up to the practice of religion," and "used to keep days of private fasting to promote his spiritual welfare."8 He was a representative in the General Assembly, Speaker of the House in 1721 and 1722, and a member of the Governor's Council, or Senate, from 1723 until he died in office four years later. Before he died the Legislature had given

-- 34 --

him, in gratitude for his service, a farm of three hundred acres in Goshen.

Hezekiah's wife, Dorothy Hobart Mason, had been the widow of Daniel Mason, a grandson of Major John Mason who defeated the Pequot Indians in 1637. Her son, Jeremiah Mason, was two and a half years old when she married Hezekiah Brainerd on October 1, 1707. Her father, Jeremiah Hobart, had been Haddam's first minister. His father, Peter Hobart, was a preacher in England who had settled in Hingham, Massachusetts, and three of Jeremiah's brothers were ministers. Dorothy's maternal grandfather, Samuel Whiting, had been one of the foremost Puritan divines, first at Boston, Lincolnshire, and then at Lynn, Massachusetts, and three of her brothers were ministers. Her maternal grandmother, Elizabeth St. John, was a sister of Oliver St. John, Chief Justice of the Common Pleas under Oliver Cromwell: and so it was that when David Brainerd was born, April 20, 1718, he came into a family with a long ministerial and Puritan tradition on his mother's side.9

David was the sixth child born of his mother's second marriage. The first five children were Hezekiah (born 1708), Dorothy (1710), Nehemiah (1712), Jerusha (1714), and Martha (1716). When David was two years old his brother John was born (1720); Elizabeth was born when he was four (1722), and Israel when he was seven (1725). The house in which the ten children lived had been built by David's grandfather, Daniel, and stood one hundred feet above the west bank of the Connecticut River, between Walkley Hill and Higganum, two miles above Haddam village. It was a well-run house, wherein Scripture reading was daily required. Church attendance was also rigidly enforced, and for this purpose David was taken to the town's new meetinghouse, built in 1721. Shingled, clapboarded, and plastered inside with lime made from oyster shells, this house had pews and galleries, in which David was compelled to sit. Because neighboring Indians had the right to fish and hunt in the countryside, and at times harassed the town, the villagers carried arms on their way to meeting. The congregation, it would seem, was entirely English, but the sight of an Indian must have been a familiar and early one in Brainerd's life. In this provincial place, school was kept at first in a village house; no money for a schoolhouse appears on

-- 35 --

the town records before 1728. Whether or not David attended cannot be determined; he may have been taught by the minister, or at home.1

The Brainerd children, it would appear, formed close bonds among themselves; for their parents, as we have seen, did not live long. David was nine when his father died at the age of forty-six. When the mother died five years later David was only fourteen. As a result, he became obsessed with the brevity of life—a theme that pervades the diary throughout. Indeed, he seems never to have fully recovered from these deaths, which left him feeling deserted. It has been said of Brainerd that from childhood he was "terrified by the thought of death," and that as a child he was "highly emotional, unhealthily introspective, over-conscientious, and subject to periods of dark depression."2 It has also been said that he was "a thoughtful boy, with a tinge of melancholy, which the loss of his parents and the barrenness and narrowness of his education, according to the views of the day, doubtless intensified." His emotional and spiritual anxieties, we are told, may also be attributed to sheer isolation: "The loneliness of the frontier settlement where he was living also had its effect…. His continual self-examination, his highly sensitive and melancholy temperament and his inherited trait of piety combined to produce in him that highly exalted state in which he saw visions of light, and felt the actual presence of the Invisible."3

When David had to some degree recovered from his mother's death, he began a new life. His sister Jerusha, then eighteen, had married Samuel Spencer of East Haddam. She was David's favorite sister and closest confidante at the time. Edwards says that "a particularly dear affection, and much intimacy in spiritual matters" had "long subsisted" between them.4 Indeed, Edwards describes this friendship in much the same way that he later describes the bond that grew between Brainerd and the second Edwards daughter, also named Jerusha, who at seventeen nursed Brainerd in his last days. But whether or not Brainerd was later drawn to Jerusha Edwards because her name and spiritual interests brought back the memory of his sister cannot be known. We

-- 36 --

know only that the young orphan, in the spring of 1732, just before his fifteenth birthday, moved to East Haddam, where he lived for four years with his sister and brother-in-law and their first three children. During these years of adolescent life he describes himself as "something sober, and inclined rather to melancholy than the contrary extreme,"5 a habit of mind that he would carry with him long after he had left his sister's house.

On his nineteenth birthday, in April of 1737, Brainerd inherited a share of his father's land—a farm at Durham, ten miles west of Haddam. That month he moved to the land, spent the summer working it, and soon found out that the farmer's ways were not what he wanted. What he did want, it appears, was to excel as his older brothers had done. Hezekiah had in 1734 succeeded his father as Representative to the General Assembly; Nehemiah, Yale class of 1732, had chosen to become a minister; and David during the winter of 1737–1738 decided that he too should study for the ministry at Yale.

The diary fails to reveal, however, the full reasons for his choice. Did he want mainly to escape from the farm that his father had assumed he would work? Did he choose the ministry for the social rank that it assured? Or did he want simply the liberal education that went with preparing for the clerical life? Ten years later he wondered about the sincerity of his choice: "I became very strict and watchful over my thoughts, words, and actions; and thought I must be sober indeed, because I designed to devote myself to the ministry; and imagined I did dedicate myself to the Lord."6 But whatever the cause of his doubts, it is more than likely that his choice of career had been greatly influenced not only by Nehemiah, who trained him for college, but also—at an earlier time in life—by his mother. As the three younger brothers, David, John, and Israel, all successively entered Yale with the ministry in mind, each must have felt somewhat pressed by the clerical tradition on their mother's side.

Nehemiah, however, had not at first been David's teacher. When David returned from his farm to Haddam in April of 1738, he had intended to prepare for college with Phineas Fiske (1682–1738), the family pastor. But Fiske died six months later, leaving David to study under Nehemiah. (Fiske's daughter Elizabeth was

-- 37 --

Nehemiah's wife.) Most important of all, it was during this time that David found spiritual relief the likes of which he had never known before. After years of struggle and prolonged anxiety of soul he at last felt himself converted. According to the diary for July 12, 1739, he had that day withdrawn from the open into a "dark thick grove" for prayer, and there a sense of "unspeakable glory" filled his soul. As a result, he felt that he had entered a "new world" in which everything appeared with "a different aspect from what it was wont to do."7 He was prepared to leave his family with high hopes for the coming year at Yale.

Evangelists

Still, it was a sad young man who in September of 1739 found himself at Yale. Brainerd, who was twenty-one at the time, was obliged to live amongst other freshmen, most of whom were seventeen and younger. Moreover, he had by custom to endure hazing from upperclassmen, although few were older than he. The drunken and frivolous behavior of his classmates greatly distressed him, and when an epidemic of measles struck the college his misery was compounded: he had briefly to go back to Haddam to get well. The effects of this illness, together with the rigors of his studies, so weakened him that by spring he was exhausted. In August he was told to go home once again, for by now it was clear that he suffered from a grim disease: he had begun to spit blood.

Brainerd went back to Haddam for two months, and on returning to New Haven in November, 1740, found the atmosphere changed. The English evangelist George Whitefield (1714–1770) had preached to the students at the college so effectively that many now were deeply concerned for their souls. In addition, Whitefield had opposed the rigid forms into which religion had fallen, had spoken against the spiritual paralysis that had gradually overcome New England, and had criticized both Yale and Harvard for their lack of piety and zeal. "As for the Universities," Whitefield later wrote, "I believe, it may be said, that their light is become darkness. Darkness that may be felt, and is complained of by the most godly misters."8 Edwards, who was

-- 38 --

not a close friend of Whitefield, but who had allowed him to preach at Northampton just a few days before the visit to New Haven, agreed with this criticism of Yale: and Brainerd, as the diary shows, soon found himself torn between such views and those of the college administration.

When Thomas Clap (1703–1767), Rector of Yale College, made it clear that he opposed the Awakening, a group of students began imitating Whitefield's enthusiasm and evangelical zeal. In spite of college and civil authorities, they formed their own congregation, left the established church, and preached in dissenting pulpits: and though Edwards, by his own stance, had encouraged these enthusiasts, he felt that they had gone too far. Indeed, what interests Edwards about Brainerd's life at Yale is that the young man, who was clearly a leader of this group, later confessed that he had been wrong to do these things and had given up enthusiasm for the warm, steady principles of religion that Edwards himself preached.

Brainerd's passions had been stirred, however, by men who were older than he, and among these were Gilbert Tennent (1703–1764), Ebenezer Pemberton (1705–1777), and James Davenport (1716–1757), all of whom disrupted student life at Yale. Tennent, who in March, 1741, had arrived in New Haven to carry on Whitefield's work, was then thirty-eight years old. Born in County Armagh, Ireland, he was a graduate of the Presbyterian "Log College" at Neshaminy, Pennsylvania, which his father, William Tennent, had founded in 1728. Greatly impressed by the evangelical zeal that the Dutch Reformed pastor Theodore Frelinghuysen had brought to the parishes near New Brunswick, he had also been strongly influenced by Whitefield, who backed his New England tour. But it was Tennent's wild appearance—his voice and manner of preaching—that made a marked impression on all his hearers. Of a sermon this Irish evangelist delivered in New York, November 14, 1739, Whitefield wrote in his journal: "He went to the bottom indeed, and did not daub with untempered Mortar … Hypocrites must either soon be converted or enraged at his preaching. He is a son of thunder, and I find doth not fear the faces of men."9 The effect of his preaching, what is more, was even greater than that of Whitefield. Certain

-- 39 --

Boston ministers declared that in three months' time more than one thousand persons concerned for their souls visited Tennent, who before he left New England had preached in twenty other Massachusetts and Connecticut towns—always with great effect. Within a single week at New Haven he had given more than seventeen sermons from the pulpit of the First Church, then held by Joseph Noyes (1688–1761). Tennent frequently preached in the college hall as well.

We are told by Samuel Hopkins (1721–1803), then a senior, that a band of students had followed Tennent on foot when he went to preach at Milford: and though Brainerd, then a sophomore, may or may not have been among them, he soon became known for visiting fellow students for prayer. Two other undergraduates, Samuel Buell (1716–1798) and David Youngs (1719–1751?), worked with Brainerd in his self-appointed task, the three forming a mission of their own. Hopkins, who at the start resented their calls, finally went one day to an adjoining room, took up Isaac Watt's version of the Psalms, and in tears read aloud the fifty-first, marking Brainerd's first conversion. But it was Tennent to whom credit for Hopkins' conversion was ultimately owed. Although Hopkins later forsook Tennent "to go and live with Mr. Edwards," it was Tennent who had paved the way.1 Moreover, it was Tennent's zeal that had paved the way for Ebenezer Pemberton at Yale.

Pemberton, who was a graduate of Harvard College in 1721, may once have seemed safe enough to be allowed to speak at the college in New Haven; but by 1741 he was known to preach with such conviction and force that crowds followed him in the roads. As pastor of the Presbyterian church in New York, he set an example that reflects the growing momentum of the Awakening. When in 1739 Whitefield had come to New York, Pemberton was the only clergyman to welcome him into his pulpit. Later, at the high point of the New Haven tour in 1741, Pemberton preached in the college hall a sermon that vividly portrayed how far his judgment had been swayed. With little thought for the consequences, he exhorted students to embrace Christ without regard for the things of this earth. Knowledge other than that which could be derived from conviction of conscience, he declared, need not

-- 40 --

be taken seriously. Indeed, the study of divinity itself could act as a hindrance to grace. Taking his text from 1 Corinthians 2:2 ("For I determined not to know anything among you, save Jesus Christ, and him crucified") he gave an interpretation that was literal from the start. Moreover, how far students were willing to go in their approval of Pemberton's views was soon made clear; for only three undergraduates did not contribute to the cost of printing the text. Brainerd ordered six copies, as did Buell and Youngs, and Hopkins ordered eight.2 Pemberton's influence, in short, was profound.

There is no indication in the diary, however, that Brainerd at the time of Pemberton's visit thought of becoming a missionary. In spite of illness and bouts of melancholia, he had by then reached the top of his college class and clearly did not intend to take up a lowly career. But later Pemberton did pave the way for Brainerd to take up the missionary life. It was Pemberton who asked the Society in Scotland to hire Brainerd when as punishment for revival activities he was expelled from Yale. It was Pemberton who preached his ordination sermon (June 12, 1744), and who arranged for parts of the manuscript diary to be published in 1746. Pemberton, one might say, did as much as he possibly could (perhaps by way of compensation) to restore Brainerd's self-confidence and to start him off on a new life.3 But Pemberton was not the most extreme evangelist who in 1741 had shattered the New Haven air. By far the most dangerous was James Davenport, a man who strongly influenced Brainerd and whom many considered not to be entirely sane.

As a member of the Yale class of 1732, Davenport had taken his degree at the age of sixteen and was one of the youngest men ever to be graduated from the college. He was the great grandson of John Davenport, founder of New Haven Colony, and in 1741 was minister at Southold, Long Island. That summer the thirty-three-year-old bachelor had crossed over the waters of the sound to preach in Connecticut, even though the previous May the New Haven County Association of Ministers had passed a resolution against itinerants. No pastor, it was held, could preach

-- 41 --

in any parish "not his own charge" without permission of the settled minister.4 That resolution, of course, merely expressed the wishes of the Association. Connecticut still lacked a law that could keep Davenport out: and the result was disastrous. When he came to New Haven in September, just before commencement, he denounced Joseph Noyes from his own pulpit and advised the congregation to leave him for good.5

It has been said of Davenport that this deranged man "produced a powerful effect upon the mind of David Brainerd … though an effect which was at best of a mixed character."6 Ezra Stiles (then a boy soon to attend Yale) had heard the Long Island evangelist and was taken aback by the "indecent, mad, and blasphemous religion" that Davenport preached.7 Nevertheless, it was not long before the boys in the college increasingly came to resent the sermons they were forced to hear from Noyes in the New Haven church. When one student was called to account for missing Sunday service, he said that he had stayed away "because of the coldness of the air and of the preacher,"8 and the New Light students, who thoroughly disliked Noyes, were happy to have this sentiment so vividly expressed. But the Yale trustees saw that other cases of disrespect were likely to follow, and as a result, adopted regulations for the punishment of students who asserted that tutors, trustees, or Rector Thomas Clap were "unconverted, unexperienced and unskillful guides in matters of religion." During commencement week, 1741, while Davenport raged and fumed, matters came to a head, and on September 9, commencement day, it was voted "that if any student of this College shall directly or indirectly say, that the Rector, either of the Trustees or Tutors are hypocrites, carnal or unconverted men, he shall for the first offence make a public confession in the hall, and for the second offence be expelled."9

In the wake of Davenport's excesses, with the college broken and divided, this rule, it was thought, made sense. It was also thought that Edwards, the commencement speaker of the day, would come down hard on a movement that had gone too far. Such, however, proved not to be the case. Edwards came forth not to criticize the evangelists, as the trustees had expected, but to defend their work. With Brainerd in the assembled crowd, listening to every word, the man who had described with care the stirrings in his own Northampton church in 1735 now rose to insist that the current excesses in themselves are "no argument that the work in general is not the work of the Spirit of God." The horrors of the revival, Edwards declared, are no sign that the Holy Spirit is not at work. Moreover, such excesses as visions, tears, and the condemning of others as unconverted are not incompatible with an authentic work of the Spirit. "And here in particular," said Edwards, "it is no evidence that a work is not the work of God, if many that are subjects of it … are guilty of too great a forwardness to censure others as unconverted."1

Although the excesses of the New Lights were in Edwards' speech condemned (with a warning against spiritual pride), the evangelists received in this commencement address the strongest possible support. Indeed, with Yale reeling under Davenport's lash, with Noyes and others subjected to bitter attack, Edwards seemed now to be hallooing the hounds: and to this extent he set the stage for what was to come. He must share the blame (along with Tennent, Pemberton, and Davenport) for the events that led to Brainerd's expulsion from Yale.

Adversaries

In the following winter (1741–1742) Brainerd broke the rule enacted at commencement and was heard to say that Chauncey Whittelsey (1717–1787), tutor in the college, had "no more grace than a chair." He was also reported to have said that he wondered that Rector Clap "did not drop down dead" for fining the students who had followed Gilbert Tennent to Milford. The second charge Brainerd denied, but because he would not confess the first offence in Hall he was expelled. Edwards, who took a

-- 43 --

distinctly detached view of the affair, thought that it was "injuriously required of him … to make a public confession … for what he said only in private conversation." However, Edwards then judiciously took the blame off others (himself included) with the remark that "Brainerd had the unhappiness to have a tincture of that intemperate indiscreet zeal … to the habitual temper of his mind."2 At no point would Edwards blame the evangelists for his young friend's plight. And though Brainerd wrote the trustees a letter of apology for what he had said, he was not allowed to take the degree with his class. Moreover, the degree was never awarded, although Brainerd Hall now stands in New Haven on Prospect Hill.

What is remarkable about the affair, and the punishment meted out, is that Brainerd (although he confessed when accused) was brought down on indirect evidence, or hearsay. Indeed, it was only by the slightest chance and by a series of unfortunate circumstances that the affair took place at all, for Brainerd had not made his remark as part of a public campaign to stir up excess zeal. Quite to the contrary. On the occasion itself, after Tutor Whittelsey had led the college in prayer and had retired from the chapel (leaving Brainerd with one or two friends in the hall), a freshman overheard Brainerd make the remark, "He has no more grace than a chair." But the freshman could not tell of whom the remark was made. This meddling undergraduate then told a woman, through whose gossip the authorities were alerted, and these authorities, by intimidating Brainerd's friends, drew from them a confession that it was Whittelsey whom Brainerd had so severely judged. Not content with Brainerd's forthright admission of guilt, however, the college administrators then insisted that the self-confessed student should shame himself by a public confession before the whole body of students, and it was at this juncture that Brainerd drew the line: he refused to comply. Later on, when by full confession of the wrong to the authorities, and by the powerful intercession of Edwards and others he again asked to be forgiven, the request was denied, and subsequent efforts to regain admission show how deeply injured he continued to feel for the rest of his life.

In the opinion of Thomas Brainerd, David's collateral descendant,

-- 44 --

the repentant student "ought to have been forgiven at once." Looking back at the event from the mid-nineteenth century and declaring himself at the time to be a strong advocate of "order and subordination among college students," he went on to say that "the authorities of Yale College allowed no candid discernment to discriminate between a courser and a plough-horse,—between a sensitive and high-spirited genius and plodding obstinacy,—and, by their persistence, obliterated the name of David Brainerd forever from their 'Triennial Catalogues.'" Whether Brainerd or the college lost most by this omission, he continued, "I think the world has long since decided."3

There can be no doubt, wrote Thomas Brainerd, that the missionary's life was "shortened by his college persecution." A section of the diary reveals, we are told, "the most intense and overwhelming mental suffering from the stigma fastened on him":4 and that section, which was part of the manuscript diary that Thomas Brainerd then possessed, but which Edwards for reasons of his own had excluded from the printed text, is quoted for July 9, 1743, at New Haven:

I was still occupied with some business depending on certain grandees for performance. Alas! how much men may lord and tyrannize over their fellow countrymen, yet pretend that all their treatment of them is full of lenity and kindness,—that they owe them some special regard,—that they would hardly treat another with so much tenderness, and the like. Like the Holy Court of Inquisition, when they put a poor innocent to the rack, they tell him that what they do is all for the benefit of his soul! Lord, deliver my soul from this temper!5

There were several separate diaries from which Edwards gleaned his material for the 1749 printed text. Two of these manuscript volumes, which together made up one hundred and twenty pages, were in Thomas Brainerd's hands as late as 1865. One was a record of David's religious experiences, which Edwards had copied in large part, and the other included the history of the struggle with Yale. Of this second journal, we are informed,

-- 45 --

Edwards published "not more than a fourth part."6 It had been written at Kaunaumeek from May to November, 1743, and its record of the trip to New Haven between July 2 and July 25 was entirely left out. Therefore, the fragment that Thomas Brainerd has preserved in his Life of John Brainerd is greatly to be valued, for it reveals a sense of outrage and frustration that is not to be found in Edwards' text. Moreover, this fragment from what can only be called the "lost journal" of David Brainerd suggests that a bitter face-to-face confrontation took place between Brainerd and the authorities—making Edwards' job all the more difficult; for under such conditions none could convince Yale to grant Brainerd his degree. We shall never know, however, more than the fragment reveals, for Thomas Brainerd, although he intended to publish the whole manuscript, never did so. "It is justly severe on the college authorities," he wrote of the since-vanished document; "they broke his heart."7

It should be stressed, nevertheless, that in spite of the grief and outrage that the fragment from the lost journal reveals, Chauncey Whittelsey was innocent of Brainerd's accusation, and Brainerd must have been thoroughly ashamed when he realized that this was true. Whittelsey, who tutored in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin, had started his teaching in the college when Brainerd first arrived. Only six months older than his accuser, he was relatively moderate in his opposition to the revival. Moreover, he was much like Brainerd in that he kept a diary in which, at the time he was licensed to preach (September 30, 1740), he expressed the ardent wish that he be made fit for service in the church.8 But the excesses of revivalism offended his sense of decorum—his conviction that reason is superior to experience. He belonged, moreover, to a family that condemned the Awakening in Connecticut, a fact which may account for his moderate tone in prayer.

Whittelsey's father, Samuel, was a friend and cousin-in-law of Charles Chauncy (1705–1787), Boston's greatest opponent of the Awakening: and for these men, as for others of Arminian tendency, religion had always to be tested by reasonable persuasion. "The general rule of the divine conduct as the moral governor

-- 46 --

of the world," Chauncey Whittelsey later wrote, "is to grant more grace, more power and richer advantages to those who have been faithful to their past improvements."9 For Brainerd, needless to say, such a theory had little to do with his own experience of grace—with the sheer miracle of divine intervention; but in rashly declaring that Whittelsey lacked grace, Brainerd had stepped beyond the judgment of charity. What is more, his remarks had been carelessly made about one of the most learned and effective teachers at Yale.

Whittelsey, it should be said, was greatly admired in the college, both by New and Old Lights alike. Although his prayers may not have been flavored with evangelical zeal his range of learning was wide. Indeed, his knowledge of the liberal arts was rivaled only by his knowledge of the sciences, and his ability to impart both humanistic and scientific truths could be equaled by no other teacher in New Haven. Ezra Stiles, who as an undergraduate had attended Whittelsey's classes, later said of this unusual man that he had amassed through laborious reading "a great treasure of wisdom," that his "very happy talent at instruction" allowed him to teach with success "in every branch of knowledge," and that all agreed he should be counted in the end "among the most learned and eminent tutors of Yale College."1

Whittelsey remained a tutor, however, for only six years (1739–1745), after which he took up a business. By 1747, the year that Brainerd died, this teacher turned importer had been asked to manage a lottery by which sums could be raised for new college buildings. What is more, between 1751 and 1756 he was Deputy from New Haven to the Connecticut General Assembly, and in 1758 the First Church on the Green recorded a unanimous vote to ask him to become co-pastor with the aging Reverend Noyes. Accordingly, on March 1, Whittelsey left the secular world and was ordained minister (six months past his fortieth birthday). After the death of Noyes, three years later, Whittelsey continued as sole pastor until July 24, 1787, when he died in his seventieth year.

How, then, may we compare the accuser and the accused? Unlike

-- 47 --

Brainerd, Whittelsey lived a long and prosperous life. It was a life, moreover, in which piety and prosperity seemed always to go hand in hand. Although his first wife died when she was thirty-five, and five of his fourteen children perished in infancy, he managed to persevere with courage, stamina, and charm. Stiles, in a funeral sermon that might readily be compared with Edwards' oration on Brainerd, commended Whittelsey's "order, zeal, and assiduity in the duties of the pastoral office," his "singular talent at accommodating himself with ease to all characters, high and low, rich and poor," and "his … charity to his fellow Christians."2 It was also said of Whittelsey that he was a man of great dignity who joined integrity and philanthropy to an accurate knowledge of men and affairs.3 In short, he stood for all that Thomas Clap had intended his graduates to be.

As opposed to Brainerd, whose health and temper had never been even, and who spent what money he had on education and books for others, Whittelsey enjoyed robust health, an even-tempered mind, and left an estate valued at £3500 (which included a library of more than two hundred volumes). Indeed, his ministry was later described as "prosperous for the age in which he labored."4 At the First Church on the Green (under Whittelsey's pastoral care) one was able to forget that surge of revivalism which had been ushered in by Whitefield, reenforced by Tennent, and compounded by Pemberton and Davenport in their misguided efforts to deal with students at Yale. One was even able to forget that Thomas Clap, once a strong opponent of revivalism, had later changed sides.

In the Whittelsey affair, Clap had wanted to protect Yale; he had felt at first that the evangelists were his enemies. He had further assumed that students who shared their views were also enemies, with the result that his high-handed methods brought trouble not only to Brainerd but to others as well. At the time of Brainerd's expulsion Clap was thirty-eight years old. By 1750, curiously enough, he had changed his stand and had joined those who supported the revivals. However, nothing that he said or did

-- 48 --

in the 1740s intimated that he would one day invite Whitefield to New Haven, a gesture that would mark the end of a stormy career.

That career had begun on February 22, 1726, when in Windham. Connecticut, Clap was installed at the Congregational Church. There he became known as a strict taskmaster of indomitable will: and though the members of his Hock endured him for fourteen years, when he accepted the Yale rector ship in 1739 it was said that "they acted like boys let out of school."5 When Clap was installed as the new Rector on April 2, 1740, the "Collegiate School" (as the college was then called) needed desperately to be given new life, and to this task he gave all his energy and strength. With a teaching staff that included only three or four tutors, he embarked on an ambitious program for Yale. Soon after taking office he proposed a more liberal charter, granted by the Assembly in 1745. In addition, he drew up a new code of laws and made plans to construct buildings of which the college had been in great need for a long time. But he held, at the same time, that "the principal design and institution of this college was to educate persons for the work of the ministry,"6 and to this end had cast off the liberalism of Harvard, where in 1722 he had taken his degree. On the whole, his administration was two-sided and a mixed blessing for Yale. Indeed, his tenure has been described as "one of the most decisively constructive, as well as one of the most troubled, in Yale history."7 Students who did not follow the rules were made to feel his wrath: and Brainerd, it should be stressed, was just one of many who were either fined, expelled, or threatened with the loss of their degrees.

On the student who failed to attend chapel "because of the coldness of the air and of the preacher," Clap had imposed a fine of five shillings. Two years later, in 1744, John and Ebenezer Cleveland were expelled from college for going to a Separate meeting (while at home on vacation) in Canterbury. But on every occasion the Rector did not win the day. Once when some seniors had taken up a subscription for reprinting Locke's Essay on Toleration, Clap demanded a public confession of their sins or the loss of their degrees; but when a student who refused to confess

-- 49 --

found his name missing from the catalogue at commencement, he was able to win his degree by threatening to appeal to the King in Council. At another time, when some students asked that one of their number not be punished so severely as to ruin his health, Clap fined the petitioners severely; in response they turned over his horseblock and privy—forcing him to take a "ride to Hartford for about a fortnight to give them time to cool."8 Brainerd, compared with some others, gave in with relative calm to Clap's rule.

Still, this formidable man remained "the venerable Rector," even to those who endured his fines or struggled against his policies. "The only serious defect in his presidential character," wrote Timothy Dwight, "was that he was prone to consider men as boys."9 There were, however, other defects as well—not least of which was an utter lack of charm. "Had he been possessed of politeness," Ezra Stiles explained, "he would have figured as one of the lights, one of the first literary characters of his age." But Clap was "determined, resolute, firm, immoveable—even to absolute despotism,"1 and these characteristics did not endear him to all. When he died, leaving Yale in a shaky state, those who had known him felt obliged to render a verdict, and Stiles (who had distrusted Clap's conversion to New Lightism) wrote Chauncey Whittelsey the following report:

For a while his hobby horse ran away with him and who is there that is not hobby horsical at times. But he was truly a man of literature and great abilities, and his Presidency from 1740 to 1752 did him honor; in 1757 he was a little befogged in the atmosphere of New Lightism and yet was never a New Light. This was his natural despotism, and high notions of dominion wro't him much mischief and did him not the benefit he expected. Though no great classical scholar, and a man of no politeness, yet he had many excellent endowments … You and I knew him perfectly—he was once a good friend to us. From 1742 to 1752 I found him my best friend; and though upon his political conversion his love waxed cool, yet I still honor the memory of my once Micaenas…. But what shall

-- 50 --

we say of that transmutation he underwent from 1754 to 1757?2

Clap is finally described by Stiles as a man whose "reasoning powers were good and well improved." "As to his person," Stiles continued, "he was not tall, yet being thickset he appeared rather large and bulky…. He was a calm, still, judicious great man."3 He was also, however, a great adversary—a man who without remorse disrupted human lives, and in Brainerd's case he disrupted a life that had never been filled with joy.

Brainerd, of course, had faults of his own which may have hardened Clap against him, for the undergraduate had never embraced Yale. His freshman year, even before the measles epidemic had struck, he confessed that he "longed for death" in spite of his admittance to the college. The scorn that he showed for his classmates, on the one hand, and for his tutor, on the other, showed traits of character not to be entirely condoned. But one suspects that events of his early life had shaped his view of the world long before he got to Yale. He may have felt abandoned—even rejected—and as a result disliked himself and was intolerant of others. One suspects, above all, that his disease of the lungs was well under way long before he first spat blood; that his health was bad when his college days began. This would help to explain those character faults that did not endear him to others, and would also make it easier to understand why he reacted as he did to the emotional upheaval that cut across his first college year.

What cannot be known, however, is the full extent of Brainerd's activities at Yale. Because he destroyed his college diaries before he died, and because Thomas Brainerd lost the journal in which Clap and the others were condemned, little is known about how far Brainerd pushed his enthusiasm at the time. In his published diary he admits to having attended a Separate meeting and records for posterity that after he left college he was "more refined from some imprudencies and indecent heats."4 Beyond that, however, not a fragment of evidence remains on which to build a case. Edwards, as Thomas Brainerd has shown, had access to the since-lost journal, but did not print extracts from it. Moreover,

-- 51 --

if the charges against Brainerd were exaggerated, Edwards did not say so: rather, he defended his friend on the grounds of "youth," stating that the wayward undergraduate (who was considerably older than his classmates) was "not only young in years, but very young in religion and experience."5 Above all, Edwards stressed the extent to which Brainerd abhorred himself for the part he played in the affair: the repentant side of his nature is stressed throughout; for Edwards wanted to tone down the controversy with Yale.

It is not possible fully to explain Brainerd's college behavior; nor is it possible to suggest much more than the diary reveals. But what has been preserved from the diary is considerable, and what the text shows is that in spite of his spiritual views—in spite of his conversion—he could not maintain at college a vision of the world that allowed him to see everything as "new" and "different" from what he had known before. The world of his youth, of his pre-conversion years, seemed always to haunt him. Moreover, the period before he actually took up missionary work was perhaps the darkest in his life. Smarting from the pain of expulsion, with no real activity to keep his mind off it, he wrote: "I thought … I had no power to stand for the cause of God, but was almost 'afraid of the shaking of a leaf.'"6

Friends

Among the friends to whom Brainerd immediately turned was Jedediah Mills (1697–1776), pastor at Ripton—ten miles west of New Haven. In April of 1742, soon after his dismissal from Yale, Brainerd went to live and study with Mills, who for eighteen years had eked out a slender living by preparing young men for college. Ordained in 1724, two years after finishing at Yale, Mills had gained a wide reputation as a teacher; but in 1742 opinion ran against him, and he lost influence with Yale—mainly because of his strong reputation for evangelical zeal.7 Brainerd, nevertheless, with Mills' support, asked a council of ministers at Hartford to intercede for him with Rector Clap and the trustees—although he must have known ahead of time that the council would fail. The events of the time were working against him. Because

-- 52 --

of the increase of lay preachers, a law now provided that "no person who has not been educated at Yale College, or at Harvard College in Cambridge, or some Foreign College or University, shall be allowed the special privileges of the established ministers of the government,"8 and this law must have increased Brainerd's efforts to gain his degree. Another law, passed by the Hartford Assembly in the spring of 1742, outlawed itinerant preaching. No minister could preach in the parish of another without permission, and no consociation of ministers could license candidates for the ministry to preach within the jurisdiction of another. The Assembly, which opposed the revival as a threat to law and order, realized that only through such a law could they restrict the influence of those consociations of ministers that favored revival preachers and sent them as itinerants throughout the country. Therefore, Brainerd, who was first an itinerant preacher before he became a missionary, had either to break the law or remain silent.

That decision he soon made, as he turned to a friend who without hesitation encouraged him to break the law. Joseph Bellamy (1719–1790), minister at Bethlehem, was a year younger than Brainerd. Although a moderate New Light, he encouraged itinerant preaching in spite of the law against it. He not only invited itinerants to preach at Bethlehem, but also influenced Brainerd's decision to preach in the districts that were hostile to New Light ministers. He was a close friend of Edwards and became one of Brainerd's closest friends. (According to one of Edwards' letters to John Erskine, Bellamy is the "particularly dear … friend" to whom Brainerd refers throughout the diary.)9

As a member of the Yale class of 1735, Bellamy had been graduated at the age of sixteen. He had then studied with Edwards for two years and was only eighteen when he accepted the call from Bethlehem in 1740. Throughout the summer of 1742 he and Brainerd shared living quarters and preached in the barn that they used as a meetinghouse. That July Brainerd and Reuben Judd, a college friend, were licensed to preach by the Association of the Eastern District of Fairfield County, a group of New Lights who opposed the established New Haven Consociation and

-- 53 --

who wanted a licensing body of their own. Because Brainerd had been licensed as a probationer, he was not yet allowed to administer baptism or communion, but he could preach as an authorized minister within the Association's district. If he left the district he would have to hide from authorities, and with Bellamy's help he often did.

It is curious, however, that Brainerd should have taken up with Bellamy, who even more than Mills could only harm his cause with Yale. In 1740 Bellamy had angered Thomas Clap by entering at once into the spirit of the revival. Later, when he took up itinerant preaching not only in Connecticut but also in neighboring colonies, he had inspired further wrath. But all who knew him were by no means appalled. Benjamin Trumbull, who followed Bellamy at Yale by fourteen years, has described the Bethlehem minister as "a large well built man of a commanding appearance who had a smooth, strong voice, and could fill the largest house without any unnatural elevation." It was Trumbull's opinion, what is more, that Bellamy had "a truly great mind."1 Others who knew him, however, held a more negative point of view. Payson Williston (Yale class of 1783) wrote in 1855 from personal recollections that Bellamy was prone to "great natural severity of temper." "He was a capital teacher," said Williston, "and yet I have heard of instances in which young men who went to study with him were so unpleasantly impressed by his abrupt and dogmatical manner, that they refused to remain with him a single day."2 Of those who condemned Bellamy outright, Ezra Stiles was the most severe. Stiles lists more than a dozen ministers whom Bellamy had "gutted," "stabbed," or persecuted in one way or another because they were obnoxious to him. "He was of a haughty, domineering temper," wrote Stiles, "and till of late years unmercifully censorious of his brethren in the ministry and others who opposed him."3

Bellamy remained throughout his life a controversial figure, having taken to an extreme that tendency to censure others which had brought Brainerd down at Yale. Like Brainerd, moreover, he had turned to Edwards for consolation and advice: "He was highly carried away with New Lightism in 1741," Stiles observes,

-- 54 --

"but soon recovered himself from the extravagancies of those times by the friendly counsels of President Edwards, to whom he was greatly attached."4 But Brainerd was not like Bellamy. Nor was he the sort of person who should become an itinerant preacher, much less go into the wilderness by himself. As the diary clearly reveals, Brainerd's itinerant preaching, followed by his life as a missionary, must be seen not as a positive step in his career but as a result of the disruption of his career; for he continued to hope that Yale would allow him to finish his course of studies and take his degree. He could not give up the hope of gaining the full credentials needed for a settled ministry. Far more than he was willing to admit, he needed the stability of a settled life and the closeness of friends.

Of those to whom he turned for friendship the most reliable was Jonathan Dickinson (1688–1747), a man who differed from Bellamy on every count. Dickinson, then the leading Presbyterian in New Jersey, had paid a visit to New Haven in February, 1742, and had taken up Brainerd's cause. He may also have discussed with Brainerd the possibility of a missionary career, for Dickinson was by then one of the commissioners for the Society in Scotland, which was now looking for a volunteer. Whether or not Brainerd actually told Dickinson at this time that he would accept such a post cannot be known, but his activities for the following year indicate that he had. From May of 1742 until March of 1743 he traveled more than twelve hundred miles on horseback—preparing himself for the missionary work yet to come: and when he traveled in New Jersey he stayed at Dickinson's house.

Dickinson played a part in Brainerd's life that was equal to, if not greater than, the role of Edwards himself. Had Brainerd toward the end not sought out Jerusha Edwards in Northampton, he might well have remained at Elizabethtown and died in Dickinson's house. No other man, apart from Edwards, took such an interest in Brainerd's plight. None was so vividly aware of how perfectly he served the New Side Presbyterian cause. He personified, on the one hand, what the Tennents' Log College (which Dickinson now disliked) had failed to produce, and, on the other hand, what Dickinson's own college, Yale, had refused to tolerate. In short, he stood for what a new college in the middle colonies

-- 55 --

might strive to produce, a sound reason for founding the College of New Jersey (which became the goal of Dickinson's life).

Toward such a goal, however, Dickinson had long been heading, before the need for a college had been stressed by Brainerd's plight. From 1709, when he was ordained pastor of the Elizabethtown church, he had felt uneasy with its strict Congregational organization. By 1717 he had been able to persuade the congregation to join the presbytery of Philadelphia, and as moderator of the Synod of 1721 he had insured that the liberal voice prevail. In addition, he at first gave the Tennents' Log College his full support: when the "Old Side" Presbyterians virtually blocked from ordination graduates of that college, he left the Philadelphia body and organized the Synod of New York. He also convinced many "New Side" brethren of the need for an institution that could train men for their ministry, and he was determined that the new college take a liberal Presbyterian stand.5 Therefore, when the College of New Jersey was chartered in October of 1746, Dickinson was chosen president, and when the door was opened at the end of May, 1747, classes were held in his house. Because Brainerd was in residence that year, he is considered to be the first student enrolled.

According to David Dudley Field, Princeton College was founded because of Brainerd's expulsion from Yale. In 1857 Field gave the following firsthand account to support his claim: "I once heard the Hon. John Dickinson … son of the Rev. Mr. Dickinson, of Norwalk, say, that the establishment of Princeton College was owing to the sympathy felt for David Brainerd, because the authorities of Yale College would not give him his degree, and that the plan of the college was drawn up in his father's house."6 Archibald Alexander also makes much of Brainerd, while at the same time giving Aaron Burr the position of co-founder. "Messrs. Dickinson and Burr," he maintains, "took the lead in this enterprise." He then goes on to say: "Both these distinguished divines were graduates of Yale College; but just at this time their minds probably experienced some alienation from their alma mater on account of the harsh treatment which Mr. David Brainerd had received from the officers of that college." Alexander,

-- 56 --

in some closing remarks, concludes that "the attachment of all the members of the New York Synod to Mr. Brainerd was warm, and deservedly so," and that the expulsion had probably "quickened the zeal of these excellent men to get up a college of their own."7

Alexander, like Field, also offered a firsthand account: "Some years ago," he declared, he had heard "the relict parenthetical of the late Dr. Scott, of New Brunswick, say that when she was a little girl she heard the Rev. Mr. Burr declare in her father's house in Newark, 'if it had not been for the treatment received by Mr. Brainerd at Yale, New Jersey College would never have been erected.'"8 It was, however, Thomas Brainerd who in summing up the evidence made the final claim: "This testimony of the Rev. Dr. Alexander so corroborates the statements of the Rev. Dr. Field," he wrote, "that we may regard the question as settled, that the expulsion of David Brainerd from Yale led to the founding of Princeton College."9 The Brainerd tradition—well into the nineteenth century—remained strong.

Brainerd, of course, was not the sole reason that the college was founded, but there can be little doubt that Dickinson and Burr used his case to their advantage. (When Clap had complained that the New York Synod was receiving under its care members of Yale College who had left under censure, he clearly meant Brainerd.) Burr, like Dickinson, had also pled for Brainerd at Yale, having gone in 1743 to the commencement at which Brainerd's class was graduated, hoping to have him restored.1

Only two years older than Brainerd, Aaron Burr had taken his degree at nineteen, had been granted a graduate scholarship, and had gone on from Yale to a prosperous church. By 1742 he was widely known, having been installed in the Presbyterian Church of Newark in 1738. After Dickinson died in 1747 he was chosen president of the College of New Jersey, and with that institution under his command had, in 1752, married Edwards' third daughter, Esther, younger sister of Jerusha. Burr was thirty-six and Esther was twenty-one. Neither was immune to the numerous

-- 57 --

diseases of the time. Esther, at twenty-six, died of a smallpox vaccination. Burr, only a year after he had moved the College from Newark to Princeton, died at forty-one "of a nervous fever," leaving two orphaned children behind.2 One, his name-sake, later gained notorious fame.

Among those who had looked to Burr for advice was David's brother John Brainerd, a frequent visitor to the Burr house. Although Burr himself was not inclined toward missionary work, he had inspired both David and John to persevere in that task, and both had looked upon this man as a close friend. A question that must be asked, however, is whether or not Burr should have given these brothers the same advice. Although John faced mission work with some calm, David clearly did not. As the diary reveals, his ambitions as a scholar had now to give way to the role he took on as an obscure missionary, and in this role he loathed having to "give up all for gone."3 But what biographers have not sufficiently stressed is that he need not have gone at all; for he had already shown—before his mission work began—that he was greatly in demand at home.

Associates

When in January of 1743 Brainerd stopped at Stonington, Connecticut, he faced his first major challenge after leaving Yale. Joseph Fish (1705–1781), Harvard class of 1728, had for a decade been secure in the North Stonington parish. Then in 1741 Davenport had invaded the town and had turned Fish's congregation against him. Although Fish had been active in the revivals, he was a preacher of scholarly bent. His sermons, it was said, "smelt of the lamp": and he came out of the clash with Davenport despairing of his own salvation. (In December of 1742 he had told his parishioners that he could no longer preach.)4 But immediately Brainerd arrived and preached to the rebellious people, the matter was turned round. Fish soon resumed his pulpit and went on to serve for forty more years. Although faced with sporadic discord now and then, he remained until the end—running for a time an Indian school in the town—and he owed

-- 58 --

his survival to Brainerd, whose triumph over Davenport's work had saved the day.

This incident at Stonington is given limited space in the diary, but it is important, for it shows how strong in his own world Brainerd could be. What is more, it shows a side of Brainerd that is greatly to be admired: his willingness to lend a hand to an associate in need of help. Coming so soon after the expulsion from Yale, the episode also shows that Brainerd could stand up when challenged—that he had in his own society the strength to endure.

When he went from Stonington to East Hampton, where in the winter of 1742–1743 he spent six weeks, he was again faced with the need to bring order out of strife, and again he showed that in spite of his commitment to the missionary life he was wanted, needed, and effective on his native ground. Recently disgraced in New Haven, Brainerd was now embraced by East Hampton, at the time a whaling village and one of the largest parishes on Long Island. This town too had been disrupted by Davenport; it needed a new preacher as well as calm advice. Moreover, the church, although originally established along Congregational lines in fellowship with the churches of Connecticut, was moving toward the Presbyterian pattern established in New York; so that Brainerd, as a Congregationalist with Presbyterian leanings, was regarded as the right man to manage the shift. During the time that he spent there, however, he did not encourage the town to offer him a livelihood; by then he was committed to the commissioners and to his coming work in the mission field. But this commitment, it should be said, had by now become a serious worry in his life.

Brainerd, of course, had been exposed to Indian ways as a boy. He had observed at a distance lowly native life along Connecticut's shores. But now at East Hampton he made a visit to Montauk Point, sixteen miles away on the east side of the island, where Azariah Horton presided over two towns containing one hundred and sixty-two souls, and it was here that he had to confront fully the "flatness" and "deadness" of his own response to Indian life.

When he first set out to see Horton's flock, he felt "some inward sweetness" on the road. That feeling, however, soon gave way to dread, and before he had left these people he was so undone by the "blackness" of his nature that he thought himself unfit

-- 59 --

for the task ahead.5 Never before had he seen at close hand such squalid conditions; never before had he fully understood what life amongst such people might be like. He had, moreover, in Horton an associate whom he observed in dire apprehension for his own success.

Azariah Horton (1715–1777) had like Brainerd been ordained by the New York Presbytery and was supported by the Society in Scotland. Of the class of 1735 at Yale, he had since 1741 been laboring among a struggling group of Long Island Indians who barely survived along the southern shore. But the enterprise was too vast and could not be managed. In addition to the two towns, Horton was responsible for scattered groups of Nantics, Mahicans, Pequots, and Naragansetts who lived along one hundred miles of sandy coast, and with these his ministry was especially weak. Indeed, of the four hundred souls under his complete domain, he baptized only forty-four children and thirty-five adults, and of these only twenty could be said to have given sound evidence of saving faith. Many fell prey to whiskey and rum, so that Horton, with little to show for his efforts, eventually had to give up.6

Brainerd sensed that Horton's efforts were futile. Although the diary does not say so, the decision in March of 1743 to leave East Hampton (where he had been "frequently invited" by whites to settle) must have been the most difficult in his life.7 Certainly it sealed his commitment to the commissioners; it also cut him off from a lucrative post. In the years that followed he would yearn for a settled community; but by frequent contact with friends and associates, he was able to endure.

Among those who now entered his life was John Sergeant (1710–1749), missionary to the Housatonic Indians at Stock-bridge. Sergeant looms large in the diary not only because he trained Brainerd for his task but also because the Stockbridge mission set a standard for all others. Sergeant's Housatonics, unlike Horton's scattered tribes, were all housed in one town. Because Sergeant had only one tongue to master, he was able to control his people as Horton could not.

After taking his Yale degree in 1729, Sergeant was from 1731

-- 60 --

to 1735 tutor at the college, where he also studied theology. But he was not bound for a settled church. In answer to an appeal from Samuel Hopkins and Stephen Williams, ministers at Springfield, and from Colonel John Stoddard of Northampton, the commissioners of Indian affairs had asked Sergeant to set up a mission post in the western colonial parts. These commissioners, who were also agents for the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in New England, had heard it said of Sergeant that "he had rather be employed as a missionary to the natives, if a door should open for it, than accept a call any English parish might give him": and so in September, 1734, he was offered the mission. Later he wrote that he had taken the job, among other reasons, because the "pains taken by those of the Romish Church, not only in other parts but also in America … should methinks excite us to emulation, who at least think we profess Christianity in much greater purity."8

These commissioners had also determined that the Upper Valley of the Housatonic River was ripe for mission work—mainly because of the concentration there of Mahicans, who had moved eastward when the Dutch had inhabited the west bank of the Hudson. These natives, of Algonquian heritage, called themselves Mahiccondas, or river people, and later were called Housatonics by the English, who found them to be friendly, few in number, and easy to manage. Because their chief, Konapot, was receptive to Christian doctrine, Sergeant with relative ease could set up a mission.

He had first gone to the natives at Great Barrington, using one Ebenezer Paupaumunk as an interpreter. Between October and November, 1734, he had then got the Stockbridge Indians to erect a building, which soon served as both a church and a school. By the time of his ordination he was well prepared to manage the mission for which the Society in Scotland also provided support.9

On August 31, 1735, when he was twenty-five, Sergeant was ordained at Deerfield in the presence of the governor of the colony and a large group of Indians and ministers. Not since the time of John Eliot, whose pulpit at Roxbury had served as the first mission church in 1645, had so much concern for Indian affairs been shown. The General Court, the following year, then

-- 61 --

set up a town at Wnahktutook, where six square miles and the full rights of Englishmen were granted to the native inhabitants. When four white families were chosen to settle with these Indians, the town was incorporated as Stockbridge (1739), and the original natives were later to be known as Stockbridge Indians. It was with these people that Sergeant soon built his reputation as a missionary: amongst them he learnt the Algonquian tongue.1

On the western frontier Sergeant did not gain great success (in spite of frequent long trips to the Susquehanna River in New York). Only at Stockbridge did his mission work thrive. It was there in 1739 that he married Abigail Williams, daughter of Ephraim Williams, a distant cousin of Edwards. There he built a house (which still stands) well-removed from the Indian town. Sergeant combined missionary zeal with the comforts of Berkshire country life. He was, at the same time, the only man to whom Brainerd could then turn. No other person had by 1743 known nine years of missionary life. None had given that much time to the "Moheekanneeus" dialect, or had translated into it passages from Scripture, prayers, and Isaac Watt's Catechism for Children.2

As early as 1737 Sergeant had baptized the sachem Yokun and fifty of his followers, and so had paved the way. (When Brainerd later got to Kaunaumeek, his first mission station twenty miles to the west of Stockbridge, he found that "prejudices" against Christianity had already been removed by Sergeant's work.) It was, however, with little hope that Brainerd on the last day of March, 1743, rode into Stockbridge, intending to study the Indian language and to assist Sergeant in his task. Just a year had gone by since he had started the third book of his diary, while living at Ripton with Jedediah Mills. Since then he had visited close to forty towns and had delivered countless sermons; his skill in itinerancy was now well established. But his spirits were not high; he still looked upon himself as a probationer, so that without Sergeant he might have faltered at the start. It was Sergeant who inspired him to found his Indian School at Kaunaumeek, where John Wauwaupeguunnaunt—a Stockbridge Christian—served as master. Through this Indian, who also acted as an interpreter, Brainerd learned to pray with his people in their language and to translate psalms into their tongue.

Later, in other regions, Brainerd's knowledge of Algonquian did him little good. It could not be used to win over the Iroquois on the Susquehanna. But there, although he keenly felt his failure, his efforts inspired others. Eleazer Wheelock (1711–1779), to whom Brainerd sent a copy of his diary (see Related Correspondence), was one of these. Wheelock's determination to propagate the faith among the Iroquois was greater than that of any other figure of the time. Inspired by Brainerd's example, he founded in 1748 a boarding school for Iroquois and whites at Lebanon, Connecticut, where in time he accepted two Delawares from New Jersey (sent by John Brainerd).3 Later the school was moved to Hanover, New Hampshire, where in 1770 Wheelock founded Dartmouth College as well.4 But his effort on behalf of the Indians came to very little. The Indian school declined as Dartmouth College grew. Wheelock soon discovered, as had Brainerd, that the native scholars were likely to die of disease. Such had been the case in the Indian College at Harvard the century before, where the only graduate died within a year after taking his degree. Still, the desire to educate Indians had been strong from the start; of the funds spent on missionary activities in the colonial period, a high percentage went to education. Brainerd, as the diary shows, spent as much time educating his people as he did in catechizing them, and the motive was not exclusively religious. New Englanders had commonly held that the education of the Indian would improve his character, as with whites, and that religion in the process would benefit as well.5 It was hoped that educated Indians would go out as teachers and ministers to other Indians; but it was also hoped that the educated red man would improve society as a whole, and to this end Wheelock had founded his school. To this end he had followed Brainerd's lead.

For the founding of Dickinson's Princeton, Brainerd had provided the perfect model of scholarship combined with evangelical zeal. For the founding of Wheelock's Dartmouth, his example continued to inspire an educational effort. Yale's outcast had not,

-- 63 --

of course, set out to be concerned with the founding of sister institutions. As the diary shows, Brainerd had resented institutional life from the start of his freshman year. More than that, he had suffered from the imposition of institutional rules and regulations. But he was, above all, a scholar—a scholar by profession and a missionary by default: and he was vividly aware of the colonists' need for learning. In this regard, his example as an educator has had far more lasting effects than his effort to make Christians out of natives. Both Dickinson and Wheelock owed him a great deal.

Confidants

In Brainerd's personal life, however, Wheelock seems not to have played a major part, although he did on occasion give the younger man advice and concerned himself closely with Brainerd's career.6 In the personal sphere it was John Brainerd, more than anyone else, who prevailed. To no other person did David in writing reveal his thoughts so openly, as the letters to John at college clearly show. In the last days of his life David is reported to have told Jerusha Edwards that he was willing to part with her, with his friends, and even with his brother John, although he loved him "the best of any creature living."7 It was John who brought David's diaries to Northampton just before the end. After David's death it was John who carried on the work of the mission known as Bethel, at Cranbury (old form, Cranberry), New Jersey.

Although John persevered in this job, he seems also to have shared the doubts and struggles of David. Indeed, their common melancholia may have formed a bond between them. Like David, John alludes in his diaries to ill health: and he appears to have been subject—like his brother—to severe mental depression. Of an entry in John's diary for August 27, 1749, where it is written, "felt very poorly, not able to sit up," Thomas Brainerd has made the following observations: "These frequent allusions to ill health are remarkable and painful in a young man of twenty-eight. He shared, we fear, in the feeble constitution that carried his brother David to the grave at twenty-nine, Nehemiah at thirty-two, Israel at twenty-three, and his sister, Jerusha Spencer, at thirty-four.

-- 64 --

Though he himself reached sixty, his whole life seems to have been a struggle with physical infirmity. It must, however, be confessed that in the whole Brainerd family for two hundred years there has been a tendency to a morbid depression, akin to hypochondria."8

John entered Yale in 1742 and was graduated in 1746, when he was twenty-six years old. Because he came as a freshman the year of the Whittelsey affair, he must have felt deeply the manner in which his brother had been treated. But he did not abandon his goals. With his brother's moral support, fully expressed in David's letters, he went on with his studies. Indeed, the letters are of great interest not only because they show how much the elder brother cared for the younger but also because they show how readily David was able to "unburden" himself to John, how much franker he could be about his feelings than is evident in the text of the diary. Written in April and December of 1743, and in December of 1745, the letters reveal a deep concern for John's welfare ("Dear brother, may the God of all grace comfort your heart and succeed your studies, and make you an instrument of good to his people in your day") combined with a profound sense of despair ("The whole world appears to me like a huge vacuum, a vast empty space, where nothing desirable or, at least, satisfactory can possibly be derived").9

Although John throughout the diary remains in the background, he is a major figure. David thought him the only person qualified to carry on with the mission when his own health had failed. Yet John, only twenty-seven when his brother was dying, must have been a source of great anxiety to all, especially to himself. He was without experience—either as a minister or as a missionary. He had not been exposed to Indian life, had no knowledge of a tribal tongue, and could not have had a clear idea of what he was taking on. Nor is it clear why David was so eager to have his brother take his place: John might suffer the same hardships from which he himself was dying. Or did David feel that the hard work had been done and that John's job was simply to carry on? In any event, Brainerd wrote in his diary, April 13, 1747: "Assisted in examining my brother" by the New York Presbytery, for licensure; and the April 14 entry reads: "This

-- 65 --

day my brother went to my people."1 Certainly for Dickinson, Burr, and Edwards the ultimate success or failure of the Bethel enterprise depended upon John, and the authorities in Scotland placed all their hopes on this "pious and ingenious youth" whom they praised for "diligence and success in his mission."2 But the enterprise at Bethel was doomed. Incoming white settlers were determined to push out the surviving Indians and take their land. Although an effort was made to secure fresh land in New York, under a scheme to which Edwards gave great support, nothing came of the plan, and the Bethel mission, to which David Brainerd had devoted his short life, and to which John gave all the energy at his command, dwindled away.

This end, however, had been long in the making. Until the time of Robert Morris, who after 1738 was appointed chief justice, Indian land titles had been respected in New Jersey. But Morris having got from the proprietors a title to large holdings, began to treat Indian titles as a nullity, starting actions of ejectment against large numbers. Through legal technicalities he was able to drive from their fields and farms the Indians of the colony, and eventually those at Bethel as well.3 Of these events Edwards at the time remarked: "Mr. Morris, the chief justice of the province, … is suing the Indians for their lands under pretext of a will made by their former king, which was undoubtedly forged. However, he is a man of such craft and influence that it is not known how the matter will issue."4

For these reasons John listened favorably to suggestions for removal to another district. Edwards, distressed by John's plight, pressed hard in the spring of 1753 for the prospect of his going to Onohguanga (now Unadilla) on the upper reaches of the Susquehanna in New York. But the Correspondents for the Society failed to act, so that Edwards then observed: "Probably, if the removal cannot be brought about the next year, it never will be."5 Another effort was made to buy for Indian settlement a tract of

-- 66 --

land of more than four thousand acres in New Jersey. The Society in Scotland endorsed the proposition—voting a good deal of money for the purpose; but this project also ultimately failed.

By 1754, therefore, the prospects for the future were dim. Bethel, which David had procured by paying a debt of the Indians amounting to some ninety pounds, was now threatened, and on May 7, 1755, John was dismissed from his post. It was later argued in Edinburgh that because the Bethel Indians had parted with their lands, and because the Indians in the "back part of the country" were up in arms after General Edward Braddock's defeat (July 9, 1755), that John could not open a new mission. It was further agreed that William Tennent would now and then visit John's former congregation, "catechizing their children, and sometimes … to administer the sacrament of the Lord's Supper to them."6

John, of course, was deeply hurt by his abrupt dismissal, which he thought premature. "I am still in a great plunge," he wrote to Eleazer Wheelock; "I hope I am desirous to know the will of God, but am at a great loss."7 Edwards reacted with the deepest concern. Of the action taken by the Correspondents, he wrote to William McCulloch in Scotland: "I cannot say I am fully satisfied with their conduct in doing this so hastily, nor do I pretend to know so much concerning the reasons of their conduct as to have sufficient grounds positively to condemn their proceedings."8 But there was, in truth, little that could be done to save John's job. The Correspondents, unable to purchase land on which the Indians might settle, simply could not keep a missionary. Plans were later made to buy land for the Indians near New Brunswick, and in June, 1756, John was reinstated; but the scheme failed and he was dismissed again in September of the following year, having briefly taken charge of the church at Newark—lately served by Aaron Burr. When in 1758 Edwards became President of Princeton and the post at Stockbridge fell empty, it was offered to John. He did not, however, accept.

The final years of John's life were not happy. The death of his wife in 1757 was followed by the deaths of two of his three children. In the summer of 1758 he went briefly to the northern frontier as chaplain of the expedition to Crown Point against the French. The return to civilian life found him again settled as minister at Newark, having been asked once more to resume his mission, which had now been removed to Brotherton. To the end of his life he held on to the remnant of the flock which had originally come to him from his brother. The Synod of New York and Philadelphia continued up to 1780 to vote that "the interest on the Indian fund be paid to Mr. Brainerd for his services among the Indians."9 Before he died in 1781 he had established an Indian school and had encouraged Wheelock with the founding of Dartmouth College.

Neither the Scottish Society nor the Synod, however, seemed able to assume responsibility for the Bethel congregation after John had gone. By 1802 only eighty-five Indians remained, and that year they were required to leave for western New York. Along the way, it is said, they passed the night at Cranbury, sleeping on the land once occupied by Bethel, where David Brainerd had established them. According to one witness: "When the Delawares emigrated to Oneida in 1802 or 1804 they passed my residence …. They had twelve wagons for their baggage and those too feeble to journey on foot."1 But David, had he lived, could not have prevented this end, no more than could John, who had done all that he could to preserve the Bethel mission. Whether or not John had sworn an oath to do so—before his dying brother—cannot be established, although in David's last days he made two trips to be by his side.

When in April of 1747 the brothers had parted at Elizabethtown, neither then knew that he would see the other only twice more. Nor did they know in parting that this trip to New England would be David's last. Riding slowly on horseback David reached Northampton at the end of May. He rested there until June 9, and then started out for Boston, accompanied by Jerusha Edwards. In July, with his brother Israel and Jerusha riding beside him, he returned to Northampton. He was, according to

-- 68 --

John's account, "on his return from Boston to New Jersey, detained by the increase of his disorder."2 There is, however, nothing in the diary to suggest that David thought he would reach Bethel once more, for the doctors at Boston had told him that the end was near. Indeed, throughout his stay in the city David had been given up for dead several times; only through a last remission was he able to endure the five days' ride with Israel and Jerusha back to the Edwards house.

This final phase of Brainerd's life, naturally enough, has given rise to much interest, mainly because of Jerusha. From June, 1747, until the following October, when David died, she rarely left his side. But in reading the diary, the part that Jerusha played can only be guessed at, in spite of the temptation to put a romance at the center. Although Field remarks that the two "had anticipated great happiness in married life,"3 there is no direct reference to Jerusha throughout the diary. Unless Edwards has suppressed references to his daughter, Brainerd does not mention her name. When toward the end he is brought to the "gates of death" in Boston, and remarks that "divers times in this season my friends gathered round my bed to see me breathe my last," Jerusha is not singled out. On his return to Northampton, "extremely tired and faint on the road," he does not mention Jerusha, who had ridden with him. And in the final entry, where he writes that he is willing to commit to God his dearest friends and even his "absent brother" parenthetical, there is no mention of Jerusha.4

Edwards never says, furthermore, that the couple were engaged; there is no hint on his part that the friendship ever developed into a romance. Even more curious, Edwards is reluctant to mention Jerusha; until the final pages her name and sex are withheld. It was agreed, Edwards wrote of Brainerd's final trip to Boston, that "one of this family should go with him, and be helpful to him in his weak and low state;"5 but the "one of this family," Jerusha, is not singled out either by gender or name. Only toward the end is she identified; but the origin of the friendship is never revealed.

-- 69 --

Because there is nothing either in Edwards' or Brainerd's text that tells us when or where the friendship began, we can only guess at its beginnings. Perhaps it started during commencement week, 1743, when Brainerd's classmates gathered to take their degrees. The only bright spot in the events of that week may have been the meeting with Jerusha for the first time. We cannot be sure that she attended; there is no recorded evidence to say that Edwards' second daughter accompanied her father to New Haven that week. But she may have been there; for Edwards was known to take on trips one or another of his daughters, seated on a pillion behind his saddle. Jerusha would have been fourteen years old, which was not then considered too young for serious courtship. Whether or not Brainerd actively courted her that year or in the years that followed cannot, however, be known; we see the two together for the first time, in June, 1747, on David's arrival at the Edwards house.

One is tempted to imagine the couple when they first set out for Boston (as some biographers have done), hoping for a recovery from illness, making plans for the future, and romantically happy. There is, however, no romance in the text. What one finds instead is a dying man attended by a loyal companion. Edwards quotes from two letters that Jerusha wrote in Boston, both of which vividly depict Brainerd's condition. She describes him in one as "very ill with a violent fever, and extreme pain in his head and breast, and, at turns, delirious."6 In these letters we hear the voice of a conscientious person who is also a keen observer, but in neither letter can one find a hint of romance. Moreover, one is hard put to know how Brainerd felt about Jerusha. Unless two messages to anonymous recipients were addressed to Jerusha, we have no proof that he even so much as penned a note to her.

Of the letters preserved in the Life, one is described as a note that Brainerd wrote to a "special friend," dated July 31, 1744. In it the missionary condemns himself for his failings and decries the wilderness life. He then goes on to say: "It would be very refreshing to me to see you here in this desert; especially in my weak disconsolate hours." The letter concludes with words that anticipate his final remarks to Jerusha at Northampton: "But I think I could be content never to see you or any of my friends

-- 70 --

again in this world, if God would bless my labors here to the conversion of the poor Indians." He also declares: "I have much that I could willingly communicate to you, which I must omit, till Providence gives us leave to see each other."7 However, it cannot be known whether or not the letter was written to Jerusha. Nor can it be known if some later remarks were intended for her.

On May 22, 1746, Brainerd had experienced what he called "a frame somewhat remarkable," and what he wrote that night may have been addressed to Jerusha. "Farewell, friends and earthly comforts," he exclaimed, "the dearest of them all, the very dearest, if the Lord calls for it; Adieu, Adieu!"8 But Edwards put it in the text as part of the diary, and here too the figure of Jerusha can only be surmised. Indeed, the extent to which thoughts of Jerusha came into the manuscript diary at all can only be guessed at.

We learn from Edwards that in the last days at Northampton Jerusha took care of David much of the time. Dr. Mather, the village physician, had told the failing Brainerd that he would not recover, and Jerusha in these final weeks appears to have managed his life. But she knew nothing about tuberculosis—least of all that it was contagious. Brainerd was free to go where he liked, infecting as he went all those with whom he came into contact. Dr. Joseph Pynchon, who had treated him in Boston, knew as much about the disease as anyone in New England; but even he did not know that it was infectious. Whether or not Edwards ever suspected that Jerusha had contracted Brainerd's sickness cannot be deduced from the text, although the graphic details of the disease (the diarrhea, the vomiting of pus and blood) must have been her report. Suffice it to say that the two deaths were linked in Edwards' mind. "Since this," he wrote, "it has pleased a holy and sovereign God to take away this my dear child by death, on the 14th of February, next following, after a short illness of five days, in the eighteenth year of her age." She was a person, he went on, "of much the same spirit as Mr. Brainerd."9 He noted as well that she was "generally esteemed the flower of the family" and that "herein we have a great loss."1 Five days after her funeral he delivered a eulogy in which he spoke of her as "my own

-- 71 --

dear child"2 and expressed his personal feelings more freely than on any other recorded occasion in his ministry. He had her buried next to Brainerd in the town lot, where her stone still guards the slab that marks his grave (see photographs, pp. 72–3).

It was not in Edwards' nature to blame Brainerd for his daughter's death. Nor did he complain of the disruptions that the invalid's presence had brought into family life. Because of Brainerd, daily routine had in every way to be adjusted. The best that the sick man could do, according to medical opinion at the time, was to continue riding, as it was believed that such activity would prolong his life. Why the doctors held to this remedy cannot be fully explained, but the trek between saddle and bed became a ritual part of the Edwards house. Near the end, bedside care was continuously required; pans had frequently to be brought back and forth. But we shall never know, oddly enough, who had invited the missionary to come. Did the invitation come from Edwards, from Jerusha, or had Brainerd invited himself?

4. Notes on the Text and Manuscript

A question that has long been asked is to what extent the Life of Brainerd may with fairness be called a "biography" or even a "work" of Edwards? Because it consists of selections from the diary, which in turn are accompanied by Edwards' comments, the text is essentially Brainerd's. For this reason the 1808 Worcester edition of Edwards' works includes only the last part of the diary, together with Edwards' "Reflections." The editor defends his omissions on the grounds that the text "consists almost wholly of extracts from Mr. Brainerd's Diary, and his own words." It is true, the editor concedes, that "a few brief remarks are interspersed by Mr. Edwards, to connect the extracts, and give the whole the cast of a continued narrative," but "the account taken at large is too much of a mere compilation to be numbered properly among his works."

It can be argued, nonetheless, that the Life of Brainerd stands as a key volume in the Edwards corpus. The 1806 Leeds edition of the works has the Life bound with The Narrative of the Surprising Conversions (Vol. 3); for in both Edwards continually looks at

-- 72 --

-- 73 --

-- 74 --

the meaning of faith and ethics. He is able to show by example in a way that treatises and polemical writing cannot do. If it is true that his treatises were too abstruse to make an impact on the spiritual life of the ordinary person, then his Life of Brainerd represents an effort to reach a larger audience and to teach by example; to show how a "whole man" of faith should function: and the skill with which this is done makes the volume more than a "mere compilation." Because the biographer functions not as a mere compiler, but as a close observer who is also one of the principal characters involved, the work is significant. For this reason, among others, it has been reprinted more frequently than has any other text that Edwards produced.

Edwards' gleanings from the diary were not, however, the first to appear in print. Portions of Brainerd's diary were first published by William Bradford of Philadelphia in 1746, part 1 under the title Mirabilia Dei inter Indicos, or The Rise and Progress of a Remarkable Work of Grace Amongst a Number of the Indians in the Provinces of New Jersey and Pennsylvania, and part 2 later in the same year under the title Divine Grace Displayed, or The Continuance and Progress of a Remarkable Work of Grace among Some of the Indians Belonging in the Provinces of New Jersey and Pennsylvania. Though the two parts have separate title pages, the signature and pagination are continuous. These were commonly known as Brainerd's Journal and were published for the Society in Scotland for Propagating Christian Knowledge. Philip Doddridge then edited An Abridgment of Mr. David Brainerd's Journal Among the Indians (London, John Oswald, 1748), which was followed by a complete Edinburgh edition (1765) and by an abridged American edition (Worcester, 1793). An abridgment entitled The Missionary in the Wilderness, or Grace Displayed among the Heathen was later published by the Presbyterian Board of Publication (Philadelphia, 1843).

Because of the published Journal, Edwards omitted from his text those parts of the diary that had already been printed. But later editors were inclined to do otherwise. The Edinburgh edition of the Life (1765) contains both parts of the journal, together with Ebenezer Pemberton's sermon preached at Brainerd's ordination. This version was followed by the Worcester abridged edition, which has bound with it the separate abridgment of the journal (1793). Although the editor claims that "excepting

-- 75 --

some few retrenchments" the Worcester edition is an "exact copy" of the 1749 edition, Edwards' preface is missing and Brainerd's text is greatly reduced. Edited by Samuel Austin (1760–1830), it was reprinted in the nineteenth century without alteration (Newark, John Austin Crane, 1811).

A second Edinburgh edition includes both parts of the journal, Pemberton's sermon, and Charles Beatty's "Journal of a Two Months Tour; with a view of Promoting Religion Among the Frontier Inhabitants of Pennsylvania" (T. Maccliesh and Co. for J. Ogle, 1798). The account of Beatty's efforts in 1768 to take religion west of the Allegheny Mountains is appended only to this edition, which also appeared as a London imprint (R. Ogle, 1798). But of all the early versions of the Life John Wesley's had the widest appeal. Wesley's Extract of the Life of the late Rev. David Brainerd went through two Bristol editions (W. Pine, 1768, 1771) and four London editions (G. Paramore, 1793; G. Whitfield, 1800; Penryn, W. Cock, 1815; and J. Kershaw, 1825). In a still more reduced form it was printed in volume 12 of Wesley's collected Works (32 vols., Bristol, W. Pine, 1771–74). It was the first version to incorporate the journal into the text and the only one to exclude Edwards' name from the title page, which states that the volume is "By John Wesley." Edwards' preface is omitted, although his "Reflections" and some of the commentary are retained.

Wesley was not, however, the sole violator of the text. John Styles (1782–1849), the popular Methodist preacher of Brighton, not only abridged the Life but also replaced Edwards' commentary with his own (London, Williams and Smith, 1808). The title page reads "From President Edwards, By John Styles." "As the work is now becoming scarce," wrote Styles, "a republication was at first proposed." But "a learned and excellent friend, in whose judgment I have implicit confidence," he explained, "suggested that to re-write the life, and judiciously to select from the original volume the most important and interesting portions of the diary and journal, would be conferring upon the public a real benefit, as it would greatly reduce the book both in size and price, without at all diminishing its intrinsic worth." The original text, in Styles' opinion, suffered from bad editing. "The life by Edwards," he observed, "has been supposed to contain much unimportant and exuberant matter." He accused Edwards of "not

-- 76 --

using sufficiently the pruning knife while preparing the private diary of Mr. Brainerd for the press." It was Styles' plan not only to "re-write" Edwards' commentary, but also to rely on Wesley's text. "For the materials I am indebted to President Edwards," he wrote, "and for the extracts in their abridged form … I have to express my obligation to Mr. Wesley." But Styles even further abridged the diary, leaving ample space for his own interpretation of the missionary's life.

The Styles text, which gained immediate popularity, went through four editions. The English imprint of 1808 was followed by the first American edition (Boston, Samuel T. Armstrong, 1812), by a second English edition (London, F. Westley, 1820), and finally by a second American edition (Boston, Samuel T. Armstrong; Crocker and Brewster, 1821). But the Styles version, as popular as it was, had also to compete with other editions, including a complete text, "carefully revised and corrected" (London, Burton and Smith, 1818), and a revised edition of the time that has continued to be printed down to the present day. Compiled by Edwards' grandson, Sereno Edwards Dwight, and entitled Memoirs of the Rev. David Brainerd, it is still the best known version of all (New Haven, S. Converse, 1822).

It was the editor's intention, said Dwight, "to render this volume as a memoir of Brainerd, complete." He therefore had included, among other things, letters from John Brainerd and, for the first time in any edition of the Life, Edwards' sermon preached at Brainerd's funeral. He claimed, moreover, that Brainerd's journal was "now for the first time incorporated with the rest of his diary, in a regular chronological series," although both Wesley and Styles had done the same thing. But Dwight cast aside their editions with a flourish. "The abridgment by Wesley," he wrote, "the editor has not seen." "That by Styles," he observed, "is a cheap 12 mo. volume made at a time … when probably it was supposed that a complete work would not sell."

Dwight, of course, took great liberties with the text, as he did with all his grandfather's works. He did not, like Styles, literally "re-write" the Life, but he changed the wording according to whim and altered the sentence structure throughout. Although at first he had intended to include the volume in his complete Edwards edition, he changed his mind on the grounds that it was "almost wholly the work of Brainerd," and that "the works of Edwards

-- 77 --

are very voluminous without it." He suggested, nevertheless, that "subscribers … can easily bind it as the last volume of these works."

An abridged version of the Memoirs, printed for the American Sunday School Union, was published at Philadelphia (1826, 1827, and 1830). Toward the end of the century James Manning Sherwood republished the full text, to which he added an essay of his own (New York, Funk and Wagnalls, 1884). This "Sherwood" version was then reprinted as a "Mission Study Reference" by The Young People's Missionary Movement, and went through three editions (c. 1884, 1885, and 1891). In recent years Scholarly Press has issued a facsimile reprint of the original 1822 edition (St. Clair Shores, Mich., 1970).

By the turn of the century the distinction between the Life and the journal had been blurred, with the Life frequently referred to as "Brainerd's Journal." Such was the case with Extracts from the Journal of David Brainerd (Chicago, The Moody Bible Institute, 1900). Abridged by Homer W. Hodge, it was followed by an imprint entitled David Brainerd, the man of prayer, compiled from his diary as recorded in "The Works of Jonathan Edwards" (Grand Rapids, Zondervan Publishing House, 1941). Edited by Oswald J. Smith, it was clearly an effort to reach as wide an audience as possible. Smith later published David Brainerd, his message for today (London, Marshall, Morgan, and Scott, 1949).

The most recent version of the Life bears the title Life and Diary

-- 79 --

of David Brainerd (Chicago, Moody Press, 1949). Abridged by Philip E. Howard, Jr., and published in the Wycliffe Series of Christian Classics, it also incorporates selections from the journal. But the volume leaves much to be desired. Because Edwards' commentary is reduced, his presence is barely felt. The "Reflections" are excluded, as are other related texts. Even less satisfying, however, is the "condensed" version of this same imprint (Moody Press, 1958?, 1966?). Cheaply printed and bound, it deletes the editor's name as well as all explanatory notes.

Not much better, but in another category, is Alfred Stucki's David Brainerd, Ein Leben für die Indianer (Basel, Heinrich Majer, 1961?), which retells the saga, quoting from the Life and journal. Even worse is Richard A. Hasler's Journey with David Brainerd (Downers Grove, Ill., Inter Varsity Press, 1975). This sentimental volume, subtitled "Forty Days and Forty Nights with David Brainerd," contains brief selections from the Dwight text, each followed by a prayer of Hasler's own devising. Hasler chose the Dwight text, he tells us, "because it is the first edition to include a complete collection of Brainerd's writings in chronological order." The extent to which Dwight had altered these writings is not taken into account. But neither Dwight nor the others had been alone in tampering with the diary, for Edwards had been the first. As the manuscript reveals, he took great liberties throughout.

The Beinecke Library owns the thirty-six manuscript pages of the diary that correspond to the first fifteen pages of Edwards' printed text. It is Brainerd's account of his youth, conversion, and his first eighteen months at Yale. Written on sheets about 6″ × 6¾″, some words have been crossed out; but for the most part corrections are few. In the top margin of page 30, in Brainerd's hand, is the inscription: "I have corrected no further. D. B." At the top of page 31 is written in another hand, "The Author's own corrections: by another person." The large number of variations show that the manuscript is by no means the printer's copy. A comparison with the 1749 edition shows that drastic alterations were made in the original text.3 Because the Yale edition is the

-- 80 --

first to print the manuscript, set in parallel columns with Edwards' text, these alterations can readily be discerned throughout.

Edwards, for example, simply omitted the opening paragraph: "For my own private satisfaction, etc., (may it likewise be for the glory of God) I make the following remarks upon the various scenes of my life. I was born April 20th, 1718." Because Brainerd had put "private satisfaction" above the "glory of God," the passage could not stand: and this practice of omitting passages is followed at crucial points. When Brainerd too vividly describes his thoughts, or shows a self-concern that Edwards deems extreme, the sentence or paragraph is dropped. In addition, when Brainerd suggests that he has been drawn to doctrine that departs from orthodoxy, the passage is omitted. In describing his conversion Brainerd writes: "I constantly strove after whatever qualifications I imagined others obtained before the reception of Christ, in order to recommend me to his favor" (p. 110). But the passage in the manuscript that follows is omitted from Edwards' text: "and hoped that if I got those terms, upon which I supposed God shewed mercy to others, wrought in me, that then God would be as good to me as others and by this means still sought salvation as it were by the works of the Law; for though there was no goodness in those things, I thought requisites (or qualifications) by my own confessions to God frequently, yet I hoped God would save me when they were wrought in me" (p. 110).

Passages that show defiance of divine will are also left out. Brainerd writes: "for I thought God designed my damnation" (p. 125). The sentences that follow, however, are omitted; for in these Brainerd confesses his "dreadful enmity and blasphemy against God" and his refusal to pray that "the will of the Lord might be done." Because Brainerd concludes that " 'twas God's will to thrust me into hell; and if I should pray that that might be done I expected he would do it" (pp. 126–27), Edwards deleted the whole passage from the text.

Because Brainerd frequently sank into deep despair, Edwards condensed certain passages and changed the wording. When Brainerd sank too low, the passage was simply left out. Brainerd writes: "I was something eased of that distress which I felt while struggling against a sight of myself, and of the divine sovereignty" (pp. 131–32). What follows in the manuscript Edwards

-- 81 --

omits: "I felt something like a criminal at the bar waiting for his sentence, excepting this, I felt but little concern which way my case went, for the fear of hell was almost if not entirely taken away from me" (pp. 131–32). The passage then continues: "And in this case I felt neither love to God or desire of heaven as I used to imagine I did. Neither fear of hell, or love to the present world" (p. 131). However, this sentence and the six that follow it are also omitted from the text.

Edwards allows Brainerd to say of his prayers that "there was no more virtue or goodness in them than there would be in my paddling with my hands in the water (which was the comparison I had then in mind); and this because they were not performed from any love or regard to God" (p. 134). But the manuscript, which is not followed here, goes on to explain how through a vision Brainerd came to that conclusion:

"Methought I saw a stately house, a very beauteous building, and I had been heaping up dirt, filth, and rubbish against the foreside of it supposing I was doing the building some service, but when I saw that what I had been heaping up by the side of it cleaved off from it and did not in the least measure touch it, and so consequently could not possibly answer the end I thought I proposed, which was in part the advantage and strengthening of it, and likewise that the building was sufficiently established by its own strength and did not need the assistance of any of my pile of dirt; and farther discovering that I had so much as in one day dishonored and eclipsed the glory of the building by throwing rubbish against it, though indeed it cleaved from it and so did not actually mar and destroy the beauty of it, this sight brought me to that amazing stand and affectually stunned all my projections and attempts to get relief in my own way" (pp. 134–35).

Clearly Edwards did not want the Life to contain visions. Nor did he want it to reflect Brainerd's intolerance, a trait that had brought on the Whittelsey affair. Brainerd writes: "I was afraid, ashamed, and guilty, to come before God, and was exceedingly pressed with a sense of guilt" (p. 141). But this sentence, which Edwards alters, continues "… and I knew the sin that caused this amazing guilt before God," which Edwards deletes, together with what follows:

-- 82 --

"I used now (as well as when under conviction) to marvel at the conduct of most (both ministers and people) that called themselves Christians, that when they met together they could talk of all the world, and spend their time in jesting and the like and never say a word of spiritual concerns or anything of that dreadful wilderness that I have been led along through. I used sometimes to leave the company and society of such and get alone in the woods, or any other place of retirement in order to pray. Sometimes I used to think such persons were not nor could be Christians, and sometimes I thought there were two sorts of Christians, but I desired not to be of that sort, for I thought they felt nothing of the sweetness of religion. At other times I thought I was a creature alone and by myself and knew not of any that felt as I did, though I thought some certain persons did, by their actions and discourse that I had formerly observed; but being now at a distance from them I had no opportunity to converse with them" (pp. 141–42).

Edwards at times followed the manuscript fairly closely; at other times he rewrote the text, using his own words with little regard for the original sentences or phrases. He often improved the sentence structure or clarified the meaning, but the vitality of expression he suppressed. Because he altered what he did not like and omitted what he found unacceptable, one finds in the manuscript pages a far more imaginative and certainly more desperate man than the printed text will allow. One also finds a voice that is decidedly less genteel. Where Brainerd writes that he was not addicted to "frolicking," Edwards adds "as it is called" (p. 102). Where Brainerd writes, "I walked away a considerable distance back of college where I was undiscovered at noon," Edwards writes, "I walked to a considerable distance from the college, in the fields alone at noon" (p. 145). Moreover, the tendency on Edwards' part is always to tone down what he finds. When Brainerd confesses, "In short, I was a very good Pharisee, i.e., had a very good outside, but rested entirely on my duties," Edwards simply puts down, "In short, I had a very good outside, and rested entirely on my duties" (p. 105). Where Brainerd declares that he felt "such a sense of my amazing danger and the wrath of God and hell, as it were, under my feet," Edwards substitutes "such a

-- 83 --

sense of my danger and the wrath of God" (p. 105). The sentence "And sometimes there appeared mountains before me to obstruct my hope of mercy, and the work of conversion appeared so great I thought I should never, never get through" becomes "I thought I should never be the subject of it parenthetical" (p. 106).

Brainerd's imaginative exploits are never allowed to stand. The words "amazing" and "amazed" are frequently cut out. There is, in addition, a conscious effort made to detach the experience from the speaker. Brainerd writes, "but in the season of communion I felt some abatement of this sweet life and fervor," which Edwards renders as "but in the season of the communion there was some abatement of this sweet life and fervor" (p. 148). The voice is made less personal, the felt experience disguised. Although it was common practice at the time to take great liberties with a manuscript, the reader has a right to expect greater fidelity to the text. Because Edwards altered not only the thirty-six pages of manuscript that correspond to the first part of the printed text, but also diary leaves for the summer of 1743 and the spring of 1744, there is every reason to assume that the whole manuscript was revised according to the pattern established at the start.

Apart from the Yale manuscript, there are two other extant "Brainerd" manuscripts, neither of which is composed in the missionary's hand. The Connecticut Historical Society holds 236 manuscript pages, recording events on the New Jersey and Pennsylvania frontier from June 19, 1745, to June 19, 1746. (Only the "General Remarks" and addenda at the end are in Brainerd's script.) The Edinburgh University Library holds 139 manuscript pages, in still another hand, which is a transcription of some of the events also described in the Connecticut manuscript (November 24, 1745-May 10, 1746). Although both manuscripts are counterparts of the 1746 published Journal, neither is the printer's copy, for alterations abound in the printed text. The Edinburgh manuscript, written in an elegant, professional script, bears no resemblance to the Connecticut manuscript, which is composed in a cramped hand that is closer to printing than to script. (The Connecticut chirography is characteristic of an earlier period and may be that of a person older than Brainerd.) Bound in leather (pages 43–82 are missing), with sheets inserted under the front cover binding, it has inscribed on the cover: "This ms. copy of the Journal of the Rev. David Brainerd among the Indians

-- 84 --

at Crossweeksung in New Jersey was obtained by J. W. Barber of a very aged lady in Hebron, Conn., about the year 1830. If I am correctly informed the brother of this lady had lived among the Indians."

Were it not for Brainerd's letter to Eleazer Wheelock (see Related Correspondence), this manuscript would present somewhat of a puzzle. The letter, which describes it, had been sent along with it: "The large manuscript that accompanies this letter," wrote Brainerd, "is a copy of a Journal I lately sent home to Scotland." He further explained: "The pressure of my business is such that I was obliged to employ another hand to transcribe it for me, except the general remarks at the latter end, which I wrote myself." An apology for the copyist's errors explains the addenda: "he has not been careful to preserve the printing and distinct paragraphs as he ought to have been," Brainerd wrote. "The former of these difficulties might have been redressed, if I'd had time, but the latter could not. I hope, however, you'll be able to read it. There were some sentences wholly omitted, which I have thrown as addendas into the last page, and have there shown where they ought to be read." Although the letter itself is a copy, not in Brainerd's hand, it identifies the Connecticut manuscript as the one sent to Wheelock in 1745.

In addition to the Yale, Edinburgh, and Connecticut manuscripts, two autograph leaves survive from the 1743 section of the published Journal: one numbered 13 and 14, for July 8, 12, and 15, held at the Pierpont Morgan Library, another for August 8–13, mounted in glass at The Mission House, Stockbridge. A diary leaf for August 14–19, 1743, and another for April 27, 29, May 1, 10, and 11, 1744, are held in the Beinecke collection at Yale.
