Editorial Statement

The text of Jonathan Edwards is reproduced in this Edition as he wrote it in manuscript, or if he published it himself, as it was printed in the first edition. In order to present this text to modern readers as practically readable, several technical adjustments have been made. Those which can be addressed categorically are as follows:

1. All spelling is regularized and conformed to that of Webster's Third New International Dictionary, a step that does not involve much more than removing the "u" from "colour" or "k" from "publick" since Edwards was a good speller, used relatively modern spelling, and generally avoided "y" contractions. His orthographic contractions and abbreviations, such as ampersands, "call'd," and "thems." are spelled out, though pronounced contractions, such as "han't" and "ben't," are retained.

2. There is no regular punctuation in most of Edwards' manuscripts, and where it does exist, as in the earliest sermons, it tends to be highly erratic. Editors take into account Edwards' example in punctuation and related matters, but all punctuation is necessarily that of the editor, including paragraph divisions (especially in some notebooks such as the "Miscellanies") and the emphasizing devices of italics and capitalization. In reference to capitalization, it should be noted that pronouns referring to the deity are lowercase except in passages where Edwards confusingly mixes "he's" referring to God and man: here capitalization of pronouns referring to the deity sorts out the references for the reader.

3. Numbered heads designate important structures of argument in Edwards' sermons, notebooks, and treatises. Numbering, including spelled-out numbers, has been regularized and corrected where necessary. Particularly in the manuscript sermon texts, numbering has been clarified by the use of systematic schemes of heads and subheads in accordance with eighteenth-century homiletical form, a practice similar to modern analytical outline form. Thus the series of subordinated head number forms, 1, (1), I, a, (a), in the textual exegesis, and the series, I, First, 1, (1), I, a, (a), in Doctrine and Application divisions, make it possible to determine sermon head relationships at a glance.

-- x --

4. Textual intervention to regularize Edwards' citation of Scripture includes the correction of erroneous citation, the regularizing of citation form (including the standardization of book abbreviations), and the completion of quotations which Edwards' textual markings indicate should be completed (as in preaching).

5. Omissions and lacunae in the manuscript text are filled by insertions in square brackets ([ ]); repeated phrases sometimes represented by Edwards with a long dash are inserted in curly brackets ({ }). In all cases of uncertain readings, annotation gives notice of the problem. Markings in the text designate whole word units even when only a few letters are at issue.

6. Minor slips of the pen or obvious typographical errors are corrected without annotation. Likewise, Edwards' corrections, deletions, and internal shifts of material are observed but not noted unless of substantive interest.

7. Quotations made by the editor from the Bible (AV) and other secondary sources are printed verbatim ac literatim. Edwards' quotations from such sources are often rather free but are not corrected and are not annotated as such unless significant omissions or distortions are involved.

Other conditions of textual preparation are related to differences of genre and factors unique to particular texts or manuscripts. For information on such matters please consult the note on the text in each volume or the first volume of each series within the Edition.

Editor's Introduction

Jonathan Edwards was a person of his time and place, and no more so than in his understanding of the church and ministry. Native son of the "New England Way," he absorbed the conventions and constraints of the Congregational tradition. In the main this was the system articulated in 1648 in the Cambridge Platform, though for any minister in mid-eighteenth-century Hampshire County, Massachusetts, Congregationalism encompassed certain amendments relating to membership and the independence of each gathered church. Inheriting this system, Edwards was also heir to historic lines of tension. He indicated one of these when, late in life, he professed himself no admirer of congregational democracy.1 Uneasy with the balance of power between lay members and the ministry, Edwards felt a similar disquiet with the relation between councils and individual congregations; here too the fruits of experience left him disenchanted. But the tension that especially figured in his ministry arose from the dual role of the church: functioning as an "instituted" means of grace to everyone and financed by a townwide system of taxation, the church in Northampton, like most others elsewhere in New England, was also a "gathered congregation" in which membership was restricted to "visible saints."

It was Edwards' fate to be caught up in circumstances that exacerbated these long-standing tensions. One of these circumstances was the drift of theological opinion away from orthodoxy. Alarmed by this drift, a majority of the Hampshire County ministers attempted in 1734 and 1735 to prevent First Church, Springfield, from choosing Robert Breck, an apparent "Arminian,"2 as its minister. Another circumstance was the outbreak of revivals. Edwards led a revival in Northampton in 1734–35, and the town was shaken a second time

-- 2 --

during the Great Awakening of 1740–42. Out of the Awakening emerged a movement of protest against any practices that compromised the gathered church. In his own way Edwards shared in this reaction. Hence in February 1749, he told the Northampton church that he had come to believe that persons requesting admission to the Lord's Supper must be "godly or gracious persons."3 His dismissal followed in July 1750.

This volume in The Works of Jonathan Edwards contains one publication in which Edwards defended the actions of the group that tried to thwart the ordination of Robert Breck, two publications in which he defended his conception of the church, and a fourth in which he narrated the sequence of events in Northampton that culminated in his dismissal. Collectively, these texts bring home how much Edwards was engaged with issues that grew out of Congregationalism, the amendments to it known as Stoddardeanism, the revivals of the 1730s and 1740s, and religion as experienced by the laity of Northampton, or what we may term "popular religion."

The standard interpretation of the turmoil about church membership that led to Edwards' dismissal remains the one advanced early in the nineteenth century by his grandson and biographer, Sereno E. Dwight. Dwight's thesis may be reduced to the phrase, "Stoddardeanism (i.e. 'laxness') begat Arminianism." Writing as a committed Congregationalist and evangelical, Dwight connected two quite different processes and made one the cause of the other. The first of these was an easing of access to church membership that he traced back to the "half-way covenant" decision of the Synod of 1662 and that he saw as abetted by the Reverend Solomon Stoddard in his Northampton ministry, for Stoddard had allowed "unregenerate" persons to enter the church and participate in the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. According to Dwight, the easing of access to the church accelerated a second process, a weakening of doctrinal orthodoxy as "liberals" began to emphasize free will and human ability, a tendency that culminated in the rise of Unitarianism.4

-- 3 --

Only recently has it become clear that Dwight, together with a host of nineteenth-century evangelical writers, misrepresented Stoddard and the consequences of Stoddardeanism in eighteenth-century New England.5 The larger error has been to impose a framework of "declension" on the relation between orthodoxy and lay people, for the received tradition, which continues to be widely reiterated among twentieth-century historians of religion in New England, has it that sincerity gave way to hypocrisy among the third- and fourth-generation colonists.6 That such failures of understanding should prevail for almost two centuries is perhaps a measure of how easily moral judgments—in this case, a contempt for "formalism" and, if I may say so, popular religion—have infiltrated the telling of the history of New England Congregationalism. The dismissal of Jonathan Edwards in 1750 has stirred up further bouts of special pleading.7 The interpretive tradition includes yet another vein of speculation, the efforts some have made to discern themes from Edwards' philosophical theology or his fascination with the history of redemption in the controversy about admission to the Lord's Supper.8

-- 4 --

The purpose of this introduction is to locate An Humble Inquiry and its companion, Misrepresentations Corrected, within more appropriate contexts: first and foremost, debates and decisions about the nature of the church that unfolded in English and American Puritanism in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries; second, popular religion as Edwards encountered it in Northampton; third, the thinking of Solomon Stoddard and the ministers who espoused a Stoddardean understanding of the church; and fourth, Edwards' place in the revival of the 1740s and his response to the more radical tendencies that emerged from that revival.

These four contexts were parts of a whole in which the social and the religious were commingled and inseparable, a whole that we may term a culture. Growing up within this culture, Jonathan Edwards was constrained by its boundaries in imagining (or reimagining) the nature of the church. He was constrained for another reason. Because this culture was a whole, any change in one of its parts reverberated in the others. The nature of the church was no narrowly religious or doctrinal matter, but a many-sided issue that quickly became charged with politics. In bringing popular religion into the forefront of my narrative, my purpose is to reveal more fully the politics of the Northampton controversy. Why did the townspeople explode when Edwards proposed to restrict their access to baptism and the Lord's Supper? The answer to this question has to do with the mingling of the social and the religious in eighteenth-century New England.

The Robert Breck Affair

The story of the entanglement that is known to us as the Breck Affair has been told before, and the full details need not be repeated here.9 Daniel Brewer, the longtime minister of First Precinct, Springfield,

-- 5 --

died in 1733. Seeking to replace him, the church attempted twice to recruit graduates of Yale. These efforts failing, it turned to Harvard and in May 1734 invited Robert Breck to preach with a view to settlement. Breck was installed and ordained in January 1736 despite the opposition of a majority of the ministers in Hampshire County.

Edwards was one of the Hampshire ministers who opposed Breck's settlement in Springfield. Fifteen years later, Edwards wrote to his congregation that "I had no hand at all in opposing these ministers parenthetical in anything they did; for all that was done of that nature, was done when I was not in New England, and when I was totally ignorant of anything that was done until all was over. My crime was merely defending what others had done, at their request." This retrospective judgment is contradicted by evidence that Edwards did indeed play a significant role in the controversy. As late as August 1735 he cosigned letters critical of Breck that the Hampshire Association sent to the Springfield church. During September and October he was out of New England, spending at least part of the time in New York City.1 This absence explains why Edwards did not attend the council meeting that resulted in Breck's arrest, yet it did not hinder his being asked to write the defense of the Hampshire Association's actions. That defense was A Letter to the Author of the Pamphlet (1737), the final shot in a very public quarrel between certain Boston ministers who befriended Breck and those in Hampshire County who opposed him.2

-- 6 --

Robert Breck arrived in Hampshire County with his reputation clouded by reports of how he had behaved as a student at Harvard and as a ministerial candidate in Windham County, Connecticut. Born in Marlborough, Massachusetts, in 1713, where his father of the same name was the town minister, he matriculated at Harvard in 1726. By his own admission young Breck "fell into bad Company" and had a lively time of it until his father withdrew him in May 1729. A year later the faculty agreed to let him graduate with the class of 1730 on the grounds that, in the interim, he had "followed those studies at home whch were proper for his College study," had "Confessed his former Miscarriage at college," and, as testified by proper witnesses, had demonstrated "studious blameless behavior." Breck returned to Harvard in 1732 and took his M.A. in 1733. He began to preach in December of that year as a candidate for the ministry of Third Parish, or Scotland District, in Windham. He quickly found himself in trouble, for the sermons that he preached, including a guest sermon from the pulpit of his neighbor Thomas Clap of First Parish, contained statements that ran contrary to the Westminster Confession. Breck seems to have asserted that persons who followed the "Light of Nature" could be saved with or without faith, that certain passages of Scripture were corrupt, and that predestination gave "no Encouragement to Duty."3

The winds of change were blowing in the intellectual universe of New England Calvinism, and Breck was shifting with them. As some who had heard him preach in Windham testified, he spoke admiringly of the English Arian Thomas Chubb. Breck conceded to his critics that, at the outset of his ministry, he was not "fully settled and established in" the "Point" of faith as "necessary to Salvation." In his own

-- 7 --

defense he painted himself as someone still at bottom soundly orthodox, though liking to voice speculations from the pulpit. Hindsight tells us that, along with others in New England, he was moving toward a more rational theology that his contemporaries usually termed Arminianism. Yet he was willing to avow the Westminster Confession and to declare that he "hated the Name of an Arminian."4

His older neighbor and fellow Harvard graduate, Thomas Clap of First Parish, remained traditionally orthodox. Dismayed by the ideas Breck was voicing, Clap was also troubled by rumors of Breck's misdoings as an undergraduate. Had he been expelled from Harvard for stealing books? When Clap put this question to him, Breck denied the story and defended his opinions.5 Third Parish gave him a vote of confidence by calling him to the pulpit. Even so, aware that Clap was not convinced of his merits, Breck decided to look elsewhere for a permanent position.6

The offer from First Precinct drew Breck to Springfield in May 1734. Word followed swiftly of his problems in eastern Connecticut. The main line of communication between Windham and Springfield ran through members of a clerical and civil elite, the Williamses. The controversy would eventually involve five members of this clan, all of them ministers and all committed to defending orthodoxy. In 1734 the family patriarch, the Reverend William Williams of Hatfield, Massachusetts, was the senior minister in Hampshire County and the man most likely to serve as "moderator" of meetings of the Hampshire ministers. One of his sons, Elisha, was rector (chief officer) of Yale College, and another (by a different wife), Solomon, was minister in Lebanon, Connecticut. A cousin of theirs, Stephen, was minister in Longmeadow, adjacent (on the south) to Springfield. Stephen's

-- 8 --

brother Eliezer was minister in Mansfield, Connecticut, which adjoined Lebanon. Two other Hampshire ministers were or soon would be related to the Williamses. Jonathan Ashley of Deerfield married one of William Williams' daughters in 1736. And because William Williams had married, as his second wife, a daughter of Solomon Stoddard, he was Edwards' uncle. As clan and cousinage, the Williamses had been known to act forcefully in securing the appointment of ministers they favored and in blocking those they disliked.7

Hearing of his neighbor Thomas Clap's concerns, Eliezer Williams wrote in August 1734 to Stephen that Robert Breck was "not a sutable Person to be employ'd in the Ministry." Thus forewarned, the ministers in Hampshire County, acting in their organized capacity as the Hampshire Association and assuming a customary role as supervisors of an ordination, told Breck on September 4 that they wanted him to secure a "certificate of orthodoxy" from Thomas Clap and two other Windham County residents, the Reverend Daniel Kirtland of Norwich and a layman, Nathaniel Huntington, of Windham. But the rupture between Breck and Clap was not easily healed. In late August Breck had written Clap a letter in which he complained of what was being said about his preaching and his misdemeanors as an undergraduate. "I took you always to be a Gentleman," he told the older man, "& not only so, but a Christian." A visit he paid to Clap in September failed to produce "Satisfaction." Two months later, Breck was telling friends and members of the Springfield church that his Windham County critics did not speak the truth. Clap, unreconciled, communicated directly on October 7 to the Association about errors in Breck's preaching. In late November, six of its members, including Edwards, advised the parish not to proceed to his settlement.8

Settlement in Springfield seemed all the more unlikely since Breck had reached an impasse in negotiations over salary with the parish. But Breck returned from Cambridge early in the new year after the church members, having decided that he was without fault and resenting what they saw as interference from the Hampshire ministers, recalled him on January 1. Hoping all would work out for the best,

-- 9 --

William Williams urged Breck to send Clap a "Confession," which he did. Breck presented this confession and a new "Vindication" to the members of the Association on April 8. In the latter he acknowledged having "tho't it not an unprofitable way of spending time in discoursing upon some of the controverted points in divinity." But he also insisted on his orthodoxy.9 Thereupon three members of the Association, William Rand, Ebenezer Devotion,1 and Isaac Chauncy, declared that they were willing to begin the ordination process. Six others, led by William Williams, voted for a full hearing of Breck's critics, to take place at a special session of the Association. From this point on, the majority clung to the condition that, before ordination could proceed, Breck should meet face-to-face with those who accused him of misbehavior.

On April 17 the Springfield church, ignoring a cautionary letter from William Williams, gave Breck a formal call. The vote was not unanimous, and the small handful in opposition—the "dissatisfied"—turned to the Association for advice and assistance. Their complaint was discussed at a meeting of the Association on August 14. But by this time Breck, armed with a certificate of orthodoxy signed by eight ministers from elsewhere, had accepted the call of the church, which in turn invited four of the Hampshire ministers—the three who voted in his favor in April, together with William Williams—to participate in the ordination council. William Williams declined the invitation. When he learned that Breck had also invited four ministers from outside Hampshire County, he wrote to one of them, the Reverend William Cooper of Fourth (Brattle Street) Church in Boston, suggesting that one or two of Breck's supporters in Boston join with their Hampshire colleagues to air all the issues. But the Boston clergy were embarked on a different course. Cooper and three others, Samuel Mather, William Welsted, and Joshua Gee, turned up in Springfield and convened an ordination council on October 7. Thomas Clap was on hand as principal complainant. In the midst of these sessions, which took place behind closed doors and extended into the next day, Breck was abruptly arrested, charged in civil court with preaching heresy,

-- 10 --

and taken off to Connecticut, the site of the alleged offense. Released on bond, he returned to Springfield one day later in time to arrange for a reconvening of the council in January 1736. All was calm on this occasion; Cooper preached the ordination sermon, and Breck was formally installed and ordained.

Thereafter the controversy was fought out via pamphlets and letters to the press. Someone sympathetic to Breck fired the first shot, a letter in the Boston Gazette of October 10, 1735, mocking the literary style of "country" ministers, accusing them of meddling, and ridiculing another letter (itself not fully quoted) written by a person whose identity was partially concealed. As was later revealed, the author of this second letter was Elisha Williams, who had written to his cousin Stephen urging that the Hampshire ministers publicize "the Folly and wickedness" of the ordination council. Other letters followed, pro and con.2 In December the Springfield church petitioned the Massachusetts House of Representatives to affirm that the council was in order. The House conducted an inquiry into the conduct of the three justices of the peace who had ordered Breck's arrest, and in January, by a close vote, officially rebuked them for having "interrupted that Church and Ecclesiastical Council."3

The Hampshire ministers fought back, telling their side of the story, with due emphasis on Breck's limitations as preacher and person, in A Narrative of the Proceedings of Those Ministers of the County of Hampshire, Etc. that have Disapproved of the Late Measures Taken in Order to the Settlement of Mr. Robert Breck (1736). A riposte was soon forthcoming, the anonymous An Examination of and Some Answer to a Pamphlet, Entitled, A Narrative and Defense…With a Vindication of Those Ministers and Churches, that Approved of, and Acted in the Settlement of Said Mr. Breck (1736). The series ended with the reply of the Hampshire majority, Edwards' A Letter to the Author.

The author of An Examination was almost certainly William Cooper and not Robert Breck, to whom it is attributed in certain older bibliographies.

-- 11 --

Neither at this stage nor in his previous intervention was Cooper motivated by a personal relationship to Robert Breck. "As to Mr. Breck himself," he declared in a letter to William Williams, "tho' I heartily wish his Usefulness, and entertain good Hopes concerning him, yet I have no particular Interest in him, and know of nothing to give me an undue Byass towards him."4 The truth of this statement is borne out by Edwards' silence on the matter. And Edwards could not accuse Cooper of defending a fellow liberal. The Boston minister spoke in favor of the Westminster doctrines in the sermon he preached at Breck's ordination.5 When the Great Awakening erupted in the 1740s, he defended it against its critics and, more tellingly, signed three prefaces to as many books by Edwards in which the revival was presented as a "work of grace." His endorsement of Edwards extended back to 1731, when he lent his name to the preface of Edwards' first published sermon.

These circumstances force us to look elsewhere for the motives of the Boston ministers. Their rhetoric is at once revealing and concealing. From the outset of the controversy, these men described the Hampshire majority as "a set of ambitious, arbitrary, and unjust usurpers" intent on disrupting the "liberty" of local congregations to choose as they pleased. "And a fine Scheme it is," Cooper blustered, "to fetter and enslave the Churches; and have Things carried according to the Will…or arbitrary Disposition of a particular Set of Ministers." Cooper and his colleagues argued that associations like the one in Hampshire County were a violation of Congregational principles as articulated in the Cambridge Platform of 1648:

And in a Word, We don't look upon them parenthetical to be any Part of the Constitution of these Churches…such an Association of Pastors…is an Usurpation upon the Liberties of Congregational Churches, and those Churches are servile indeed that tamely submit to them therein.

Local churches could decide for themselves whether to seek "Advice" from a ministerial association, and the authority of that advice was

-- 12 --

contingent on the "free and voluntary" actions of the congregation. From this reasoning followed Cooper's case against the Hampshire ministers: they intervened in Springfield without ever being asked to do so by the church.6

Via such assertions, Cooper cast the friends of Breck as defenders of the rights of local congregations as enshrined in the Cambridge Platform, and his critics as "Presbyterian"-minded ministers. But let us look more closely at this rhetoric. We may do so, first, by returning to the Cambridge Platform, as Edwards and the Hampshire ministers did in their rebuttal. This description of Congregational church polity was the product of the founding generation of New England ministers, the immigrants from England who experimented with a system of church government that empowered individual congregations—hence the term "congregational" or its near synonym, "independent"—and entrusted the "power of the keys," or decision making in the congregation, to (male) full church members. In both respects the Congregational system curtailed the authority of the ministry as it was customarily sustained in the "presbyterian" or "classical" system of church government. The framers of the Platform modified the strict independence of each local congregation by urging a process of "communion" or consultation between churches and by affirming the legitimacy of "synods" as "necessary to the well-being of churches, for the establishment of truth, & peace therin." As defined in the Platform, synods were a relatively toothless instrument. Intervention from outside was proper when the church itself requested advice. Yet the Platform also recognized that situations could arise that would justify a "fuller act of brotherly communion," a process of "admonition" undertaken without prior invitation. And in chapter 8, which described the election of ministers as performed by individual congregations, the Platform urged that "neighbour-churches be advised withall, & their help made use of in the triall of church-officers, in order to their choyce."7

Much had happened between 1648 and 1735 to fill out these rules. The many episodes of conflict over issues like church membership and whom to elect to the ministry had prompted an increasing use of "councils," often though not always a small group of ministers, to mediate disputes. Experiences of this kind, together with the felt need

-- 13 --

to affirm a stronger role for the ministry as a collective body, lay behind a resolution passed by the Synod of 1662 declaring that "Consociation of Churches" was scriptural and "expedient." Not until the 1690s, however, did the ministers in New England formalize the existence of "consociations" or "associations," groups that enrolled the clergy in a given region for the purpose of discussing issues of the day. An attempt in Massachusetts to give legal standing to these groups, the so-called Proposals of 1705, collapsed in the face of opposition from the civil government and from certain ministers. The clergy in Connecticut succeeded where their colleagues failed, securing in the Saybrook Platform of 1708 a state-recognized system of ministerial consociations. Yet their victory was but partial since many congregations in Connecticut refused to accept the Saybrook Platform.8

The Hampshire Association emerged in the wake of these events. Organized in 1714 and endeavoring to meet monthly, it undertook the same kinds of business that concerned the four or five associations already functioning in eastern Massachusetts. One role such groups expected to assume was the ordination of new ministers and, perforce, a review of each candidate's credentials. Ordination in New England was initially downplayed, even to the point of allowing laymen to perform the function. But the reemergence of a higher sense of clerical office, a process under way as early as the drafting of the Cambridge Platform, was accompanied by a new insistence on ordination as exclusively a function performed by the clergy.9 Hence the expectation that Robert Breck would seek ordination from his Hampshire colleagues should the Springfield church vote to have him as its minister. That his colleagues could refuse this function and, by doing so, prevent the church from exercising its undoubted right of choosing someone for the ministry was implicit in the process. Indeed, precedents existed for refusing to ordain and for looking closely at a candidate's beliefs.1

-- 14 --

These contexts accentuate the decision William Cooper made to raise the cry of usurpation and to pin the label "Presbyterian" on the Hampshire clergy. To do so was to invoke a well-worn rhetorical tradition that dated back to the seventeenth century, and one that lay opponents of the Saybrook Platform had recently revived. But as some of his contemporaries knew, Cooper was no populist at heart. The central task awaiting Edwards was to defuse the rhetoric of Breck's friends. He did so by exposing them as critics of the Cambridge Platform.

The longest section of A Letter to the Author has to do with issues growing out of the Cambridge Platform: on the one hand, the legitimacy of the council that ordained Breck, and on the other the legitimacy of the Hampshire ministers' intervention. Edwards saw the former as turning on the composition of the council. Repeating a line of attack pursued in A Narrative, he insisted that the phrase "neighbour-churches" in the Platform did not sanction bringing ministers from as far away as Boston to a Springfield ceremony.2 And what of Cooper's protestations that the Boston ministers were the true friends of congregational liberties? Here Edwards' tactic was to quote from a short pamphlet, Some Brief Remarks upon a Letter to a Gentleman Relating to the Office of Ruling Elder, published in Boston in 1731. No name appeared on the title page, and no bibliographer has proposed an attribution.3 But Edwards knew that its author was one of the Boston members of the ordaining council. Criticizing the campaign to restore ruling elders, an "office" described in the Platform, the author mocked the very rhetoric Cooper was employing. In one instance the Platform was embraced; in another, it was ridiculed. The exact passage follows as part of A Letter.

If the Cambridge Platform was not really the issue in 1736, what was? We may best explain the intervention of the Boston ministers by regarding the controversy as a local echo of disputes in the early eighteenth century among Nonconformist ministers in England and Presbyterians in Scotland and Ulster. These controversies originated in the demand by certain clergy—usually the more orthodox or evangelical—that their colleagues formally subscribe to the Westminster Confession. Among the London Nonconformist clergy a debate at Salter's Hall in 1719 found some ministers arguing in favor of freedom of

-- 15 --

conscience. Hindsight tells us that the faction speaking on behalf of liberty of conscience, or the right of private judgment, was beginning to break with the doctrines of high Calvinism. The same "liberal" tendency was present in the Church of Scotland, where the issue of "subscription" was disputed over a much longer period and with schism as its lasting consequence. The situation in New England was different in two respects: each side included ministers of undoubted orthodoxy, and subscription was displaced by the ambiguities inhering in associations and their power to review a candidate's credentials. But the outcome was the same, to begin the process of relaxing the accountability of individual ministers to their peers. William Rand, the firmest of Breck's friends in Hampshire and himself becoming doubtful of the orthodox tradition, underlined this outcome in an ordination sermon he preached in late 1735. "Every person," Rand declared, "is to judge for himself in such cases, and not to submit his reason and conscience to any human determinations whatsoever, but to examine whether they be agreeable to the mind of Christ."4

The majority of the ministers in the Hampshire Association were of a different way of thinking. For them the greater danger lay not in "persecution" of dissent, but in allowing false opinions to corrupt the household of God.5 Such was Edwards' frame of mind. Like others in New England he was alarmed by the signs of Arminian opinion at home and abroad. In 1734 he was preaching to his Northampton congregation on the doctrine of justification by faith and insisting in

-- 16 --

these sermons on the Reformed principle of humankind's natural inability. Some years later, in A Faithful Narrative of Surprising Conversions, he recalled how a "great noise…about Arminianism" broke out in Hampshire County in the mid-1730s. Those who shared his sense of alarm were determined to keep a close watch over candidates for the ministry. Thomas Clap had written Stephen Hopkins in April 1735 that "it is of absolute Necessity and Importance, in order to preserve the Purity of Religion, in these dark Times of Error and Declension," that any suspicion of "any thing amiss, in the Principles or Morals, of Candidates for the Ministry," be aired to the fullest. Edwards agreed. In what is doubtless a veiled reference to the Breck affair, his biographer Samuel Hopkins reports him as feeling

it of Importance that Ministers should be very critical in examining Candidates for the Ministry, with respect to their Principles, as well as their religious Dispositions and Morals.…His Opinion was, that an erroneous or unfaithful Minister was likely to do more hurt than good to the Church of Christ; and therefore he could not have any Hand in introducing a Man into the Ministry, unless he appeared sound in the Faith.

"On this account," Hopkins observed, Edwards "met with considerable difficulty and Opposition in some instances."6 Agent though he may have been of his uncle, cousins, and the Hampshire majority in the Breck affair, Edwards was nonetheless a willing participant in the effort to prevent the young man's ordination. It is telling that, thereafter, the two ministers never became friends or were reconciled.

The general task of rebutting Cooper involved Edwards in a host of minor issues and details, most of them already covered in A Narrative. These issues included the role of Elisha Williams in the affair, the interception of the letter he had written to his cousin, the responsibility of the Hampshire ministers for the debacle of Breck's arrest (it seems likely that the plan—possibly instigated by the "dissatisfied" in Springfield church—was for the magistrates to arrest the ordaining council), the behavior of the "man on horseback" who read pro-Breck testimonies to the crowd in Springfield, the privacy of a letter sent to William Williams by Benjamin Colman, Williams' willingness to oppose Breck, the irregularity of the ordaining council, and letting

-- 17 --

Breck choose four of its members. The most crucial matter was undoubtedly the accusation that the Hampshire ministers had never been invited by the Springfield church to intervene. Here Edwards had to reach to make his case, in part by making much of the "neighbour-churches" clause and the apparent irregularity of having Breck himself designate some of the churches that participated in his ordination. It is symptomatic of still-persisting tensions that Edwards raised anew the issue of Breck's orthodoxy, reiterating one more time the charge that he had preached false doctrine.

The significance of the Breck affair must not be exaggerated. Of little importance to Edwards' earliest biographers, the controversy dramatized the weakness of ministerial associations in an ecclesiastical system ill-suited to the task of preserving unanimity among the clergy. A new ethos of liberty of conscience, though but faintly present in New England in the 1730s, would in time become another obstacle to orthodoxy. But this is to anticipate. The war between liberalism and orthodoxy lay in the future. As it was, Robert Breck became friends with his neighbor Stephen Williams and in 1741 was admitted to the Hampshire Association. Not until another decade had passed, and in a wholly different setting, did Edwards raise the issue of qualifications for church membership.

Congregationalism and the Gathered Church

In February 1749, Edwards informed the Northampton congregation that he would admit to full communion only those he judged "in profession, and in the eye of the church's Christian judgment, godly or gracious persons."7 This announcement touched off an emotional and political earthquake. Contemporaries described the church members as "transported into an uncommon Degree of Rage and Madness," and the man who was responsible for this reaction "perceived so great a ferment in the town" that he delayed preaching on the topic.8 Moving quickly and acting almost as one, the church and the town told Edwards bluntly that, unless he recanted, he was betraying his ministry and would be asked to resign. Here is not the place to recount the

-- 18 --

successive councils that met in Northampton or the prolonged negotiations that preceded them, most of which Edwards described in a text that follows.9 The relevant events are these: after telling the church of his decision, Edwards wrote An Humble Inquiry in the spring of 1749; printed books reached Northampton in the fall. In February and March 1750, he explained his thinking in five public lectures. By a bare majority, a council of ministers and lay delegates that met in June concluded that Edwards and his congregation could not be reconciled. Thereupon the church members voted by an overwhelming majority (some two hundred for, twenty opposed) to dismiss him.1 On July 2 he preached his Farewell Sermon.

All this while the townspeople had cast around for someone in the ministry to defend their position in writing. Solomon Williams, the minister of Lebanon, Connecticut, met their needs with The True State of the Question, published in May 1751. Edwards, who by this time was living in Stockbridge, replied in Misrepresentations Corrected (1752). He never spoke out again on the issue, but others did, in an exchange of publications that lasted into the nineteenth century.

The issue that aroused Edwards, the townspeople, and Solomon Williams in the middle of the eighteenth century had been of critical importance to the founders of the New England Way in the seventeenth century and almost as important to Protestant reformers in the century of the Reformation, especially those who came to be known as Puritans in England. Its genesis may be traced to Augustine and his controversy with the Donatists. But though Edwards knew something of these contexts, the one that really mattered to him was much closer in time and place. No less a person than his grandfather Solomon Stoddard, the man whom he succeeded in the ministry of Northampton, was the principal advocate in New England for a broadly inclusive definition of church membership. Point by point, the Humble Inquiry takes up Stoddard's reasoning and rejects it.

But as the townspeople complained, and as Edwards himself acknowledged,

-- 19 --

he had followed "Mr. Stoddard's Way" for most of his ministry and had not given any signs of discomfort with the system. That is, Edwards had been a Stoddardean by choice. To understand where he started from and, in light of this position, where he ended up, we must therefore return to Stoddard and the evolution of his thinking about the church. Here at once the whole of the New England Way—both in theory, as elaborated in treatises of the early decades and in the Cambridge Platform, and in practice, as modified over time and by the Synod of 1662–becomes relevant. So do certain debates among English Nonconformists, for Edwards and Stoddard were responding to men like Thomas Blake and Richard Baxter as well as to writers within the New England tradition.

In general Puritans expected the church to consist of a body of true Christians. From the very outset of the Puritan movement in England, its leaders struggled to transform the state church into such a body. For them the existing Church of England was a "mixt multitude," an ungainly mass of people, many of whom ignored the "duties" of the moral life. "Discipline" would be the means of shaping this mixt multitude into a more vital and observant Christian community. Behind this program lay a complex symbolism taken from the Bible. As the historian John Coolidge has observed, Elizabethan Puritans drew on Paul's letter to the Ephesians in describing the church as a house of which Christ was the cornerstone. This symbolism enforced an exclusiveness: not everyone, but only those who participated in the life of the risen Christ and became "vital" or "living" stones, could be members of this "temple."2

The English reformers derived this vision of the church not only from Scripture, but also from Martin Bucer, John Calvin, and other leaders of the Rhineland reformation. Speaking of Calvin's understanding of the church, Ernst Troeltsch has remarked that "the Church is not merely an organ of salvation which provides the objective means of grace.…It ought rather at the same time to provide the means of sanctification; it ought to prove itself effective in the Christianizing of the community, by placing the whole range of life under the control of Christian regulations and Christian purposes." To make effective the element of sanctification, or the routinized pursuit of holiness, the Genevan reformer set up a system for reviewing

-- 20 --

the moral conduct of persons who wished to participate in the sacrament of the Lord's Supper; under his system, the sacrament became "the occasion for the official inspection and purification of the congregation." He also emphasized the procedures of admonition and excommunication (that is, processes for rebuking and excluding the unworthy) as means of ridding the church of persons who seemed immoral or profane. Yet Calvin did not make personal or collective holiness a mark of the true church. Critical of the Anabaptists for their puristic zeal in seeking "a church besmirched with no blemish," he held fast to the Augustinian distinction between visible and invisible. Here on earth the church is unable to differentiate the elect from the reprobate; here "the church is at the same time mingled of good men and bad," as Christ indicated in the parable of the wheat and tares (Matthew 13:24-26). Imagining the visible church as a household in which believers progressed in faith, Calvin proposed that the preaching of the Word and the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper had an objective status as means of grace to everyone. The Calvinist understanding of the church thus incorporated two distinctly different elements: the visible church as objectively constituted and comprehensive and as a special kind of ethical community.3

The clergy who became Puritans in Elizabethan England affirmed both parts of Calvin's doctrine, the church as means of grace to everyone and the church as disciplined community. But in the distinctive setting of a hierarchical and partially reformed Church of England, they gravitated toward a de facto voluntarism that worked against an inclusive, corporatist conception of the church. So it happened that zealous parish clergy fashioned special covenants between themselves and the "godly" who wished to withdraw from the mixt multitude. The many varieties of such local arrangements all had the same consequence,4 namely, to increase the distance between the Reformed tradition and English Puritanism on the nature of the church. That distance is evident as early as the 1570s, when Thomas Cartwright, a

-- 21 --

leading reformer, argued that although the state may require adults to attend the preaching of the Word, church membership itself was distinct from attendance and depended on demonstrating "profit" to a group of ministers. Those who passed this scrutiny would become regular members; those who failed could be dismissed. Cartwright also felt that no one should receive baptism or attend the Lord's Supper without satisfying the prior conditions of Christian knowledge and godly behavior. Meanwhile his contemporary John Field was describing the church as "a company or congregatione of the faythfull called and gathered out of the worlde by the preachinge of the Gospel." Only when we stop to ask why an English Presbyterian (as Field has traditionally been termed) sounded so much like a Congregationalist of the 1640s do we sense the drift into "voluntary" religion.5

This mixture of traditions—on the one hand, the Reformed system, on the other, the development of voluntarism—was conveyed to New England with the Puritan migration of the 1630s. By the end of that decade, the clergy and the more zealous of the colonists in New England had enhanced and formalized the voluntary aspects of the church. The New England Way began with the principle that the proper mode of church order was a system of congregations, each with an explicit covenant and a ministry elected by the male lay members, who shared the power of the keys to decide cases of church discipline. A more daring decision was to require adult candidates for church membership to describe in public their experience of the work of grace, the premise being that such a test would differentiate "visible saints" from counterfeits. In practice as in theory, the test of a "relation" worked to exclude a certain number of the colonists from church membership.6

We may regard the church history of New England for the next hundred years as a whole. During this century the clergy looked for ways of restoring a broader, more inclusive membership even as they continued to defend the principles of Congregationalism. Pressure for change had several sources. The Puritan community in England reacted at once, and for the most part critically, to the ecclesiology that

-- 22 --

emerged in New England. English clergy warned that the insistence on explicit covenants implied that the Church of England was not a true church and that the test of a relation brought the colonists to the verge of abolishing the distinction between the visible and invisible churches. The Presbyterian Samuel Hudson was one of many critics in the mid-seventeenth century who insisted that "visible" was not to be confused with "actual" and who wondered if, in terming members of the church "visible saints," the colonists had merged these two categories.7 Once the Civil War broke out in England and thrust Puritans into power, middle-of-the-road reformers were angered by the fact that Independents (some of whom had returned from New England) were organizing gathered congregations in their very midst. Writing in 1657, the minister Richard Baxter denounced the Independents who proposed that "it is only the Regenerate and such as shall be saved, that are to be added to the Church." In response, Baxter reasoned that "then we must admit none, becaus we know not one man to bee such." No group here on earth, Baxter affirmed, could differentiate the true saint from the apparent saint.8

Responsive to such critics, the New England clergy also had to deal with the divergence between practice and principle. From the side of practice the anomalies were mounting—of people left outside the gathered church and of baptized children who, once they reached adulthood, were having children of their own. What was the status of these baptized children turned adult? And did their children somehow come within the covenant? From the side of principle a barrage of English publications continually reminded the framers of the Cambridge Platform that the church was a means of grace to everyone. Baptists in England and New England, though few in number, spoke against this doctrine.

When the New England clergy met in Cambridge in 1646 and 1648 to draft a platform, they were seeking some way of acknowledging their critics but also of reaffirming the ideal of gathered congregations limited to godly people. Taking heed of contradictory signals, the Cambridge synod embarked on a process of accommodation that was resumed at the synods of 1662 and 1679. Debate among the clergy was not confined to these meetings. The Synod of 1662 and its decision

-- 23 --

to affirm the halfway covenant prompted a cluster of publications.9 So did the emergence of Baptists, who in 1665 organized a congregation linking Charlestown and Boston. At the end of the century the founding of the Brattle Street Church, which did without a covenant and laid aside the requirement of a relation of spiritual experience, stirred up further controversy. Certain clergy played a leading role in articulating a compromise that reaffirmed the "first principles" of New England while acknowledging important changes. One such person was Jonathan Mitchel, minister of the Cambridge church. The two Mathers, Increase and Cotton, took up where he left off.

The affirmations that emerged from synods and statements by these clergy were as follows:

1. Describing, in the Cambridge Platform, the matter of the visible gathered church as "Saints by calling" and insisting that prospective members "ought to be examined & tryed first," the framers of the Platform asserted that "a personall & publick confession, & declaring of Gods manner of working upon the soul, is both lawfull, expedient, & usefull." This language meant that gathered churches were entitled to ask candidates for membership to make a relation of the work of grace, or conversion, as well as offering a "confession" of doctrinal knowledge. Presumably, no one could enter as an adult without having experienced the work of grace.1

2. The Platform emphasized, however, that "the weakest measure of faith is to be accepted in those that desire to be admitted into the church: becaus weak christians if sincere, have the substance of that faith, repentance & holiness which is required…& such have most need of the ordinances for their confirmation & growth in grace." In the literature leading up to the Platform and in its aftermath, the ministers declared that in scrutinizing candidates they exercised the "judgment of charity," a phrase that enabled them to acknowledge uncertainty and to accept the appearance of faith. In the same breath the ministers acknowledged that their congregations included many who were "hypocrites."2 Indeed, in a passage others after him sometimes quoted, John Cotton averred that "it were better to admit diverse

-- 24 --

Hypocrites then to keep out one sincere Child of God from coming into the Church."3

3. The children of those admitted as members were to be included in the church covenant and baptized.4

4. Once these children became adults (or in the language of the time, children of the church), they were to remain as members even though they failed to make a relation of spiritual experience.

5. In turn the children of these members were considered to be in covenant and therefore entitled to its benefits, the sacrament of baptism and the "watch" of the church.

Propositions 4 and 5 were controversial. Defending them, Jonathan Mitchel, Richard Mather, Thomas Shepard (1605–49), and his son Thomas Shepard II (1635–77) reasoned that the scope of baptism under the New Testament duplicated the scope of circumcision under the Old. According to their reading of the Old Testament, an entire nation participated in the covenant even though "many of them were not sincerely godly" or "were never of the invisible church."5 Proposing that baptism be as generously inclusive and that it not be based on prior evidence of repentance and faith, these clergy differentiated two kinds of holiness and two kinds of covenants: real holiness and the covenant of grace, on the one hand, and "federal holiness" and the "external covenant" on the other. The basis of federal- or covenant-holiness was, quite simply, the "covenant-relation" into which children entered through their parents. In effect, the ministers were arguing that God manifested the covenant of grace through an "external dispensation," the visible church and its covenant. To be sure, the parents who claimed the privilege of the external covenant for their children had to meet certain conditions: they must "understand the doctrine of Faith, publickly profess their assent thereto," and not be "scandalous

-- 25 --

in life." By meeting these conditions the parents established themselves as "Confederate visible Believers, though but in the lowest degree such."6 As here, so in succeeding passages the language of the Synod of 1662 was full of equivocations: the "parents…are confederate visible Believers, at least in some degree"; "this sort of persons showing nothing to the contrary, are in charity…visible Believers"; they had "Faith and Repentance" simply by virtue of being in the church, and as long as "they do not reject it." The reasoning was very nearly tautological: the external covenant produced benefits that qualified the halfway members and their children to remain within it.7

In response to the Baptists, who rejected the typological fusion of Old Testament and New, the clergy turned increasingly to another argument, that children of the church, although members only in external covenant, were more likely to be saved than if they remained unbaptized. To exclude them was to hasten their degeneracy. To include them was to make use of the church as a "school" or "garden" in which they were nurtured. Granting that all of those in outward covenant were not inwardly elect, Thomas Shepard the elder insisted nonetheless that those within this covenant were a "special people unto God":

He will prune, and cutt, and dresse, and water them…; nay…he will take away this refuseing heart from among them indefinitely, so that though every one cannot assure himself, that he will do it particularly for this or that person, yet every one, through this promise, may hope, and pray for the communication of this grace, and so feel it in time.8

In yet another version of this argument, the ministers emphasized the many good qualities and advantages of children of the church—"the good Effects of a pious Education," the time they spent "under the Means of Grace," their "Profession of the Faith, not contradicted by any thing scandalous in their Life." Essentially, this was to reason that membership within the external covenant was a positive event, with benefits to follow; to those of this status, Increase Mather declared, God will "infallibly make them the offer of his covenant parenthetical." This way of thinking culminated in Mather's assertion that God had

-- 26 --

"cast the line of Election" so that it ran in part "through the loyns of godly parents."9

6. But if the "seed" of the covenant could receive baptism and be subject to church discipline, the only persons admitted to the sacrament of the Lord's Supper were members in "full communion," or those who qualified in the old way. The ideal of purity was transposed, therefore, to this sacrament.

Since it was access to this sacrament that was ostensibly the issue in the Northampton controversy, it is important to review how the clergy who preceded Jonathan Edwards understood the Lord's Supper, and on what grounds they restricted full church membership to "saints by calling."

The meaning of the Lord's Supper within the Congregational tradition was consequent upon the doctrines of the church covenant and the covenant of grace. The sacrament had little independent meaning of its own, functioning instead as a sign or seal of prior actions—that is, of entering into each of these two covenants. In this regard the sacramental theology of the colonists allowed for little or no emphasis on the elements themselves or for the real presence.1 Yet in writing about the sacrament in manuals of devotion, English reformers in the first half of the seventeenth century had more to say about spiritual preparation for the Lord's Supper than about any other aspect of personal and collective religion. At great length, these reformers made the point that the Lord's Supper was only for persons who, beforehand, engaged in stringent self-examination and repentance for sin. These writers warned that communicants must feel themselves to be truly of the faithful. The more elemental message was that no one could come to the Lord's Supper who was spiritually unclean. In support of this taboo, these clergy pointed to 1 Corinthians 11:27-29:

Wherefore whosoever shall eat this bread, and drink this cup of the Lord, unworthily, shall be guilty of the body and blood of the Lord. But let a man examine himself, and so let him eat of that bread, and drink of that cup. For he that eateth and drinketh unworthily,

-- 27 --

eateth and drinketh damnation to himself, not discerning the Lord's body.

Citing these verses time and again, English writers assumed that their horrific message would protect the Lord's Supper from the mixt multitude that filled the Church of England. Who would dare participate except those assured of being spiritually renewed?2

The ministers who came to New England reiterated this understanding of the sacrament despite having put in place other means of excluding the unworthy from church membership. But in later years, as they moved toward more inclusive baptism, the sanctity of the Lord's Table took on a fresh importance. To this sacrament, they insisted, only those could come who knew the experience of the work of grace, had "actual" knowledge of Christ, and manifested their inward state in the "fruits" of the Spirit. The men and women who met these conditions were "full" church members, as contrasted with the children of the church who were limited to the sacrament of baptism. The Synod of 1662, which reaffirmed the legitimacy of these children, went on declare that the Lord's Supper remained closed to all but those who were visible saints.3

The thinking of Jonathan Mitchel of Cambridge, chief author of this "middle way" between "extreames," is revealed in a document that Cotton Mather included in his biography of Mitchel. Having brought himself to favor the halfway covenant, Mitchel felt it all the more important "to plead for the Holiness" of the gathered church "by Requiring further Degrees of Preparation, in those that they received unto the Supper of the Lord." Citing 1 Corinthians 11:28-29, he argued that "Worthy Receivers" must "Examine themselves, and Discern the Lord's Body." Though he used the word "charity" four times in a single sentence, the thrust of the conditions he outlined was that visible saints must in some manner manifest "a Work of grace." Echoing a long exegetical tradition, he cited the three thousand Peter baptized, on the condition of their "conversion" (Acts 2). And he warned of the "Formality and Deadness" that would follow should the churches admit persons to the Lord's Supper who had "only Doctrinal Knowledge, and Outward Blamelessness."4

Mitchel consciously positioned himself between strictness and laxity. So too he sought a compromise formation that encompassed both "grace" and "holiness" in the church—the former via a more generous policy of baptism, the latter via restricting the Lord's Supper to visible saints. Compromise imposed itself not only upon Mitchel but upon most of his fellow clergy. Valuing the purity of the gathered church and worried lest the profane enter it in such numbers as to gain control of church discipline, they also realized that to exclude everyone who could not meet the test of a relation would drastically diminish the size of their congregations.5 Moreover, it would mean rejecting as not adequately Christian thousands of people who seemed decently behaved and who knew their catechisms. So it happened that, by the close of the seventeenth century, most of the clergy rationalized their situation.

Increase and Cotton Mather reaffirmed the legitimacy of this middle way in a series of books they wrote in response to its critics, chief among them Solomon Stoddard.6 The arguments they used were much the same as those Mitchel had employed. The two Mathers were already on record as defenders of the halfway covenant. Yet they continued to insist that the visible church was a particular gathered body consisting of "Saints and true Believers" and that "none but such as are (so far as men exercising a due Charity are able to judge) Sincere Converts, or True Believers on Christ, ought to be received into the Churches." Citing the eunuch that Philip baptized after having heard him affirm, "I believe that Jesus Christ is the Son of God" (Acts 8:37), Increase reasoned that the church was entitled to ask its members "whether they have truly Repented of their Sins, and whether they truly Believe on Christ" and to restrict the Lord's Supper to those who, according to this testimony, were "Regenerate persons."7

Increase retreated from this argument the instant he had made it. Calling on the churches to use "tenderness and Charity," he conceded

-- 29 --

(quoting John Cotton) that "it were better…to admit diverse Hypocrites then to keep out one Sincere Child of God" and spoke of the "Unregenerate" as qualifying for the sacrament because in "Outward appearance" they resembled the saints. Taking up the objection that his view of the church required ordinary Christians to achieve assurance of salvation, which they rarely did, he answered that assurance was not really necessary before someone ventured to the Lord's Table. Candidates needed only "a probable hope of Regeneration," a hope would-be saints could strengthen by participating in the sacrament. Mather's middle way allowed him to sanction admitting "the weakest Christians" so long as they were "Sincere." Notwithstanding these many qualifications, the two Mathers continued to insist that "Profane Persons" must not be allowed into the church, at least not as a matter of explicit policy.8

The two Mathers were aware that English Nonconformists had continued to debate these same issues. In general, English Presbyterians remained unsympathetic to the concept of the gathered church and therefore to any emphasis on limiting membership to the holy. Presbyterians viewed the church as "catholic visible," the church covenant as unnecessary, and membership as requiring only knowledge of doctrine and "moral sincerity." An important advocate for this point of view was Thomas Blake (1597?–1657), minister of Tarnworth, Staffordshire, who affiliated with the Presbyterian party during the period of the English Civil War. He entered the lists against two groups, the Baptists for denying infant baptism and any and all others (including English Independents and New England Congregationalists) who regarded the church covenant as restricted to the elect. Blake argued at great length in Vindiciæ Fœderis; or, A Treatise of the Covenant that since the covenant was unchanged in its scope from the Jewish to the Christian dispensation, the church under the New Testament was no less comprehensive than under the Old, when the "seed" of members were included in the covenant and the "unregenerate" were also welcome. Differentiating the external covenant from the internal, he insisted that the former encompassed unregenerate people. For him it did not matter that the church on earth contained many who were impure. The alternative, he reasoned, was complete paralysis: since no

-- 30 --

one except Christ knew who was truly regenerate, a church that adopted such a rule could not admit or baptize anyone. Instead of imposing the conditions of faith and repentance prior to membership or baptism, Blake regarded them as consequent to these steps. Turning to the Bible, he interpreted the baptism of the three thousand as occurring without any knowledge on the part of Peter of repentance and faith. And these conditions did not figure in Philip's baptism of the eunuch or in the parables of the wedding feast (Matthew 22:2) and the wheat and tares. Blake made a further distinction between "dogmaticall" or "historical faith," which was the most that any church could require, and "justifying faith," which was known only to God. From his point of view the visible saint was someone quite likely to be unregenerate, though this same person had historical faith and wanted church membership for his or her seed.9

Richard Baxter, parish minister of Kidderminster and a man who refused to affiliate with any ecclesiastical party, did not fully agree. Like his near-contemporary Jonathan Mitchel, Baxter wanted a middle way between extremes. Though he favored limiting the church to "Regenerate Believers," he detested the Independents who unchurched so many English people and applied to their policy of restricted baptism a line of reasoning similar to Blake's: if in saying that "onely the Regenerate…are to be added to the Church…they mean onely such as are Really sanctified and sincere, or elect, then wee must admit none, becaus wee know not one man to bee such." When it came to admission to the Lord's Supper, Baxter shifted ground, however, and called for limiting that sacrament to regenerate believers. The trick was to define regenerate believers in a way that took account of sincerity and a "profession of true Christian saving faith," but that also acknowledged the likelihood of hypocrisy and the near-impossibility of achieving "full Assurance." Baxter resolved this problem by proposing that ministers and congregations accept the evidence of "Profession" and not seek to know what lies beyond a verbal statement.1

This assertion carried Baxter to the edge of a linguistic maze, the relation between a sign and what it signified. That relation had to concern everyone who relied on the evidence of a verbal profession or behavior or both in deciding whether a candidate for membership was

-- 31 --

a visible saint. What did such outward actions signify of the inward heart or self? The ministers who asked these questions were quite aware that hypocrites could feign the real thing. Experience had taught them that verbal statements were deceptive, for candidates could repeat formulas learned from others. Moreover, a theological argument intruded: no one but God could know for sure "the heart of the Professor." For Baxter, the only way around these difficulties was to insist that in most cases, if not invariably, outward signs were sufficient evidence of inward circumstances:

If they did by word of mouth say, that they believe with a saving Faith, these words are but signs of their minds; and whether counterfeit or not, the Church cannot tell.…Therefore the Church must not take the external Sacrament for a Nullity, every time a mans secret Intentions agree not with his signal Profession; for then we should not know whether ever we baptize any one.

Ever seeking compromise formations, Baxter asserted over and over again three points: we must demand evidence of grace; in doing so we are limited to signs; and we must regard signs, however outward, as signifying what they seem to be about.2

The debate between Baxter and Blake was representative of scores of such exchanges in the seventeenth century. In the course of these debates and discussions on both sides of the Atlantic the term "visible saint" became increasingly elastic. The same term could mean that visible saints (1) approximated being real saints or (2) were "morally sincere," though perhaps without saving grace, or (3) were often hypocrites even though the requirements for becoming a church member included the test of a relation. Logical and verbal precision foundered on the inevitable necessity of making distinctions, the fundamental impasse being the difference between visible and invisible, or the external and internal covenants. The drift of thinking in Nonconformist and New England circles was undoubtedly to grant more credence to a multitude of outward signs, some of them possibly denoting sanctification, without attempting to specify their relation to the inward workings of the Holy Spirit. The drift in practice was undoubtedly to broaden access to baptism while attempting to reserve the Lord's Supper for persons who (by whatever standard or evidence) were regarded as regenerate.

-- 32 --

Well into the eighteenth century most of the New England clergy remained of several moods about the church. The interplay of these moods is revealed in a document originating in the Northampton controversy, a letter by Peter Clark, minister of Salem Village, to the laity of Northampton turning down their request that he answer the Humble Inquiry. Clark had been tempted to do so, for Edwards' scheme (which he knew about from rumors) seemed to "take away that commonly received and well-grounded distinction of the church into visible and invisible." It was "absurd" for anyone to suppose that he or she could discern "the state of men's hearts," and in any case the instituted church was designed to "consist of a mixture of good and bad, sound and unsound Christians, till the great day of discrimination." Yet Clark believed that members of the visible church should possess "visible saintship," and, in contrast to Solomon Stoddard, he required a "gospel profession" of those who wished to participate in the Lord's Supper. In the same breath he spoke as though he were Blake or Baxter, calling on his fellow ministers to accept "no higher evidence…of sincerity than such a profession" and warning that many who passed as visible saints "are really and in heart unconverted."3 Moving first in one direction, then in another, Clark attempted to achieve a balance of conflicting values. In this regard he was representative of the Congregational tradition as it stabilized in the closing decades of the seventeenth century.

The voice of those who defended this tradition was often charged with equivocation, for they worried that formality and declension may have begun with their very decision to loosen the boundaries of the church covenant.4 Privately, a handful of ministers continued to rhapsodize about the pure church.5 But most of the clergy realized that the new system was a means of reconciling two contrary principles: a gathered church made up of living stones transformed by grace and the church as ordained means of grace (a school) that included hypocrites along with weak and doubtful Christians. We can characterize them as reluctant supporters of the gathered church-cum-halfway covenant: it was nothing glorious, but the alternatives were worse.

-- 33 --

During these same decades of controversy and compromise, the clergy in England and New England were occupied with describing the work of grace, or how it was that election became "actual" in the lives of the saints. It is a truism (which happens to be true) that, from William Perkins onward, one wing of English Protestantism adopted a pietistic or "experimental" understanding of the doctrine of predestination. This way of thinking flowed from the premise that the saints would realize their election via certain forms of spiritual experiences. The basic model involved a three-stage process. The initial experience was achieving a "true sight of sin," or a recognition of one's utter worthlessness in the sight of God. This stage was commonly described as being accomplished by (or under) the "law." Then came a stage of affective response to Christ, a reaching out in faith to take hold of the promise of salvation he offered the repentant. Thereafter, the work of grace consisted of "evangelical" obedience to the law, or a heartfelt commitment to achieving holiness. The process of sanctification (to use the formal name for it) entailed a never-ending struggle against sin. Even as the saint progressed toward the eternal kingdom, he or she repeatedly experienced the cycle of repentance, cleansing, and renewal.

As the history of evangelical Calvinism amply displays, this model of the spiritual life was paradoxical in one key respect. The ministers who taught it, and the men and women who sought to live by its prescriptions, assumed that the saint was differentiated from the hypocrite or reprobate. The line between the two was absolute, and a veritable mountain of sermons and devotional manuals informed the reader (or listener) how to find out in which camp he or she stood—books that signaled in their very titles the radical difference between The Sincere Convert or The Real Christian and the hypocrite. Yet in practice, and even in theory, the difference became blurred. Evangelical ministers complained that people who merely pretended to be Christians were often taken for the real thing. Moreover, every theorist conceded that election was a mystery that would be resolved only at the end of time. Here on earth, as contrasted with the kingdom of God, the separation of the precious and the vile could never be complete or perfect.

The paradox of knowing and not knowing had a further dimension. Lay men and women expected to achieve assurance of salvation but instead were "often put to sad doubts of their own Estate." The sources suggest that such "weak believers," or those who failed to achieve "peace" and "comfort," became a major problem for the clergy. In

-- 34 --

response, the ministers acknowledged that assurance could wax and wane and emphasized the possibility of gaining it from sanctification, or the practice of godly duties. This evidence was admittedly a sign of something else; it was consequent upon the work of grace and required reasoning along the lines of the "practical syllogism"—"he that repenteth and believeth the Gospel shall be saved; But I repent and believe the Gospel; Therefore I shall be saved"—that some theologians, including Calvin, found distasteful.6 The problem of assurance thus opened up the possibility that the saints were not easily differentiated from hypocrites if the difference between them lay in outward signs or statements, for these could be convincingly imitated.

The nexus between church membership and spiritual experience added yet another layer of complications. That nexus was forged in New England in the 1630s when most of the clergy agreed to require candidates for church membership to make a relation of spiritual experience—in effect, to describe the work of grace and how it had transformed them into saints. Almost immediately, however, the ministers had to backtrack. We have traced how their commitment to infant baptism and the federal covenant forced them to acknowledge that church members were of two different kinds, some being of the elect and within the covenant of grace and others merely within the federal covenant. They arrived at more or less the same point via a different road, their critique of a radical spiritism, the "antinomianism" that emerged suddenly in Massachusetts in 1636 and 1637 and again in England in the 1640s. The antinomians grounded assurance of salvation on the direct "witness of the Holy Spirit," rejected the practical syllogism as tantamount to works-righteousness, and argued that the saints could infallibly differentiate the true believer from the hypocrite.7 Opposing this purism, the ministers declared that the Holy Spirit worked through means or an objective order, insisted that the way to grace and assurance of salvation lay ordinarily through such means as the sacraments of the church and the duties of the Christian life, reasoned that everyone, elect or not, was summoned to make use of these means, and represented sanctification as providing reliable evidence of justification. No minister said that attendance on the

-- 35 --

means guaranteed the reception of saving grace. But in contrast to antinomianism their way of thinking preserved a broad understanding of church membership and sustained the moral obligations of the Christian life as incumbent on everyone.8

Here then was the ultimate version of the paradox: on the one hand the doctrine of election and its practical corollary, that Christians underwent a work of grace which enabled them to know if they were saved; on the other, the imperative of baptism, entering into the disciplinary watch of the church and relying on a routinized holiness for assurance of salvation. The outcome was a kind of double speak among the clergy.9 From the generation of Thomas Shepard and John Cotton to that of Jonathan Edwards and Solomon Williams, they summoned sinners and hypocrites to undergo conversion, lamented formality, and represented the church as the home of those who became living stones. In the same breath they extolled the benefits of duties, emphasized objective or external signs, and acknowledged that the church must always contain hypocrites who passed as visible saints.

This mixture of messages was vulnerable to criticism, and it left even those who made use of it feeling uneasy. Jonathan Edwards inherited this uneasiness, just as he also inherited the long-standing uneasiness about the halfway covenant. He had also to deal with antinomianism—not the 1630s variety but that which emerged out of the Great Awakening. Two other structures of thinking affected him. He had to reckon with popular religion, or the ways in which the laity of his congregation understood the nature of the church and its sacraments. What may have weighed as much upon him was the thinking of his predecessor in Northampton, Solomon Stoddard. Stoddard labored throughout his ministry to overcome the contradiction between piety and institutional practice. His grandson would seek to overcome these same contradictions, but by a different route.

Lay and Clerical Religion

Lay men and women in New England approached church membership and the two sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper in their own fashion, though how they acted and felt was substantially influenced

-- 36 --

by the ministers.1 In those towns of which we have detailed knowledge of church membership, the main pattern seems to be that many people in the first generation became members—not at once when small groups of males participated in the founding of congregations, but soon thereafter.2 In some communities, however, substantial numbers either did not come into the church or were kept from having their children baptized. By the third quarter of the century a series of compromises or shifts in policy—whether local variations on the halfway covenant or that policy itself—worked to make church membership more inclusive.3 But as more people gained access to baptism, the number of members in "full communion" steadily decreased. Any generalization is hazardous, but at the time of the Great Awakening the ratio of baptized (or halfway) members to full members encompassed a range that, at one extreme, approached ten to one.4 Another relevant pattern is the time of life that halfway members chose to renew their covenants or that persons outside the church asked for membership or that those in halfway membership advanced to full. In general these moments coincided with forming a family and beginning to have children. The layman Samuel Sewall was typical in waiting to apply for full membership (he was already in covenant as a child of the church) until he had married and his wife was expecting.5

-- 37 --

These rhythms of affiliation and participation signify that lay people understood the two sacraments quite differently. Parents valued baptism for their children. They accepted the ministers' reasoning (or the ministers accepted theirs) that children within the covenant were better off because of being less threatened by the devil, more likely to obtain saving grace, and more apt to survive. Family preservation, the fundamental goal of the property inheritance system, was equally the goal of those who brought their newborn children to the church almost immediately. These people thought of the church as a place of nurture, a source of preservation and protection for the family. Yet the church was also a place where protection was intermingled with judgment and danger. Lay men and women regarded the Lord's Supper as encircled with this double message. Accordingly, they reasoned that they should not approach the sacrament unless they were assured of God's love for them.6 On the eve of the Great Awakening, a minister confided in his diary that "the people…want to know they shall be sure they believe, that they love God, that they are in the right way."7 Lacking such assurance, and too scrupulous to feign conversion,8 such people harkened to the powerful warning of 1 Corinthians 11:27-28. "They have drunk in an opinion, that none but Converted Persons should come to that Ordinance, and so they neglect it," another minister observed in 1707.9 He might have added that most of these people continued to value the ideal of the gathered church, though not at the expense of foregoing infant baptism.

For the most part, ministers in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century New England were unable to alter these patterns of behavior and the values from which they emerged. Year after year, decade after decade,

-- 38 --

the ministers argued with their congregations and complained out loud about these attitudes. "The so general a neglect parenthetical…hath been both a wonder, and grief of heart to me, almost ever since I have been in the Ministry," Benjamin Wadsworth of Boston wrote in 1724.1 It was not for want of trying that Wadsworth and others like him failed. Two strategies became common in the early decades of the eighteenth century, a milder, more generous reading of 1 Corinthians 11 and the argument that abstaining from the sacrament was more dangerous than partaking of it.2 Yet the impasse lingered, for lay people continued to differentiate between baptism and the Lord's Supper. To be sure, the Great Awakening swept into full membership many who had hesitated for want of adequate assurance. But as was often noted in reports of local revivals, other people, "though brought to very hopeful dispositions…were through fear and darkness kept from entering into full communion."3 Nothing seemed to alter this reluctance—not the revival or the encouraging gestures of the clergy and, as we will see, not even Stoddardeanism.

The behavior of lay people should make us wary of Sereno Dwight's hypothesis that in the decades after 1662 the churches were filling up with lax Christians. True, the structure of the gathered church was altered in response to the deeply felt need among lay men and women for access to baptism. Yet these same lay members continued to manifest a scrupulosity about coming to the Lord's Supper. They were like the ministers in bringing to the church a mixture of values. In their own fashion, they resolved the contradiction between scrupulosity and inclusiveness by differentiating between baptism and the Lord's Supper. This way of thinking and these patterns do not warrant being termed declension. Nor is this an epithet that applies to Solomon Stoddard and Stoddardeanism.

Solomon Stoddard and Stoddardeanism

Solomon Stoddard was the second minister in Northampton. Three years prior to his ordination in 1672, the congregation had voted to adopt the halfway covenant, though the first such members were admitted only after he began his ministry. Above all else, Stoddard was to

-- 39 --

prove himself an evangelical preacher who regarded stringent preaching inversion as the one sure way of overcoming the formality that seemed to characterize so many of the colonists.4

Yet in his parish, as in so many others, the baptized adults were reluctant to advance to the Lord's Table. In this situation lay the origins of Stoddardeanism. Worrying over "his responsibilities to the unconverted in Northampton" and recognizing that "the half-way compromise had failed to meet their needs, he began to speak…for what he believed was a more workable solution."5 Thinking further about lay religion, Stoddard came to feel that unnecessary scruples about assurance of salvation stood in the way of attendance at the Lord's Supper. He traced this scrupulosity to the requirement of a relation of the work of grace, a requirement, he declared in 1690, that "discourages many good Christians from seeking Full Communion." Moreover, he shared the perception of his fellow clergy that the prospect of participating in the Lord's Supper was torment to these people because they worried so intensely about 1 Corinthians 11:27-29. "This Scripture," he lamented in An Appeal to the Learned, "being a stumbling-block to many, it is meet it should be more fully considered." More generally, he understood that lay people were caught in the logical trap of expecting that they should possess a "certain knowledge of their spiritual state," even though this very knowledge lay beyond their reach. He started, therefore, from a pastoral situation, the scruples of his parishioners concerning a requirement (or an ethos) that "scared them out of Religion."6

Unrelentingly skeptical that the visible church could differentiate true saints from hypocrites, Stoddard called on the New England churches to eliminate regeneration, however understood or measured, as the threshold for admission to the sacraments. He knew that other ministers hoped to break the impasse by telling their congregations that the "smallest measure" or "probable hope" of saving faith would qualify them for coming to the Lord's Supper. The same ministers liked to argue that uncertainty of assurance was a sign of spiritual

-- 40 --

health.7 But from Stoddard's point of view it was false encouragement to tell people that a little bit of faith would do. Such advice got in the way of conversion.8 As the historian E. Brooks Holifield was perhaps the first to realize, Stoddard was "criticizing the laxity, as well as the rigor, of prevailing New England practices."9 Rather than welcome a weak degree of faith, as the Mathers did, he reasoned it was better to do away with the distinction between baptism and the Lord's Supper and allow everyone admitted to the first to participate in the second, whereupon the full message of evangelism could come home to them.

Stoddard's was a simple remedy to a messy theological and pastoral situation. He reasoned that the church contained but one kind of visible saint, that is, someone admitted to every duty, including both of the sacraments. Defending this conception of visible saint, Stoddard insisted that the church was unable to differentiate true saints from hypocrites; what "certain Rule," he asked, was there to use in making this distinction? Granted, congregations should exclude heathens and the profane and require moral sincerity of their members. But the church could go no further. Visible saints were simply those who "make a Solemn Profession of Faith, & Repentance, & are of Godly Conversation, having Knowledge to Examine themselves, & discern the Lords Body." If he reiterated familiar words, their meaning was different. For Stoddard, a "Profession of Faith" involved "Historical," not "Saving Faith" and was unrelated to the work of grace or assurance of salvation. The "visible" in "visible saints" was similarly disconnected from "Sanctifying grace," as was the term "sincere." It was important, Stoddard argued, not to exclude the "Unregenerate" so long as there was any hope of their being saints. He acknowledged, therefore, that many whom he was willing to term visible saints were unconverted or hypocrites.1

Stoddard presented some of these ideas at the Synod of 1679 and repeated them in sermons he delivered in Northampton in the 1680s. Not until the early eighteenth century did he publish his opinions in

-- 41 --

three overlapping manifestos directed against the Mathers: The Doctrine of Instituted Churches (1700), The Inexcusableness of Neglecting the Worship of God (1708), and An Appeal to the Learned (1709). By this time he had a broader case to make against Congregational practices and principles. Critical of the Cambridge Platform, he asked for more authority for the ministry, including greater control over the procedure for admitting members. Historians have sometimes confused Stoddard's political agenda, which in essence was to make the New England church system more presbyterian, with his pastoral mission to ease the tensions that encircled the Lord's Table. But the two were largely unrelated. He did learn much from certain English Presbyterians and Erastians—Thomas Blake, William Prynne, John Humfrey, and John Timson.2 The last three of these writers had advocated a sacramental understanding of the Lord's Supper, attributing to it the power of being a "converting ordinance" or means of grace in its own right. Though not fully acquiescing, Stoddard turned this sacramentalism into a further reason for admitting everyone to the sacrament. Blake played a role in persuading Stoddard that the doctrine of a "particular covenant," another of the root ideas of Congregationalism, was "unscriptural" (the Northampton minister complained that this doctrine was "the reason why many among us are shut out of the Church") and in supporting his conception of the instituted church as a place where the issue of election was irrelevant.3

Like every controversialist of the period, Stoddard invoked Scripture in defending his interpretation of visible saints and their privileges. He argued that the parable of the wedding garment referred to the final judgment and not to the Lord's Supper. Peter could not have inquired beforehand into the qualifications of the three thousand he baptized. Judas had participated in the Passover feast and then in the very first administration of the Lord's Supper; therefore Christ had not been concerned to search the hearts of those who were present. Stoddard denied that the word "examine" in 1 Corinthians 11:28 meant examine for the "truth of grace." The typological symmetry between the Old Testament and the New signified that the church should be national under the gospel.4

-- 42 --

These expectations and policies spread to other congregations, some of which may have set aside or modified the requirement of a relation before Stoddard began to attack the practice. It is often suggested that Stoddard acted as a veritable pope in the Connecticut River valley and that other clergy were swayed by the force of his personality and perhaps by the example of his success as an evangelical minister to fall in with his way of thinking.5 In point of fact few if any clergy appropriated the concept of the Lord's Supper as a converting ordinance. But by the 1720s many of the younger ministers in New England agreed with him that exclusionary policies regarding baptism and the Lord's Supper hindered the progress of evangelism. Like Stoddard, these clergy were Calvinist in their theology, evangelical in their preaching, and inclusive in their understanding of the church.

Central makers of the evangelical and Stoddardean program were the Williams clan: the brothers John Williams of Deerfield and William Williams of Hatfield and their four minister sons. These clergy joined with many others in favoring a more objective, sacerdotal, and inclusive church as policies that assisted their evangelism.6 Facing a host of practical difficulties that included local Baptists and the persistent refusal of parishioners to attend the Lord's Supper, they embraced the argument that the federal or external covenant functioned as a means of grace to all who came within it. They buttressed this argument with the assumption, drawn from Blake and others, that the visible church under the dispensation of the gospel was continuous with the church of the Jews. In the one as in the other the condition of access to the covenant was profession, not repentance or true piety. And in both the seed of the covenant were fully entitled to the privileges of the church.7

William Williams, who made the case for the seed against the Baptists, knew from firsthand pastoral experience that people were avoiding the Lord's Supper. "Yet how many are there parenthetical," he told his

-- 43 --

congregation in 1707, "who live from year to year in the neglect of it? Pretending a want of Preparation, and a fear of polluting and profaning it." He argued the benefits of a different understanding of the church in private reflections he penned in 1693 and again in 1727 in defending the doctrine of infant baptism. It is characteristic of his thinking that he read the parable of the wheat and the tares as signifying that the two were inevitably mingled in the visible church; reiterating the most important point in Stoddardeanism, he concluded from the parable that the visible church had no sure means of discerning the experience of conversion. It was crucial, moreover, that everyone participate in the Lord's Supper and that children be baptized, for the sacraments were means of grace.8

A historiography that equates Stoddardeanism with declension is a historiography that fails utterly to account for the record of the Williamses as evangelical promoters of heart-searching religion and as major actors in the Great Awakening. And that historiography does not acknowledge the origins and consequences of Solomon Stoddard's ecclesiastical program, much less his career as an evangelical Calvinist or the appeal of his thinking to persons who, like the Williamses and many others, were responding to a specific pastoral situation, a spiritual paralysis among lay people troubled by the intertwining of assurance and access to the Lord's Supper. In denying that persons in the church covenant must be "really and Savingly Holy," William Williams and his fellow Stoddardeans never imagined that this understanding of the church compromised their preaching for conversion.9 And although this thought may have occurred to their younger colleague and relation, Jonathan Edwards, he also recognized his uncle's fervent evangelicalism. After William Williams' death in 1741, Edwards preached a commemorative sermon in which he described his uncle as "eminently an evangelical Preacher; evangelical Subjects seemed to be his Delight: CHRIST was the great Subject of his Preaching; and he much insisted on those Things that did nearly concern the Essence and Power of Religion."1 It was left to critics in more recent centuries to draw a different conclusion.

-- 44 --

Jonathan Edwards pursued the program of Stoddard and the Williamses in his Northampton ministry in part because he faced the same pastoral situation as his grandfather. Even after many years of hearing Stoddard invite them to the Lord's Supper, and after voting in 1710 to accept his policies, the church members in Northampton continued to exhibit the pattern of behavior I have termed "popular religion." The great majority of the townspeople had been baptized and therefore came within the church covenant. But some, possibly a significant percentage, resisted acting on Stoddard's interpretation of the Lord's Supper.2 As events proved, they would take stronger action when Edwards challenged the customary mode of access to infant baptism.

The traditional reluctance to participate in the Lord's Supper accounts for a sermon Edwards preached on 1 Corinthians 11:29 in 1733, four years after his grandfather's death. The theme was the familiar one of urging people to hasten to the Lord's Table and not worry overmuch about the injunction to examine themselves. In another sermon from this general period, Edwards complained that his parishioners were "wholly neglecting…the sacrament of the Lord's Supper." Remarking that some of those who stayed away "pretend scruples of conscience," he urged upon the excessively scrupulous their duty to participate in a "positive institution." "Can sin excuse you from attending on a positive institution of Christ?" he asked his congregation.3 These sermons indicate that Edwards found himself in the very same pastoral situation that drove Stoddard to articulate his distinctive understanding of the sacrament. Moreover, Edwards followed closely in the steps of his grandfather when he represented the church and its sacraments as instituted and participation as a duty.

Edwards carried on his search for a satisfying middle way in his private notebooks, or "Miscellanies." As early as 1728, he reflected in the "Miscellanies" that the church should "use all proper means to discriminate true Christians from others." But in the next breath Edwards cautioned against shutting out "multitudes…that are true Christians." The church, he was frank to say, had no good means of differentiating true Christians from those who were not, a situation to which the best response was generosity, or erring on the side of inclusion.

-- 45 --

For this and other reasons he rejected the test of a relation and offered a generous reading of the parable of the wheat and the tares:

We ben't directed to try them by an examination of their particular experiences, of their discoveries, illuminations, and affections; because such trials in multitudes of cases would leave us very uncertain and multitudes would be shut out that are real Christians; Matthew 13:19, "Lest while ye gather up the tares ye root up the wheat with them"—Christ is very careful that wheat is not excluded.

In the same entry, Edwards used Stoddardean language to define the criteria for admitting persons as visible saints: "The gospel rule seems to be, to receive those that make a profession of a hearty believing the truth of the gospel, and a walking in all the ordinances and according to the moral rules of the gospel."4 Several entries later, Edwards defined visible Christians as "Christians in what is visible, or in what appears, or in what is outward." In a succeeding sentence he used the words "outward" and "visible" four times, always in contrast to "really" having "faith and holiness and obedience of heart." A "good report" of someone's outward behavior was sufficient to qualify that person for church membership. Edwards applied similar reasoning to the sacrament of baptism. In yet another entry from this general period he argued that since the apostles baptized "many adult persons that were hypocrites," so we in the visible church may admit infants whose parents are Christians "only visibly and not really."5

The numerous entries in the "Miscellanies" on baptism, covenant, and the church were not always this supportive of the middle way of Congregational tradition. Reflecting in "Miscellanies" no. 462 on visible saints, Edwards reasoned that access to "church privileges" belonged to those who "be Christians really…for only the righteous have any right to the privileges of the church.…And therefore, they must examine and prove themselves, whether or no they believe the gospel with all their hearts, or are heartily convinced of the truth of it." In the same entry he proposed certain tests as "evidential of sincerity" and concluded that "godly" persons who did not "experience a willingness to forsake all sin and…live universally in obedience to God's commands" should keep away from the Lord's Table.

-- 46 --

Mixed in as it is with the warning that "a great number of truly upright ones would be excluded" if "positive" evidence were required this statement does not signal a repudiation of Stoddardeanism. A clearer glimpse of things to come is the argument he made in this entry that a stricter, more exclusionary practice of admission to the Lord's Supper and to baptism would give those not yet in the church (or not adequately so) a fresh incentive for seeking grace and observing the moral law:

And their being denied all privileges…it would put them upon it to come, and being well instructed, that if they do come without forsaking their sins, they will eat and drink judgment to themselves, it will put them upon turning from all their sins and complying with the rules of Christianity and seeking sincerity therein.

The importance of this entry is twofold: not only did Edwards embrace 1 Corinthians 11:28, but he also reversed the logic of Stoddardeanism, for his grandfather had reasoned that evangelism was better advanced by separating access to the sacraments from self-examination and the issue of sincerity.6

Taken as a whole, the relevant entries in the "Miscellanies" reveal a minister who exalted the Lord's Supper and the church and who craved in his parishioners the same intensity of commitment he felt within himself. Almost in the same breath he differentiated the covenant of grace from the church covenant, described the church as a place of nurture where imperfect Christians gathered to benefit from the means of grace, and represented infant baptism as a helpful act.7 The crux of these several-sided reflections was the meaning and significance of "profession," which Edwards treated both as outward and as an act that ought to be sincere.

The Great Awakening put this several-sided understanding of the church to the test. Edwards welcomed the awakenings that swept across New England between 1740 and 1742, for they vindicated orthodox preaching and restored a long-missing vitality to the practice of religion. Yet almost as soon as the revivals began, they precipitated separatist and antinomian tendencies he could not accept. The Great Awakening thus drew from him a series of statements in which he opposed a puristic understanding of the church and reaffirmed the compromises of the congregational system.

-- 47 --

Under the influence of revivalism thousands of lay people, most of them church members, lamented having practiced "external duties" without, until this moment, experiencing the work of grace. Thus transformed, some of these lay people turned their newfound fervor against the halfway covenant, which they criticized for permitting unregenerate persons to enter the church. For the same reason they rejected the concept of the Lord's Supper as a converting ordinance. These New Lights (as they were termed by their opponents) urged that congregations resume the test of a relation. When, as often happened, the minister and other church members rejected this demand, separation sometimes followed. The Strict Congregationalists or Separates who withdrew went on to form new congregations of their own in which they implemented their understanding of the church.8

This action was rooted in discontents left over from the late seventeenth century, when a minority of the laity had opposed the introduction of the halfway covenant. Another source was a strain of spiritism that had been censored as enthusiasm ever since the Antinomian Controversy of 1636–38. In contrast to the orthodox position, the Separates tended to argue that believers achieved "full assurance." "All doubting in a believer," one such group in the mid-1740s declared, "is sinful, being contrary to the command of God, and hurtful to the soul." Full assurance followed from the premise that anyone who experienced the "new birth," or conversion, would recognize the event and know exactly when it occurred. "It is as easy for persons to know when they are converted," one New Light minister allegedly declared, "as it is to know noon daylight from midnight darkness." According to another New Light premise, the Holy Spirit provided true believers with a "key of knowledge" that enabled them to differentiate Christians from hypocrites. A leading minister among the Separates, Ebenezer Frothingham, declared that "a Saint of God having Divine Light shining into the Understanding, and the Love of God…ruling in the Soul, is also to know certainly that such and such Persons are true Converts, or the Saints of God."9

Asserting their ability to recognize the saints and angry at the churches for permitting hypocrites to come within the covenant, the Separates moved to alter the basis of church membership. In the words of one opponent, they argued that "it is the will of God to have a pure

-- 48 --

church on earth, in this sense, that all the converted should be separated from the unconverted." When it came to setting up new congregations of their own, the Separates hoped and expected that they would admit only "real believers in Jesus Christ." The way of differentiating real from false was to depend on the Holy Spirit, albeit a Holy Spirit mediated through the "Fruits of…special sanctifying Grace."1

Edwards repudiated these consequences of the revival. We may judge the extent of his dismay by the fact that he likened the excesses of the New Lights to "the many wild enthusiasts that were in England in the days of Oliver Cromwell; and the followers of Mrs. Hutchinson, in New England," even though, in making this comparison, he reiterated the liberal Boston minister Charles Chauncy's critique of the revivals.2 An important sign of his concern is the extraordinary effort he made in his own parish, and in person elsewhere in New England, to subdue the extremes of the Awakening. Thus he joined forces with his cousin Solomon Williams to preach against enthusiasm in New London, Connecticut, where the itinerant evangelist James Davenport had staged the most dramatic episode of communal repentance to occur in the revivals. Edwards and his allies defended the authority of the ordained ministry, denounced lay people who usurped this authority, and insisted that "the external fruits of holiness" were the only satisfactory evidence of conversion.3

The turmoil of these years led Edwards to address the nature of "true religion" in a series of publications: Distinguishing Marks of a Work of the Spirit of God (1741), Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival (1742), the Treatise Concerning Religious Affections (1746), and the Life of David Brainerd (1749). Together with a letter describing the revival in Northampton that he published in The Christian History, these texts constitute the major body of evidence about his understanding of the Great Awakening and the confusion it engendered about true religion. In these treatises he denounced deism and Arminianism and asserted the validity of Calvinist divinity. In them he set forth his understanding of

-- 49 --

true religion as involving an infusion of supernatural grace that produced a "new sense" of the divine. But the main purpose of these publications was to criticize the New Lights, who had gone astray.4

The great error of the Separates concerned their understanding of conversion and its consequences. The New Lights celebrated a fluency in describing the work of grace that Edwards, never one to trust words alone, found distasteful because these people were not engaging in self-examination or relying on the evidence of "fruits." Any resting of assurance of salvation on the "immediate witness of the Holy Spirit" made him uneasy, as did Antinomian efforts to "cry down works." But he was especially disturbed by the premise that the regenerate could recognize who was truly a saint and who was not. Lamenting New Light outbursts against "unconverted" ministers, he also objected to their complaints against the unconverted who remained church members.5

Edwards rejected out of hand the possibility of purifying gathered churches of the unconverted. In doing so he reiterated some of his grandfather's arguments against the founders of New England Congregationalism. Espousing a policy of charity and insisting that mankind can never achieve absolute certainty, he cited the Christ who "acted the part of the minister of the visible church of God" in accepting Judas as co-worker. Similarly, he adduced episodes in Scripture in which "persons were suddenly admitted by baptism into the visible church…without strictness in their probation." And he drew on his wife's reaction to the New Lights, citing (though not by name) Sarah Edwards' "peculiar aversion to a judging others that were professing Christians in good standing in the visible church, that they were not converted, or with respect to their degrees of grace."6

He spoke out even more emphatically in the Religious Affections. There he denied that "converted and unconverted men" can "be certainly distinguished by the account they give of their experience," the reasons being that each could make "the same relation of experiences"

-- 50 --

and that "many persons have given a fair account of a work of conversion, that have carried well in the eye of the world for several years, but have not proved well at last." It is notable that, in support of this argument, Edwards quoted his grandfather's Appeal to the Learned. Repeatedly he emphasized the difficulty of distinguishing, on the basis of "outward expressions and appearances," a "hypocrite and a true saint." It was, he lamented, an exhibition of an "indecent self-exaltation, and arrogance" for "poor fallible dark mortals, to pretend that they can determine and know, who are really sincere and upright before God, and who are not!"7

Throughout the 1740s Edwards reiterated two other traditional themes, that Christ was no "searcher of hearts" and that God had given men no "certain rule" to enable them to differentiate the converted from the unregenerate.8 Similarly Stoddardean in tone is the following passage from Distinguishing Marks:

To suppose that men have ability and right to determine the state of the souls of visible Christians, and so to make an open separation between saints and hypocrites, that true saints may be of one visible company and hypocrites of another, separated by a partition that men make, carries in it an inconsistency: for it supposes that God has given men power to make another visible church within his visible church; for by visible Christians, or those that are of God's visible church, can be understood nothing else than that company that are Christians or saints, visibly so; i.e. that have a right to be received as such in the eye of a public charity.9

Here as in "Miscellanies" no. 338, the word "visible" is conflated with "charity" and, by implication, with outward. Edwards also repeated an argument Thomas Blake and Richard Baxter had used in the middle of the seventeenth century, that in generating a church within a church purists ignored the legitimacy of the broader institution. And he appropriated Stoddard's exegesis of the parable of the wheat and the tares.1

Edwards the revivalist thus coexisted with Edwards the defender of the ordained ministry and instituted church against "enthusiastical

-- 51 --

religion." Edwards the revivalist joined with his relatives the Williamses in welcoming the revivals as the answer to many years of prayer that God would intervene and awaken the New England churches from formality. However checkered, the process was moving the churches to a higher level; even though the revivals made him more aware than ever of hypocrisy and its manifold disguises, Edwards remained confident that the history of redemption was unfolding in these events.2 Meanwhile he counseled patience in warning against the premise that, here on earth, the visible church should encompass only the regenerate, as judged by experience. So he argued not only on the eve of the Northampton controversy, but after it was under way, for he wanted to make absolutely clear the line between himself and "those wild people, who have lately appeared in New England, called Separatists." To this end he began the Humble Inquiry by citing his testimony against the Separates in the Religious Affections and the Life of David Brainerd, complaining anew of the "notion of a pure church by means of a spirit of discerning" and renouncing one more time the test of experience. He could not have been more explicit: his program was different from what the radical New Lights were demanding.3

Moving toward a Change of Mind

That Edwards had any reason to quarrel with Stoddardeanism may be the real surprise. He was not under pressure from a New Light faction in his congregation or from friends and colleagues in the ministry to purify the church; after having quelled the brief eruption of enthusiasm in Northampton, he was spared the turmoil that disrupted so many other parishes. Moreover, as a Stoddardean he had found that he could generate seasons of spiritual excitement while also practicing

-- 52 --

a broad churchmanship.4 By the close of the two revivals he experienced in Northampton, the little awakening of 1734–35 and the great of 1740–42, a high percentage of the adult population of Northampton were full church members and, according to the minister who participated in admitting them, visible saints. Edwards declared in A Faithful Narrative of Surprising Conversions that the three hundred or so townspeople he "received into our communion" in 1734–35 were to be considered true Christians; "I had very sufficient evidence of the conversion of their souls through divine grace," he asserted in the first printed version of A Faithful Narrative.5 He was scarcely less emphatic in describing the consequences of the revivals of 1740–42. During this time he admitted at least one hundred others either to the Lord's Supper or to the church covenant. These figures mean that Edwards, not Stoddard, was responsible for the large enrollment of townspeople in the Northampton church; as Edwards noted in Misrepresentations Corrected, he had been "immediately concerned in the admission of more than three quarters" of the church's "present members." Those who voted to dismiss Edwards were not the leftovers from a lax admissions policy, but saints according to their pastor at the time that he admitted them.6

Nonetheless he changed his mind.7 Why he did so and in what

-- 53 --

manner are questions that can be answered only by keeping in view the fundamental elements of his situation: his defense of the compromises of the Congregational tradition in response to divisive enthusiasm; the uneasiness he shared with many others about the trade-off between purity and comprehensiveness; his confidence, inherited from evangelical Calvinism but newly reaffirmed, that true religion could be differentiated from hypocrisy; and the politics of his ministry in Northampton, especially as it had to do with the authority of his office and with popular religion.

The task of interpreting his change of mind would be easier if we knew exactly when he came to the conclusions he announced in early 1749. The evidence suggests that he had changed his mind by 1745, or when he began writing the Religious Affections. The evidence also suggests that he was in no hurry to inform his congregation.8 During these years there is little to indicate that he was reading in the older literature on Congregationalism or reflecting on the nature of the church. Was it at his initiative that the Hampshire Association debated in 1745 "Whether an Unregenerate Person has a Right in the Sight of

-- 54 --

God to the Lords Supper"? And what did he say when, two years later the Association discussed the situation that lay at the root of Stoddardeanism: "What is the Duty of a Church respecting those Members that absent themselves from the Lords Supper"?9

Only when we turn to the situation in Northampton itself are there signs of fresh difficulties. Some of these had to do with his salary. Of more significance, or at least more ominous in hindsight, was that by 1744 people had ceased to apply for church membership.1 Then there was the "bad book affair" of early 1744, a mishandled case of church discipline that was cruelly revealing of the limitations on Edwards' authority. In the Farewell Sermon Edwards referred to this event as that "which gave so great offense, and by which I became so obnoxious," and Samuel Hopkins, who knew as much as anyone about the situation in Northampton leading up to the communion controversy, went back to 1744 and the bad book affair in narrating Edwards' dismissal.2

These troubles, together with the general cooling off of religious zeal, prompted a discontent in Edwards that he expressed in the judgment, asserted numerous times in the closing years of the decade, that "multitudes" of his parishioners remained "in a christless state" despite having entered the church.3 That judgment energized a web of interrelated issues, for it broached the issue of true religion, which in turn touched on access to the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper. Definitions of church membership and access to the sacraments of baptism and the Lord's Supper were intrinsically political; were a minister to defy popular religion, the outcome was certain to be a struggle. To make matters worse, the townspeople had a second grievance against Edwards. He was demanding that he have priority in examining candidates for church membership and, in keeping with this priority, the right to decide whether or not someone was suitable.4

-- 55 --

But the laity were not about to grant an angry man this power—and that Edwards was angry at certain families in Northampton was no secret to anyone.

The deterioration of his ministry and the shape of his response to that deterioration can be glimpsed in the letter he published in The Christian History describing the revival in Northampton. Written in December 1743, it opens with a backward glance at the "little revival" and the "great abiding alteration" in "vice" that followed upon it. Edwards recalled in particular the good effects among young people "with respect to reveling, frolicking, profane and unclean conversation, and lewd songs." There had even been "a great alteration amongst both old and young with respect to tavern-haunting." True, there had also been a subsequent "decay of religious affections." But in general things were better.

There followed an account of the itinerant evangelist George Whitefield's visit to Northampton in 1740, the first wave of conversions, and the "very great revival of religion" that began under the leadership of Samuel Buell in early 1742 while Edwards was preaching elsewhere. As soon as he returned, Edwards had to deal with what he regarded as excessive emotionalism; "a great deal of caution and pains were found necessary," he told Thomas Prince, editor of The Christian History, "to keep the people, many of them, from running wild." It was at this moment that Edwards drew up a covenant and conducted everyone above the age of fourteen in the church (which is to say, practically the entire adult population of the town) through the ceremony of covenant renewal, a "vow to God" that they would "seek and serve" him in specific ways.

-- 56 --

Any modern reader of this lengthy covenant, which fills four pages in a recent printing, must begin to feel, midway through, that it asks too much, that it defies common sense. Much is made of being honest in fulfilling neighborly obligations and foregoing private interest in public affairs. Other sections have to do with feelings of revenge and how the church members should "endeavor to their utmost to root it out." In a separate clause the youth pledged "never to allow ourselves in any youthful diversions or pastimes" to do anything that might hinder religious practice or feelings. Sex was another issue. Edwards had already noted that, in the aftermath of the revival of 1735, "instances of fornication have been very rare." Now he asked the young people to "avoid all freedoms and familiarities…tending either to stir up or gratify a lust of lasciviousness." The covenant concluded with a sweeping pledge to "devote our whole lives to be laboriously spent in the business of religion: ever making it our greatest business." And a note of realism intruded; acknowledging a "proneness to forget our most solemn vows," the church members pledged to examine themselves "especially before the sacrament of the Lord's Supper; and beg of God that he would…keep us from wickedly dissembling in these our solemn vows."

This final clause is crucial, for it contrasts sincerity (or practicing what you say) and a "dissembling in these our solemn vows" and links the two possibilities to participation in the Lord's Supper—that is, links them in the sense that persons who dissembled and who came to the Lord's Table were violating the sacramental rule of self-examination. From here the road lay open to the controversy that broke out in 1749. Were Edwards to conclude that the people of the church were dissembling hypocrites, he would have to find some means of dramatizing their unworthiness to participate in the Lord's Supper.5

Edwards started down that road in his letter to Prince. In a paragraph that began by alluding to the problem of New Light excesses, he spoke of judging "the goodness of a person's state" and affirmed that the basis for such judgments should be "the effect wrought on the temper of the soul…and remaining afterwards." How were his parishioners faring by this standard? In general, Edwards emphasized the "good effects" that lingered in the aftermath of the revival. But he also added a sentence that, in hindsight, has a different ring: "The temper

-- 57 --

that some of them show, and the behavior they have been of…make me much afraid lest there be a considerable number that have woefully deceived themselves."6

The second thoughts he was having in December 1743, second thoughts implicit in the covenant of March 1742, had intensified by the time he wrote the Religious Affections. There he took up the question of sincerity as judged by "Christian practice" and the evangelical dictum that, despite the many difficulties in the way, the godly could be differentiated from the profane. Because the Religious Affections defends this distinction it deserves a place in the sequence of events that lead up to the Humble Inquiry.

The essential message of evangelical Calvinism was that grace and nature were utterly different. In the Religious Affections Edwards restated the case for the radical difference between grace and nature in terms of the objective "excellence" of God and, recasting older terminology, proposed that the Holy Spirit created a "new inward sense" in the regenerate that enabled them to perceive and participate in this excellence. These statements did not resolve the problem of knowing who was saved and who was not. What happened in the heart was invisible, and the hypocrite could seem to be transformed. Like so many of his predecessors Edwards turned, therefore, to "sanctification." The "great and distinguishing sign" of grace, and the only sure test of sincerity, was Christian practice.7

In the context of the controversy that erupted three years later, it is crucial to note that Edwards regarded practice as indissolubly connected to the affections. Asserting that Christ lives "in the heart," he went on to define the indwelling Christ as "an internal vital principle," the source of all actions in the world. The outer and the inner were one. What made them one was a common "activity, power, and efficacy." Echoing Paul's letter to the Ephesians, Edwards declared that "Christ is not in the heart of a saint, as in a sepulchre, or as a dead Savior, that does nothing; but as in his temple, one that is alive from the dead." "And thus it is," he concluded, "that holy affections have a governing power in the course of a man's life." By "course of…life" he meant the full length of someone's life, in contrast to ecstasies that lasted but a moment or some fleeting signs of Christian behavior.8

-- 58 --

Yet how many lacked "holy affections" and consequently could not manifest the fruits of Christian practice! And how many who had once been deemed among the saints had failed the test of practice. A darker tone came over the Religious Affections as Edwards described the scope and depth of hypocrisy. "It has been a common thing in the church of God, for such bright professors, that are received as eminent saints among the saints, to fall away and come to nothing." There follows a series of descriptions that enforce the judgment about hypocrisy. Several of these descriptions seem rooted in his Northampton pastorate as when he condemned persons who, like the young men in the bad book affair, were contemptuous of the authority of those set over them; persons who went to taverns; and most lamentably of all, church members who made a "profession" but were insincere in doing so.9

A paragraph of the Religious Affections—a passage Edwards would cite in 1749 as signaling his change of mind—concerns this very situation:

In order to a profession of Christianity being accepted to any purpose, there ought to be good reason, from the circumstances of the profession, to think that the professor don't make such a profession out of a mere customary compliance with a prescribed form, using words without any distinct meaning, or in a very lax and ambiguous manner, as confessions of faith are often subscribed; but that the professor understandingly and honestly signifies what he is conscious of in his own heart; otherwise his profession can be of no significance, and no more to be regarded than the sound of things without life.

Lax profession versus one that is honestly delivered—three years later, such would be the contrasting terms of the Humble Inquiry. In a further sign of things to come, Edwards cited the episodes of Peter and the three thousand and Philip and the eunuch in declaring that "repentance is a qualification that must be visible in order to baptism."1

The Religious Affections thus foreshadows the crucial argument of the Humble Inquiry in declaring that profession, properly understood, allows the church to judge adults seeking baptism for their children or access for themselves to the Lord's Supper.2 Nothing in the Affections,

-- 59 --

in the treatise of 1749, or in the "Miscellanies" that surround them indicates directly why Edwards finally allowed his animus against hypocrisy to overcome the policy of charity. Nowhere did he pause to acknowledge that certain passages in Scripture, like the parable of the wheat and the tares, had once meant something different to him. Yet the Humble Inquiry and its immediate companions, the Farewell Sermon of 1750 and the Misrepresentations Corrected of 1752, are remarkably explicit in two respects: in voicing the judgment that many of his parishioners were among the deceived; and in condemning as insincere the practice, crucial to the being of popular religion, of allowing parents readily to secure baptism for their children.

Some months after the Humble Inquiry appeared, Edwards made a revealing comment on the controversy in the course of a letter to Peter Clark, the minister of Salem Village. Here he spoke of popular religion and what he regarded as its corruptions. "The great thing which I have scrupled in the established method of this church's proceeding," he told Clark in May 1750,

and which I dare no longer go on in, is their publicly assenting to the form of words rehearsed on occasion of their admission to the communion, without pretending thereby to mean any such thing as an hearty consent to the terms of the gospel covenant, or to mean any such faith or repentance as belong to the covenant of grace…: it being, at the same time that the words are used, their known and established principle, which they openly profess and proceed upon, that men may and ought to use these words, and mean no such thing, but something else of a nature far inferior.3

What else did the townspeople regard as a "known and established principle" that Edwards now condemned as hypocrisy? Nothing less than the central motif of popular religion, the expectation of lay men and women that their children would benefit from the sacrament of baptism. The relevant passages in the Humble Inquiry have gone unnoticed in spite of Edwards' bluntness. Why, he asked, did adult children of the church renew their covenants? Because they had gotten married and wanted their children to be baptized. Renewal of covenant had deteriorated into mere "form and ceremony," "it being visibly a prevailing custom for persons to neglect this, till they come to be

-- 60 --

married, and then to do it for their credit's sake, and that their children may be baptized."4 And why did churches like Northampton's practice the halfway covenant? Because such an "expedient" gives "ease to ancestors with respect to their posterity, in times of great declension and degeneracy." Accusing his parishioners of "presumptuous lying for the sake of the honor of being in the church, having children baptized, and voting in ecclesiastical affairs," he imposed the framework of declension on their patterns of religious observance.5

He shared this perspective with his nearby ministerial ally, Edward Billings, and with his friend and former student Joseph Bellamy. What these two had to say about the halfway covenant illuminates the situation in Northampton. In A Dialogue about Sacraments (written about 1752), Billings lashed out against the "many Churches in the Country" in which the baptism of infants signified nothing more than that "their Parents are married." Observing that "nothing induces a Man or Woman to join to the Church, like marrying; and there is scarcely a Child born whose Parents (one at least) did not join to the Church, just before the Birth of the Child," Billings denounced these practices as morally wrong.6 In his The Half-Way Covenant: A Dialogue (1766), Bellamy dramatized the confrontation between a purist like himself and a parishioner who wanted a child baptized, a confrontation that ended with the minister refusing the request on the grounds that the parishioner was "graceless."7

We are now in a position to understand the rage of the church members and townspeople of Northampton. Edwards wished to abolish what for them was basic, the procedures that linked family structure and religion. So he acknowledged indirectly in the Humble Inquiry when he remarked that "parents are angry" and "complain that their children are denied the honor of having God's mark set upon them as

-- 61 --

some of his."8 And so he acknowledged openly in a letter to the Boston minister Thomas Foxcroft. "The greatest difficulty of all relating to my principles is here, respecting baptism," he wrote Foxcroft. Compromise seemed possible on the issue of access to the Lord's Supper. "But with respect to the other sacrament there is scarce any hope of it."9

In the aftermath of the little revival Edwards had described his congregation as beset by scrupulosity.1 Now he described them as systematically insincere. Like so many others in the Congregational tradition he had tried to forge a ministry that accommodated these extremes. It was the failure of that ministry, a failure all too evident in the ineffectiveness of his preaching in Northampton by the mid-1740s, that led him to give up on Stoddardeanism and the middle way of tradition. The problem was not of his doing, he reasoned, but arose from the stubborn and intractable pride that expressed itself in popular religion. The one stratagem he could still pursue was the course of action he had imagined many years before in "Miscellanies" no. 462: do away with comprehensiveness, introduce the requirement of sincere profession, and force the hypocritical to obtain baptism for their children on terms that he defined.2

The Humble Inquiry is elusive, or perhaps purposefully vague, in specifying the actual procedures for becoming an adult member of the church and gaining access to its privileges. Historians of religion in early New England have frequently assumed that Edwards wanted to restore the requirement of a relation of spiritual experience. His contemporary critic Solomon Williams said as much in accusing his cousin of requiring an "account of experiences" of candidates for membership. But as Edwards strenuously insisted in his letter of May 1750 to Peter Clark, he did not specify the procedure of a relation. The Humble Inquiry is infused with a distrust of narratives of spiritual experience, a distrust rooted in his long-standing discontent with the traditional vocabulary of stages as well as in his disenchantment with the New Lights. He turned, therefore, to the traditional alternative, the act of profession. The two model professions he published in Misrepresentations Corrected seem remarkably benign. What they do not adequately convey are three crucial aspects of the process of admission: first,

-- 62 --

Edwards' insistence, voiced at great length in these treatises, that candidates declare that they possess "true godliness"; second, and closely related, that anyone making a profession do so "heartily," which is to say, do so on the basis of an "inward assent" that saving grace alone made possible; and third, that those embarking on this process seek the counsel of a minister skilled in differentiating the sincere from the hypocritical. During that encounter, candidates were undoubtedly expected to describe the work of grace, for a young woman who came to Edwards privately in 1748 to request admission to the church gave him "a hopeful account of her religious experience and the operations of divine grace upon her mind." In effect, this scheme depended less on specific procedures than on the psychological consequences of calling upon candidates to acknowledge the absolute difference between real grace and its simulacres and to present themselves for admission only if they could profess "the real existence of…what lays a proper and good foundation" for such a statement.3

An Humble Inquiry

Edwards wrote An Humble Inquiry with great "rapidity" in March and April 1749.4 His task was to prove Stoddard wrong for maintaining that the unregenerate or the morally sincere could be accepted into the church and to the Lord's Supper as visible saints. (The issue of the Lord's Supper as a converting ordinance was peripheral to the controversy, the reason being that this concept was secondary to, and consequent upon, the admitting of unregenerate persons to the ordinance.) Point by point, Edwards moved through the arguments advanced by Stoddard, Blake, and other seventeenth-century advocates of a moderate or comprehensive understanding of the church. Taking the contrary position, Edwards maintained that visible saints were those persons who met the conditions of the "covenant of grace." He regarded the visible and the real as qualities that were connected; for him, the "visibility of saintship" signified the reality of grace, or a "real" change of heart.

-- 63 --

The central question Edwards posed at the outset and answered in the negative was this: "Whether, according to the rules of Christ, any ought to be admitted to the communion and privileges of members of the visible church of Christ in complete standing, but such as are in profession, and in the eye of the church's Christian judgment, godly or gracious persons?" He went on to clarify his terms, indicating, for example, that "complete standing" applied only to adults and not to infants.

Edwards next clarified the nature of an appropriate profession. Profession was visible, in contrast to the invisibility of "godliness itself." Instead of letting this distinction open out into Stoddardeanism, Edwards argued that profession had to be "explicit" and performed "sincerely." In this connection Edwards discussed appearance versus reality. And here he made a distinction (itself a commonplace within the Congregational tradition) between that which is made manifest to the church as over against that which motivates the candidate; between ecclesiastical right ("in foro ecclesiæ") and experiential foundation. "There is a difference between these." Thus, it is the profession of a belief and the visibility "to the eye of a Christian judgment" that give a person the right before the church, "but it is the real existence of these things, that is what lays a proper and good foundation for his making this profession, and so demanding these privileges."5

By "Christian judgment" Edwards did not mean a species of "negative charity" by which one forbears to censure because he does not know the truth for certain, but rather "a positive judgment, founded on some positive appearance, or visibility, some outward manifestations that ordinarily render the thing probable." He emphasized that probability was not the same as certainty. But for him it mattered that the church achieve the highest possible degree of probability with respect to true holiness in its members, while by no means implying the "pretense of any scheme, that shall be effectual to keep all hypocrites out of the church, and for the establishing in that sense a pure church."6

Having stated as clearly as possible his definition of the principal terms of the proposition, Edwards proceeded in the second and third sections of the book to defend his anti-Stoddardean position. His eleven reasons occupy more than half of the book. The great length of

-- 64 --

this section is to be accounted for by Edwards' tendency to strive for an encyclopedic inclusiveness. In one sense, all eleven reasons for the negative are subdivisions of a single topic, namely, Edwards' interpretation of the biblical teaching on qualifications for full communion.

The first, second, and sixth reasons deal with the question of visible sainthood. Edwards began his discussion of the first reason by assuming "that none ought to be admitted as members of the visible church of Christ but visible saints and professing saints, or visible and professing Christians." Confronting, once again, Stoddard's understanding of visibility, he complained of the absurdity of a "visibility with regard to saintship or holiness" that "had no reference to the reality…as though by visible saints were not meant those who to appearance are real saints." He granted the difference between "real saints" in the "eye of God" and in the judgment of man. The church must rely on external signs and appearances. Even so, these signs must establish the probability of grace. Stoddardeanism violated this principle, for it allowed persons to invoke "words and signs" without "so much as making any pretense to the least degree of sanctifying grace." As Edwards went on to argue in his second reason, any equivocal use of language vitiates the essential nature of public covenanting. The person who enters into an explicit covenant is understood to be complying with it "internally and spiritually."7

The sixth reason continues the biblical doctrine of visible sainthood by emphasizing the "representations which Christ makes of his visible church." On the whole, according to Edwards, "all Christ's professing disciples…are such as profess a saving interest in him and relation to him." In the parable of the ten virgins, for example, the "lamps signify in general the appearance of grace or godliness." Similarly, in the parable of the "great supper," or (as Edwards calls it) "the marriage which the king made for his son" (Matthew 22 and Luke 14), the wedding garment stands for "true piety," which is the necessary qualification both for salvation and for visible sainthood and participation in the sacraments.8

Edwards' third reason employed a variation of an argument used by Increase Mather against Stoddard. Stoddard had maintained (and Edwards echoed him in denouncing the Separates) that since God has

-- 65 --

given no "certain rule" by which the church can distinguish between true saints and hypocrites, all that may be expected of a prospective member is a historical knowledge of the doctrines of the gospel and a moral sincerity Mather pointed out that it was just as difficult to determine true belief of doctrine as to discriminate true from false professions of faith. Reversing the emphasis, Edwards saw "no good reason why" someone professing right belief could not also profess what was in the heart.9

Reasons seven and eight are based on an interpretation of the practice of the primitive Christian church. Edwards argued in the seventh reason that the early hearers of the apostles (as recounted in Acts) were first enjoined to repent, then to believe, and finally to profess their belief before being baptized and admitted to the church. He conceded that, in those extraordinary times, the church did not have to inquire so particularly into these matters "as may be expedient in a more ordinary state of things." The eighth reason extends the analysis to all other relevant books in the New Testament, and the conclusion reached is that "the apostles continually in their epistles speak to and of them as supposing and judging them to be gracious persons." This judgment was not "a kind of negative charity," but, insisted Edwards, "a positive judgment that" they were "gracious persons."1

The fourth, fifth, and ninth reasons add little to Edwards' argument. Number four shows that in the Bible visible saints who are not truly pious are dubbed counterfeits and deceivers. Number five is a brief commentary on Isaiah 56:1-8. Number nine deals with "Christian brotherly love," a theme of much importance to Edwards elsewhere but of incidental importance here.2

The tenth reason focuses on the significance of the actions of both parties in the administration of the Lord's Supper. The entire ceremony has to do with matters of the heart, with professing that which the parties have truly experienced and confirming and sealing what is reciprocally deemed a reality. Here too the theme of covenant enters, with Edwards arguing that an "explicit covenanting" must precede admission to the Supper. This act of covenanting is identical with the profession of religious faith that is the necessary qualification for full communion.3

-- 66 --

The eleventh and final reason has to do with 1 Corinthians 11:28. Did the examination that the verse called for have to do with self-examination or with "discerning the nature of the ordinance," as Stoddard had argued? Edwards, of course, preferred the former interpretation and linked such self-examination to "a living faith" that must exist in the candidate.4

In the final part of the Humble Inquiry Edwards answered twenty objections to his scheme. Here he provided a massive review of his position from different perspectives, thereby clarifying, reemphasizing, and in some cases supplementing his presentation. These twenty objections may be classified into four major categories.

The objections in the first category arise from divergent interpretations of problematic passages of Scripture. This group comprises numbers one through seven. He took up the scriptural analogy of the church as a school, the typological interpretation of the nation of Israel, the Passover as type of the Lord's Supper, the multitudes that John the Baptist baptized (Matthew 3), the verse "many are called but few are chosen" (Matthew 22:14), the parable of the wheat and the tares, and the presence of Judas at Passover and the Lord's Supper. Respecting, this last, Edwards argued that Judas had left before the Lord's Supper was instituted; even if he remained, Jesus had not acted as "searcher of hearts."5

The second category of objections, numbers eight through ten, takes up the difference between "certainty" and "visibility." Edwards did not expect candidates for the sacrament—or the church in judging these candidates—to know for sure who were the regenerate; it was conscientious profession that mattered. The tenth objection concerns the pastoral consequences of heightened scrutiny. Here, in an echo of "Miscellanies" no. 462, Edwards argued that increasing the distress of "persons of a tender conscience" was not too high a price to pay if it helped overcome the "stupidity and hardness of heart" that characterized some who were admitted under the Stoddardean policy. He called for more, not less, self-examination, brushed aside the problem of perplexity, and insisted that assurance of salvation can be obtained.6

The third category of objections centers on qualifications, the purposes and effects of the sacraments, and the legitimacy of claims to

-- 67 --

them. This category includes objections 11 through 13 and number 20. In numbers 13 and 20 Edwards addresses the argument that, because the Lord's Supper has a tendency to promote conversion, it was "appointed" for that purpose. In response, he differentiated what a sacrament may cause circumstantially from the purpose for which it was instituted.

The fourth category comprises objections 14 through 19, all of which are concerned with the nature of a profession. In these objections Edwards dealt with such problems as discriminating true from false profession, guarding against the exclusion of true saints because of overly stringent requirements, and avoiding infiltration of the church by hypocrites. Edwards granted what Stoddard had said so often, that hypocrites could not be excluded from the church. But was it "not better," he asked, "to have a few real living Christians kept back through darkness and scruples, than to open a door for the letting in such universal ruin" as admitting many who were unregenerate?7

This judgment leads into the answer to objection 19 about baptism. Edwards asserted that access to baptism depended on the same "profession of godliness" required of communicants in the Lord's Supper. He repudiated the distinction, formulated not by Stoddard but by the first-generation clergy, between the two sacraments. Edwards also complained of the connection between baptism and family religion. "Why should parents covet the external honor for their children," he exclaimed, "while they are so careless about the spiritual blessing!" Not explicitly, but by the logic of his argument, Edwards repudiated the halfway covenant. Yet he could not do away with all the contradictions that figured in the New England system, for he affirmed that baptized adults who, when they came of age, failed to make the proper profession of faith, ought "not to be cast out of the church or cease to be in any respect its members."8

The Humble Inquiry concludes with an Appendix credited to Thomas Foxcroft, the Boston minister who oversaw publication of the treatise. The Appendix summarizes what had been said by other ministers in the Reformed tradition in favor of limiting the Lord's Supper to visible saints. Three groups of ministers were featured: seventeenth- and eighteenth-century English Nonconformists, some of whom had written sacramental manuals advising communicants on how to approach

-- 68 --

the Lord's Supper and others of whom had opposed the Presbyterians Thomas Blake, John Humfrey, and John Timson; the first and second generations of New England divines; and eighteenth-century Scottish Presbyterian ministers, some of them adherents of the Evangelical wing of that Church and others among the Seceders who left it over issues of evangelism and church and state.

Edwards played a part in composing the Appendix. We have to suppose, however, that Foxcroft played the larger role. In a brief note to him dated May 12, 1749, Edwards spoke of sending to the Boston minister "an abstract of your former letter to be added to my book as an appendix, if you please." In the absence of Foxcroft's original draft it cannot be determined what Edwards deleted, compressed, or added. He seems to have felt the limitations of his knowledge, for he referred to himself as "little read in church history." He may also have realized that Foxcroft, who enjoyed wide contacts with English and Scottish evangelicals, was better situated to amass the references that pile up in the Appendix. "You have vastly greater opportunities of full information concerning the present principles and practice in England and Scotland than I," Edwards wrote in May.9

The citations in the Appendix throw into relief an interesting characteristic of the Humble Inquiry, and one that is not accidental. Nowhere in the treatise did Edwards employ the Mathers' arguments against Stoddardeanism or follow them in lamenting apostasy from the principles of the Cambridge Platform and the Congregational tradition. This indifference to older debates has something to do with Edwards' determination to return to the Word of God, as he announced he was doing on the title page of the Humble Inquiry. But it also reminds us that, in contrast to the Separates, he was not seeking to retrieve the more democratic and puristic Congregationalism of the Cambridge Platform. The many citations in the Appendix mislead the reader in this respect, for Edwards was unable to invoke the past without equivocation.1

Critical Voices

The townspeople of Northampton looked for someone willing to rebut Edwards' arguments almost as soon as he informed the church

-- 69 --

of his change of mind. None of the town's initiatives succeeded. Meanwhile, Elisha Williams began to prepare a response. Hearing of this possibility, Edwards wrote to Foxcroft asking him to do his best (using reasons Edwards had supplied) to dissuade Williams from the project.2 In the event, the press of his career as a politician kept Elisha from continuing. He may have passed his notes or working draft to his cousin Solomon, the minister in Lebanon, though the more likely possibility is that Solomon had determined on his own to respond; so at least he indicated to Edwards in a letter of August 20, 1750, in which, having said frankly that he judged his cousin to be "on the Wrong Side of the Question," he reported that illness in his family had delayed the preparation of an answer—a delay occasioned also by the realization that Edwards "was so fixd" in his "Sentiments" that "it would probably be an Endless Controversy."3 Nonetheless, Solomon went ahead and published The True State of the Question Concerning the Qualifications Necessary to Lawful Communion in the Christian Sacraments in May 1751. Another critique came from Jonathan Ashley, the minister of Deerfield. Two sermons he preached in Northampton in 1751 were published in Boston in 1753 as An Humble Attempt to give a Clear Account from Scripture, how the Jewish and Christian Churches were Constituted, and what sort of Saintship is necessary in order to be a Communicant at the Lord's Table.

Solomon Williams4 took up Edwards' challenge to "attend the true state of the question" by subjecting the arguments of the Humble Inquiry to careful scrutiny on the basis of his own biblical exegesis and research among the standard authorities; responding to the citations heaped up in the Appendix to the Humble Inquiry, Williams countered with the names of Blake, Hudson, John Cotton, John Humfrey, and William Guthrie, among others, while insisting that Stoddardeanism was consistent with the "general Sense of Protestant Churches and

-- 70 --

Divines."5 The True State is formally divided into two main parts, but the actual substantive division overlaps this boundary. Most of part 1 is devoted to clarifying the question, defining the terms, and elaborating three general reasons that were fundamental to his position. The remainder of part 1 and all of part 2 consist of detailed responses to Edwards' views and reaffirmations of many of the objections posed in the third part of the Humble Inquiry. Here, Williams also noted which of these objections were Stoddard's own arguments.

Williams began by rejecting Edwards' statement of the question. The heart of the matter was not sincerity, as Edwards made it out to be, but whether "sanctifying grace is…necessary to the Lawful attending of the Lord's Supper." Williams quoted from the Appeal to the Learned to demonstrate that this had been the crucial issue for Stoddard. Another procedure against which he protested was the deliberate bracketing of all consideration of infants. Lamenting this subterfuge, Williams insisted that any discussion of policy is incomplete which ignored the problem of duly baptized infants who come of age without showing cause for excommunication; the church must determine the standing of persons baptized in infancy who attain maturity, possess doctrinal knowledge, lead nonscandalous lives, and evince a sincere desire to be godly. Reiterating the conclusions of the Synod of 1662, Williams granted such persons membership in the visible church. "At present," he insisted, the church cannot differentiate the unsanctified from those who are "professors of the Gospel of Christ." And he echoed Stoddard in arguing that admission to any one privilege or sacrament of the church was grounds for admission to them all, including the Lord's Supper: "I confess, I know of no Members of the Church, unless Persons under Censure, or scandalously ignorant, or immoral, but what the Church ought to admit to Communion & Privileges of Members in compleat Standing; if they are capable of receiving and enjoying them, and desire to do so." The question in debate is therefore the kind of evidence the church must expect "of the Saintship of those who are admitted to the outward Privileges of the Covenant of Grace."6

From this vantage, Williams construed Edwards to be saying that the church must require "the highest Evidence a Man can give of Sincerity." Edwards' scheme requires that candidates utter "words giving

-- 71 --

an Account of their Experience of the sanctifying Work of God's Spirit on their Hearts." Picking up on the distinction Edwards had drawn between negative and positive judgment, Williams asserted that his opponent was expecting the church to make "a positive Judgment, that the man is a real Christian." But is the church equipped to go beyond the outward signs of sincerity? Consistent with what Stoddard had said, Williams declared that the church lacks the means of accomplishing a positive discrimination. Instead, the church must accept "the Profession & Engagement itself…taking it in the most favourable Sense that it can bear," a point he supported by emphasizing the distinction between the "External Exhibition" of the covenant of grace, which is "all that is visible in this world," and an inward or internal sense that remains hidden and mysterious.7

In support of his position, Williams offered three general reasons. The first proceeds from the fact that "Christ in his public visible Dealings with the Church" never acted as the "Searcher of Hearts." Accordingly, Williams held that the church is to found its judgment concerning church admission "only upon the visible Profession & Behaviour of Men." The church is to exercise a "rational Judgment of Charity." The second reason reinforces the first by canvasing biblical history to show that "Christ has in Fact always acted thus in his outward Dispensations toward his Church." Thus in Old Testament times, "God took all Israel into Covenant" without requiring any evidence of regeneration. Williams asserted as his third reason the psychological and theological premise that "true Desires of Grace are Grace." There will always be uncertainty about the "secret Working" of God's Spirit in "the hearts of other men." Hence he differentiates how the church made its judgments in "granting the Privileges of the visible Kingdom of Christ" from how the judgment is made of "the final State of Men." The Stoddardean conclusion from this line of reasoning is inescapable: "And since 'tis God's Will that his Church should admit all such visible Saints, it follows that the Lord's Supper is a converting Ordinance to such of them as are converted."8

Williams went on to defend Stoddard against the charge that his way of doing things allows (or indeed requires) the church to admit the very worst of sinners to the Lord's Table. Here, he argued, Edwards had misread his grandfather, who said only that the unsanctified

-- 72 --

should not be excluded merely because of their "Want of sanctifying Grace." What is more, everyone who entered into covenant, whether sanctified or not, was making "a professed Subjection to Christ, and giving up themselves to be taught, ruled, & led by him in the Gospel way to Salvation."9 Evangelical that he was, Williams did agree with Edwards that true piety is what communicants ought to bring to the Lord's Table, and, like Stoddard before him, he warned that it is "not visibly entring into Covenant with God, that will give Men just Ground of Hope of entring into Heaven." Members of the visible church who "rest in an outward Profession…without true Faith & sincere Repentance, will be dreadfully disappointed, when the trying Time shall come."1 Yet it was wrong to impose the rule of sanctifying grace, for the judging of true faith and sincere repentance lies beyond the purview of the instituted church. Only the Separates think otherwise, and since Edwards declares that he has rejected the Separatist pretension of possessing a "Spirit of Discerning," he should either modify his position or acknowledge his inconsistency.

There is only one standpoint from which Edwards' scheme may appear tenable. Williams granted, as did Edwards and Stoddard, that assurance of salvation is attainable, "and where it is attained, and for so long as it is preserved," Edwards' scheme is practicable. But the major difficulty remains: "how to practice where it is not attained; and how Christians can act 'till they are free from Doubts." For the church in its present condition a less ideal but more realistic procedure has been established. God requires all who hear the gospel to believe, to repent, and to "attend on all the Ordinances which he hath appointed in his Word; and diligently to use all Means instituted for their Salvation…and engage to fulfil the Terms of the Covenant of Grace." The Holy Spirit will encourage some who do these things. Others who may feel that they lack the assistance of saving grace—who act on the basis of "Moral Sincerity"—are nonetheless fully entitled to "use and enjoy the Ordinances of Salvation" and are likely to benefit from them.2

Williams thus reaffirmed the reasoning that drove Stoddard to sever the connection between assurance of salvation and the Lord's Supper; where assurance is lacking, why make things worse by barring people from the means of grace? Williams predicted that a stricter system would intensify the distress of persons unsure of their salvation.

-- 73 --

Like his father before him, he defended the connection between family and church privileges, arguing that

All the Seed therefore of visible Believers who are baptized, are Seed sown by Christ in his Field the Church. When they grow up under the Education of the Church, and come for the Privilege and Seal of the Lord's Supper, I cannot find out why they have not as good a Right to it as their Fathers & Brethren that we call the Church.

Williams closed by hinting that the issue of admission to the Lord's Simper was really about power. If Edwards was proposing to decide all by himself who should be admitted, then this made him "like Nebuchadnezzer or a Popish priest."3

Jonathan Ashley based his critique of the Humble Inquiry on the traditional distinction between visible saintship and regeneration: "Men may be visible Saints, when we make no Judgment about the State of their Souls." For him, the category of visible saint comprehended "such who renounce Idolatry, and all heathenish Practices, who receive the Gospel, and are baptized." Beyond this the church was unable to proceed. The pastoral consequences of Edwards' scheme would be disastrous: "Who dare come, unless he is fully assured? If he has any the least Doubt, will he dare to approach the Lord's Table, lest the Lord command him to be bound Hand and Foot, and be cast into outer Darkness?" Further, Edwards' scheme did not take account of the need of "natural Men" to "attend upon the Duties of Religion." Ashley also challenged the uses of Scripture in the Humble Inquiry.4

Misrepresentations Corrected

Edwards was unwilling to allow someone else the last word.5 In the spring of 1752, several months after he had moved to Stockbridge, Edwards responded to Solomon Williams in Misrepresentations Corrected, and Truth Vindicated. The treatise, which was published late that

-- 74 --

year, is divided into three parts, the first treating concisely the "general misrepresentations" that Williams makes of the Humble Inquiry, the second providing a cogent examination of Williams' own scheme, and the third offering a series of remarks on his way of reasoning.

Edwards asserted in the first place that Williams had missed the point of the Humble Inquiry. The controversy in Northampton did not have to do with "the degree of evidence," as Williams said, but rather with "what was the thing to be made evident; whether real godliness, or moral sincerity?" Edwards also insisted that his intention was not to refute Stoddard but to defend himself in the controversy with Northampton.6

Edwards next considered ten related misrepresentations of "principles and tenets" expounded in the Humble Inquiry. He denied categorically the allegation that he was demanding the "highest evidence a man can give." To the contrary, he has asked for "some outward manifestation, that ordinarily rendered the thing probable," a view consistent with his repeated statement that the "point of difference was entirely the matter of profession, and the thing to be made visible; not the degree of evidence or visibility." Quoting a passage from a letter he had written to Peter Clark and citing two "drafts of the covenant, or forms of a public profession of religion" that he had been willing to accept, Edwards declared that these texts refute Williams' contention that he demanded the highest evidence.7

The next three misrepresentations involve language. Edwards complained that Williams misconstrued such phrases as "a positive judgment," a "probability of ten to one," and "singly taken," all of them used in the Humble Inquiry in connection with the status of the groups described in the New Testament as taken into communion together, and interpreted by Williams as indicating that Edwards demands assurance of salvation of everyone coming to the Lord's Table. The fifth misrepresentation is the suggestion that Edwards insists on "a certainty of others' regeneration," a view he expressly rejects. Misrepresentations six through nine deal with attempts on Williams' part to link Edwards with the excesses of enthusiasm and Separatism.

The remaining pages of Misrepresentations Corrected are devoted to an attack on Williams' scheme (part 2) and an expose of his fallacious reasoning (part 3). Edwards begins by constructing a threefold argument

-- 75 --

based on Williams' "concessions," their "plain consequences," and their "inconsistence…with the lawfulness of unsanctified persons coming to the Lord's Supper." In the first step of the argument Edwards assembles a composite of more than a score of statements from The True State of the Question in which Williams appears to be on non-Stoddardean ground, as, for example, when he says that "none ought to be admitted, but such as openly profess and declare an hearty consent to the covenant of grace," and so forth. Next, Edwards shows that the necessary consequence is a contradiction, that persons would be required to look for "such a qualification in themselves…to determine whether they may come: when yet they may come whether they have it or no."8 Would it be reasonable, Edwards asks, to imagine that God would give men without grace a lawful right to the Lord's Supper as a converting ordinance and at the same time insist that they exhibit evidence that they have grace?

The inconsistencies in Williams' scheme are traced to several mistaken assumptions. The most important of these is Williams' "notion of a public profession of godliness in terms of an indeterminate and double signification." Williams seems to allow consent to the covenant to be made in terms that do not allow anyone to know whether "the consent signified by the words is a gracious consent." Hence it becomes possible for Williams to affirm that "nothing should be expressed in the words of the profession, but what some unsanctified man may say, and speak true." This principle allows someone to use words in a double sense—to profess godliness, but in a way that signifies "two entirely distinct things, either moral sincerity, or real piety." And if someone should actually use words in this double way, Edwards charges him with being "guilty of deceitful equivocation."9

A second untenable assumption, closely akin to the ambiguity of profession, is the idea of a "visibility without apparent probability." Stoddard had maintained that, according to the rule established by God, "more unconverted persons will be admitted than converted." To Williams' defense of this statement Edwards replies, "That cannot be a credible visibility or appearance, which is not a probable appearance. To say, a thing is credible and not probable, is a contradiction."1

-- 76 --

Edwards concludes his analysis of Williams' scheme by reiterating that the practice of explicitly owning the covenant is a divine institution. As Edwards points out, Williams himself has asserted that the ground of the public judgment includes "a hearty consent to the term of the covenant of grace, and engagement on the part of a person to fulfill it." This kind of statement becomes an additional instance of inconsistencies in the True State.

The third part of Misrepresentations Corrected contains comments on Williams' "exceptionable way of reasoning" and some final remarks on several issues. Edwards enumerates ten questionable "methods" that Williams employs and provides a near-exhaustive catalogue of examples. In the closing seven sections, Edwards gathers up a few loose ends, reiterates familiar points, and returns again to certain passages in Scripture.

An example is Judas' alleged partaking of the Lord's Supper. Edwards asserts that there can be no more argument "drawn from this to prove that it is lawful for men to partake of the Lord's Supper without sanctifying grace…than that it is lawful for them to partake without moral sincerity." And since Williams insists on moral sincerity as a basic requirement, the argument violates a "maxim of undoubted verity" that an argument "can avail nothing to the purpose it is brought for" if it contradicts both positions.2 Here again, Edwards reiterates his objections to the Stoddardean conception of moral sincerity.

In the last four sections Edwards recapitulates and supplements arguments developed more fully in the Humble Inquiry. In section 14 he denies that children of visible saints, born in covenant, are ipso facto entitled to the privileges of the covenant. In 15, he deals with the Stoddardean argument about none coming except those who "know themselves to be converted"; the criterion of certainty worked as much against Williams, Edwards argues, as against his position. Section 16 is about assurance and confusion. Characteristically, Edwards asserts that it is the "negligence and sin" of believers that causes them to suffer "torment." Section 17 has to do with attending all "external covenant Duties." Edwards denies that his scheme required that God command and forbid the same thing at the same time.

At this point the text ends abruptly, to be followed by an "Appendix" in which Edwards addresses the Northampton congregation. He warns his former parishioners of the danger of being "led quite off

-- 77 --

from" evangelical principles that Stoddard had affirmed. To follow Williams is to open the way to "Arminianism, and other loose notions in religion."3

Edwards' "Narrative"

The last of the documents in this volume is Jonathan Edwards' "Narrative"4 of the Northampton controversy from its inception to the summoning of the council that met in June 1750. It details the negotiations that unfolded in the aftermath of the change of mind he announced to his congregation in February 1749. These negotiations were prompted by the intense and persisting hostility to his ministry among the great majority of church members and townspeople. Attempting to delay the rush to judgment that would end his career in Northampton, Edwards pleaded for a "fair hearing" of his ideas. He wanted everyone to read the Humble Inquiry and, this effort failing, to gain permission to preach the substance of the book. He urged the church to admit a handful of persons who were willing to satisfy the new condition of profession, and, after the members voted to cease celebrating the Lord's Supper, he asked that the sacrament be restored. The church members granted him none of these requests, but a council of ministers acknowledged his right to preach as he willed. In February and March 1750 he gave a series of lectures to an audience consisting mostly of "strangers" from outside the town.

Edwards had two other objectives. When it became inevitable that a council would be called to advise on the controversy, he insisted on the privilege not only of selecting a certain number of its members, but also of choosing them from outside Hampshire County. As he said frankly to the church and to a council called in December 1749 to advise on this very question, he was at odds with most of his ministerial neighbors and wanted persons on the council who were sympathetic to is person and point of view. He was finally accorded the privilege of acting two such delegations. The council that met in June 1750

-- 78 --

consisted of five ministers and churches nominated by the church and five by Edwards; the church at Cold Spring, one of his five, refused, however, to send a lay delegate, leaving a council of nineteen persons that divided ten to nine on the issue of Edwards' immediate dismissal.5

Edwards' other objective was to redress his reputation. No sooner had he announced his change of mind than rumors began to spread that he had concealed his thinking from his congregation and was motivated in doing so by ill will and financial self-interest. The church and precinct conveyed some of these charges in a "set of arguments in writing" that was presented to the council of June 1750. This document does not survive, but it was later described as containing "some severe…imputations on Mr. Edwards." So too the majority of the council of June 1750 referred in its "result" to having "heard of some stories spread Abroad, reflecting upon Mr. Edwards' Sincerity, with Regard to the Change of his Sentiments about the qualifications for full Communion." We are left to infer the substance of the case against Edwards from his comment in the "Narrative" that he was suspected of rejecting a candidate for church membership "from personal prejudice, and that my alleging scruples was only to cloak my ill will"; from the glimpse we catch in the same document of Seth Pomeroy asserting in a church meeting that Edwards "regarded his own temporal interest more than the good of the church"; from depositions Edwards assembled for the council of June 1750 that deal with the timing and publicity of his change of mind;6 and from passages in Samuel Hopkins' Life and Character of the late Reverend Mr. Jonathan Edwards (1765). For example, a long, apologetic paragraph in the Life has to do with Edwards' willingness to suffer financially from his decision, a response, it seems clear, to accusations that he hid his change of mind in order not to jeopardize his large salary. Acknowledging that "beggary as well as disgrace stared Edwards full in the face, if he persisted in his principles," Hopkins went on to say, using almost the very words of the townspeople, that Edwards "had the opportunity and the temptation to escape" these dire consequences "by concealing his principles." But in language that evoked the passion of Christ, Hopkins described his

-- 79 --

mentor as someone who "counted the cost, and deliberately took up the cross…and in direct opposition to all worldly views and motives."7

The "Narrative" is thus a document shaped by the situation in Northampton and perhaps especially by the attacks on Edwards' reputation and his concern for any future "usefulness." In response, he was relentless in arguing that the church and precinct were unreasonable in refusing to debate the theological issue. He also asserted that church members "unlearned" in divinity should respect his authority. As for those members, they voted with virtual unanimity from the start that Edwards could no longer be their pastor unless he returned to his former way of thinking. Of the many signs of their mood, none is more revealing than their refusal to continue receiving the sacrament of the Lord's Supper from his hands. From time to time in New England church history, ministers (including Edwards' father, Timothy, in his East Windsor, Connecticut, parish) refused to administer the sacrament to a congregation or to certain members. Here, the tables were turned. In his Farewell Sermon Edwards turned the tables once again by imagining the fate of his congregation on the day of the Last Judgment.

Conflict over Edwards' reputation erupted anew in May 1751 when a small group of Northampton residents requested the advice of another council on whether they should form themselves into a congregation, with Edwards as the minister. Once again the town produced a "remonstrance" that was "everywhere larded with…sarcastical…insinuations" and that contained "divers direct, grievous and criminal charges."8 Meanwhile the minority on the council of June 1750 had denounced the majority, which responded in kind. Edwards prepared to join in the fray with a public letter to his ally William Hobby, though he apparently decided against publishing it. His critics replied through the newspapers. The scales tipped in Edwards' direction when Joseph Hawley, Jr., a key figure in arguing the town's case before the councils of June 1750 and May 1751, sent him an apologetic letter in 1754. Hawley addressed a second letter of apology in 1760 to the Rev. David Hall of Sutton. Samuel Hopkins made this letter public in his Life of Edwards.9

"Backwards and Forwards": Edwards and the Boundaries of Tradition

Solomon Williams may have misread the Humble Inquiry when he interpreted "credibility" to mean the "highest degree of evidence" and implied that Edwards was akin to the Separates. Yet when other contemporaries read it in ways that departed from Edwards' preferred interpretation, we may suspect that the text contained ambiguities of which he was unaware or, more likely, that he wished to control on his own terms.

Even before the Humble Inquiry came off the press, Edwards was contending with the rumor (which reached Peter Clark in Salem Village) that he had thrown in his lot with the Separates or radical New Lights.1 It was in the interests of his opponents in the ministry to encourage these rumors and to represent him as requiring "sanctifying grace" of candidates for church membership. So the majority of the council that met in 1750 declared in its published report, basing this judgment on a passage in the Humble Inquiry. Chester Williams, a member of the majority, supported this reading of the Humble Inquiry in a letter he published in the Boston Gazette of June 16, 1752. Williams "freely owned" that Edwards was not proposing that a candidate "say he was converted, or godly." Was it not the case, however, that Edwards' alternative to conversion, the "Profession of Godliness," amounted to very nearly the same thing? Williams adduced several pieces of evidence that sustained a reading of profession as signifying "real Sanctification": the passage in the Religious Affections in which Edwards described a profession of the "essentials of Christianity" as "the same as to profess that" someone "experiences those things"; the early pages of the Humble Inquiry, in which Edwards linked profession to "piety of heart"; and language from the May 1750 letter to Peter Clark. On the basis of these statements, Williams concluded that the majority of the council had not misspoken in characterizing the issue in the Northampton controversy.2

-- 81 --

To another group of readers, the Humble Inquiry seemed appropriately radical. Certain Separates interpreted Edwards to be saying that "none have a Right to the Lord's Supper but real Saints, and that there is no half-way Covenant." A Separate Congregationalist-turned-Baptist, Isaac Backus, expressed his satisfaction that Edwards had affirmed the "capital article that the Separate churches were built upon," that baptism in the apostolic church was a "token of…being visibly regenerated." In the words of another leading Separate, Ebenezer Frothingham, the movement "highly esteemed" the Humble Inquiry, especially that passage in which Edwards described visible saintship as "even of that Sort of Saintship which the Saints in Heaven have." Was this not the same as what the Separates were maintaining? Yet it puzzled Frothingham that, having gone this far, Edwards reaffirmed the distinction between the visible and the real and, by doing so, split the category of saint into two. This was to repeat the error of allowing hypocrites into the church, the very error Separates accused traditional Congregationalists of making, and that Edwards himself had condemned in Stoddard. Voicing his confusion, Frothingham wondered if Edwards were someone who wrote "in a misterious Manner, backwards and forwards, about this Visibility"?3

A more moderate reading came from Peter Clark. He was relieved to learn from Edwards directly that he was not "designing to have a pure Church consisting only of true Converts." Once this misunderstanding was removed, and after Clark had read the Humble Inquiry, he told Edwards that he was "at a Loss to apprehend wherein the main ground of the Controversy, between you, and your people lies." He understood Edwards to be saying that the person making a profession could explicitly declare that "he did not think himself Converted," a

-- 82 --

reading Clark based on a statement Edwards made at the outset of the Humble Inquiry, where he declared that persons coming to the sacrament "need not esteem themselves" as such. Were this the case, and were it also true that Edwards was not asking for "Evidence of a Work of Conversion in" candidates "even to their own assurance," Clark could then envisage an "accommodation" between pastor and people on more or less the customary ground of Congregational tradition.4

These diverse readings of the Humble Inquiry remind us that, no less than Peter Clark or Solomon Williams, Edwards was seeking a middle way between extremes. These readings also alert us to changes he was making in the boundaries of the path that tradition had beaten. His revised boundaries come into view when we ask, once again, whether Edwards expected candidates for communion to be real saints; and why he broke with his grandfather's thinking. The correct answer to the first of these involves a two-part statement. Edwards said that the visible church could never know for certain who was truly saved and who was not; because it could not discern an internal work of grace, it had to rely on profession. Yet he went on to declare that the persons making a profession had to "esteem themselves truly gracious." Profession rested on and made visible "the real existence" of what was professed; it had to be sincere, in the sense of testifying to something that was actually there. Edwards rejected out of hand any attempt to qualify the meaning of sincerity and visibility: "The thing which must be visible and probable, in order to visible saintship, must be saintship itself, or real grace and true holiness…and not mere moral sincerity." And he would allow no ambiguity about profession as a sign. His theory of the sign was effectively an antitheory, a rule of morals rather than a rule of language: it was morally right that the sign and what it signified be one and the same and morally wrong for profession or visibility to detach itself from what it signified. He acknowledged that profession differed from "godliness itself," which was "internal and out of sight." But he demanded that profession be explicit, and he insisted that candidates for church membership esteem themselves "gracious persons."5

To misread Edwards as requiring the "highest degree of evidence" and not "credibility," as he accused Solomon Williams of doing, is thus to misread him only slightly. His position must be differentiated from

-- 83 --

that of the Separates in two respects: he never accepted the test of experience (and therefore, contrary to what some modern interpreters have too casually assumed, he never proposed restoring the procedure of a relation); and he rejected the claim that the spiritually enlightened could discern who was a saint. But his position must also be differentiated from the Congregational tradition. In the context of that tradition he was repudiating the customary explanation of the relation between church membership and the individual quest for saving grace. Most of his predecessors in New England understood this relation to be open-ended and inconclusive because of the difference between the external, or federal, covenant that included all the seed, and the internal covenant of grace. For nearly a century, the clergy in New England had used this distinction, together with the elastic catchwords "charity" and "profession," to justify broadening the basis of church membership. They had justified that process for a second reason, their diagnosis of the problem of "weak believers" who lacked assurance of salvation: better, they had argued, that the church include the lame and halting and serve as means of grace to the many than function only as the preserve of the few who achieved assurance of salvation.

Solomon Williams placed himself in this tradition when he quoted the Synod of 1662 on the difference between the highest evidence of sincerity, and the lowest.6 Edwards stepped outside this tradition in manifesting a distaste for charity, in advocating a policy of strictness as the remedy for weak believers, and in limiting the range of meanings encompassed by the act of profession. And although he warded off the Separates and their criterion of experience by clinging to profession, he threw all of his intellectual weight into sharpening the boundary between grace and its simulacres. In so doing, he detached himself from the middle way that so many others had preferred and that, in broad outline, Calvin had articulated in the sixteenth century.

Should it be surprising that, hemmed in as he was, Edwards would seem to readers of the Humble Inquiry both daring and moderate?

Why did he change his mind about the nature of the church? The answer to this question may seem tantalizingly simple: the Great Awakening, or else the Arminian threat. Yet as we have seen, Edwards spent much of the 1740s reiterating a traditional ecclesiology against

-- 84 --

the Separates. In and of itself the Awakening did not precipitate a change of mind. Neither was Arminianism the crucial factor; there is but a single direct reference to Arminianism in the whole of the Religions Affections, the term is peripheral to the Humble Inquiry, and there is no evidence to support his insinuation that Solomon Williams and the people of Northampton were tending toward that frame of mind.

The key lies in the situation in Northampton and in Edwards' life-long concern, which deepened in the aftermath of the Great Awakening, with the difference between true and false spirituality. As his ministry rapidly deteriorated in the aftermath of the bad book affair, Edwards came to think of most of his congregation as hypocrites. In reacting against his congregation, he was also reacting against the intermingling of the social and the religious that constituted popular religion. The intensity of his reaction against what he deemed hypocrisy is evidenced in the sentiment he expressed in the Humble Inquiry: reversing what he once had said, he argued that it were "better, to have a few real living Christians" kept out of the church than to admit any who might be false believers. Here again, he distanced himself from his more pastorally-minded predecessors in the Congregational tradition, the line that stretched back through Increase Mather to John Cotton.7

What Edwards did in the Humble Inquiry was to connect his reaction against the townspeople with the psychology of religion he laid out in the Religious Affections. A decade of experience and reflection had persuaded him anew of the evangelical premise that the saints were truly different from the unregenerate in their inner and outer lives. Convinced of the reality of difference, he wanted to restore the connections between this difference and the traditional practices of Congregationalism, the acts of professing the covenant and participating in the two sacraments. By the end of the 1740s he was blaming the collapse of the revival on the failure to maintain those connections—that is, the failure to force the issue between the "precious" and the "vile" against any counterforces, be these popular religion, pride, Arminianism, the pastoral issue of perplexity, or the Congregational tradition. So he declared in the preface he contributed to Joseph Bellamy's True Religion Delineated (1750),8 and so he said repeatedly to his Scottish correspondents. Ever more disenchanted with "that bastard,

-- 85 --

mischievous charity, by which Satan keeps men asleep,"9 he turned to the instrument of explicit and sincere profession, profession that could flow only from the affective, willing, and sincere self. Thus he brought together what his grandfather had decided were better kept apart, the capacity to judge between true and false piety and admission to the church.

Edwards placed his decision in the context of an ever-threatening "triumph of the open enemies of religion."1 Historians must step outside this rhetoric and reclaim the full spectrum of opinion among evangelicals in the 1740s, a spectrum that encompasses Elisha and Solomon Williams and the very many others who shared with them a different understanding of the visible church and how it should function. It is equally vital that historians step outside Edwards' hostile judgment of the people of Northampton. Stoddardeanism did not nun the townspeople into hypocrites; if anything, scrupulosity persisted in Northampton under Stoddard. Neither did they pass suddenly from sincerity to hypocrisy in the 1740s. The structures of popular religion were well in place before the revivals and outlasted them. Only if we adopt a wider understanding of religion, culture, and society in the eighteenth century do we grasp the nature of the politics that transformed the question of access to baptism into the controversy that cost Edwards his pulpit.

From start to finish the Northampton controversy unfolded within a relatively narrow range of terms and categories. We look in vain in the Humble Inquiry for a statement of Edwards' broader vision of the church. He explored the objective glory of God and the progress of the elect toward the kingdom in the "Miscellanies," in the sermons published long after his death as The History of the Work of Redemption, and in An Humble Attempt to Promote Union in Prayer (1748).2 But the struggle in Northampton was rooted in circumstances peculiar to that town and church, to the revivals of the 1740s, and to the Congregational tradition as it had developed in New England. Edwards as a philosophical theologian transcended these circumstances. The treatises contained in this volume cannot but remind us that he was also a person of his time and place.

Legacies of the Northampton Controversy

The two treatises in which Edwards explained and defended his understanding of the church were not reprinted in America in the eighteenth century.3 Copies of the Humble Inquiry were already hard to come by in 1750, or so Peter Clark complained to Edwards. In the initial decades their influence seems to have been as limited as their circulation, for Joseph Bellamy and Edward Billings were among the few who advanced the Edwardsean program. Thereafter, the pace of debate began to pick up.4 How these debates played themselves out—in what ways Edwards was invoked, in what ways new issues were introduced—has yet to be charted by historians with any thoroughness. In particular, we know almost nothing of the politics and social sources of the movement to reject the halfway covenant, a movement that, by the early nineteenth century, had gained much ground within Congregationalism. But the triumph of the purists was short-lived, for the liberalism that came in with Horace Bushnell broke entirely with the issues of covenant, sincerity, and assurance that had dominated the Congregational tradition for two centuries. As the tide of strictness weakened and began to ebb, the treatises that some had once looked on as weapons against false religion slipped from view. In the first half of the twentieth century they were passed over by anthologists and ignored by those commentators bent on recovering Edwards as philosophical theologian.

Note on the Texts

The texts presented here are replicated from the first editions of A Letter, Humble Inquiry, and Misrepresentations. Lists of errata accompanying the original texts have been followed. The copy of A Letter in the collection of the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library, Yale University, is William Williams' presentation copy to Edward Wigglesworth. Errata in this copy corrected in pen by Williams have been incorporated into this edition. These three books were written, prepared,

-- 87 --

and copy-edited by Edwards himself (in the latter stages of the final two assisted by his friend and literary agent, the Reverend Thomas Foxcroft of Boston). The text of Edwards' "Narrative," described below, presents its own set of problems quite distinct from these other works.

In addition to the editorial guidelines outlined at the beginning of this volume, an effort has been made in this edition to preserve original punctuation. However, certain emendations had to be made owing to the idiosyncrasies of eighteenth-century printing. Double punctuation is been eliminated. Some commas have been added, such as before and after page references, book titles, and dates as needed. Also, where the original uses brackets as parentheses, these have been changed to parentheses to avoid confusion with editorial insertions. Abbreviated names (for example, "Mr. B.," "Mr. W——ms," or "J.E.") are spelled out without comment to prevent the unnecessary proliferation of brackets.

There are several manuscript notebooks that contain notes and drafts for the Humble Inquiry and Misrepresentations. In the Edwards Papers at the Beinecke Library (f. 41.4) is a "working" notebook labeled by Edwards as "Sacrament Book I" that contains rough notes towards the Inquiry. It is a small quarto-sized book composed mostly of salvaged letter covers, stitched into a cover of stiff paper. There were at least four such books at one time, since in this remaining one he refers (p. 2) to "B. IV. p. 83." The pages are numbered 1, 40, 21, 6–7, 10–13, 20–25, 28–35, 38–47, and 70, reflecting Edwards' practice of tearing out pages that were no longer of any use to him. All of the entries in the book, which vary in length from just a few words to several pages, are deleted with a vertical line, or "use mark," a method Edwards employed to indicate he had either made use of an entry or had decided not to use it. The entries treat a variety of subjects, for example, public profession, observations on Old Testament practices, John the Baptist, the parables of the ten virgins and of the wedding garment, the covenant, rules for judging who is converted, and notes on objections to and the "method," or organization, of the treatise. There is even a list of transitional "Expressions" that Edwards records tor later use.

The preached version, most likely written from notes for the Inquiry either concurrent with or shortly after the completion of the treatise itself, is extant as a lengthy lecture series on Ezekiel 44:9. This discourse, in three booklets totaling 225 pages in length, is Edwards'

-- 88 --

defense of his views that he delivered at Northampton from February 15 to March 22, 1750. The first booklet (42 pages) is located at the Beinecke Library, and the remaining ones are at the Franklin Trask Library, Andover Newton Theological School (f. ND5.9). Based on the same doctrine as the treatise, the lecture series is essentially the same as the printed work, though some points have been dropped or added, and there is necessarily some condensing. In addition, where the treatise downplays from a theoretical perspective the ability of saints to discern true faith in others, in the lecture Edwards seems to argue for that ability for practical reasons.

An advanced, possibly penultimate, draft of Misrepresentations is located at the Beinecke Library (f. 29); it is the only known complete draft of a treatise prepared for the press and published by Edwards in existence. Two quarto-sized books, bound in coarse brown paper and labeled by Edwards, "Answer to Williams—Printed," contain the draft. (A later owner of the manuscripts penciled the inscriptions "Bk. 3" and "Bk. 4" on the covers, but Edwards himself never identified them as such.) The first book, paginated 1–96 by Edwards, with many odd-sized scraps tipped in for additions, contains part I, 1 through part III, 1, and the second, numbered 1–84, has the remainder of the treatise as well as the appended letter to the Northampton church, and concludes with the preface. Comparisons of this draft with the printed work reveal that there are differences between the two, consisting of the reordering of passages as well as additions, deletions, and differences in wording.

Edwards' manuscript of his "Narrative" no longer exists, but a transcription of 136 quarto pages prepared by one of Sereno Dwight's amanuenses is in the Trask Library. Dwight printed what he called Edwards' "Journal" in the first volume of his edition of 1829 (pp. 313–98). The text used here is that of the Andover copyist, collated with Dwight's edition at the points where the copy has lacunae. Upon inspection, the copy is seen to preserve more aspects of Edwards' style, such as characteristic contractions, idioms, expressions, and mixing of past and present tenses and singular and plural subjects, whereas Dwight's text has evidence of free interpolation. Two fragments from the "Narrative" in Edwards' hand (Beinecke Library, f. 46) have been used at the appropriate places and indicated in the notes. Drafts of letters in Edwards' hand that he includes in the "Narrative" have also been used for verification where available.

Dwight did not have access to a number of documents that Edwards

meant to insert but did not at the time the "Narrative" was written. They include the result of the previous council, a letter by Edwards to Noah Cook and another to the Reverend Samuel Hopkins of West Springfield, letters from the Lower Hampshire Association to the Northampton church and to Edwards, and Peter Clark's letter to the Northampton church committee. The documents, printed here for the first time, are inserted at their proper places. Only one of the items Edwards intended to include—the recommendations of the council of February 7, 1750—has not been located.
