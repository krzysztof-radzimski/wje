3. Reception, Influence, and Interpretation of A History of the Work of Redemption
Introduction

To turn from questions about the composition, text, and structure of Jonathan Edwards' Redemption Discourse to the issue of the reception accorded A History of the Work of Redemption and consideration of its subsequent influence is a dramatic shift. The first questions are largely certain and essentially determinate, while the others are typically problematic and unbounded. When there remain serious questions about a text, or problems in reconstructing techniques of composition, we can stipulate what evidence would put us in a position to resolve an issue, or at least to define it correctly. The question of influence is far more subtle, and must be framed in different ways depending, for example, upon whether the type of writing and the kind of influence are technical or popular.

In the case of his technical theological writing, Edwards frequently cast his work in polemical terms. As a polemicist, he mercilessly attacked a position or an individual whose writings brought a particular issue into focus. Thus his discussion of freedom of the will was basically cast as a refutation of contemporary Arminianism and was a sustained criticism of Thomas Chubb, Daniel Whitby, and Isaac Watts.5 Similarly his analysis of original sin was organized as a sustained

-- 080 --

refutation of John Taylor's Scripture-Doctrine of Original Sin.6 Of Edwards' major treatises, only his discussion of the Religious Affections was not explicitly structured by reflection upon and disagreement with contemporaries' positions, although it took account of them implicitly.7 Of course, shorter pieces, like selected entries in the "Miscellanies" and several longer essays like those posthumously published as Two Dissertations, were also much less polemical than such tracts as Freedom of the Will and Original Sin. Furthermore, Edwards' protégés often developed his points, or his defenders and detractors engaged in continued exchanges. Indeed, the development of the technical points of Edwardsean theology across several generations provided the subject for Frank Hugh Foster's ambitious A Genetic History of the New England Theology, modeled on German historicist scholarship.8 Foster was especially successful in tracing the extension of controversy and was thus able to chronicle, to some degree, the influence of Edwards and his work. We have, therefore, a means to assess Edwards' influence as it derived from his writings as a controversialist.

No such possibilities exist with respect to assessing Edwards' influence upon popular religious culture. Here his writings were on the whole very much less explicitly shaped as polemical exchanges. Most were derived from sermons. In his actual preaching Edwards did not fail to address "hard sayings" to those potentially without faith, but the sermons were in general developed in terms of the structural pattern of text, doctrine, and application devoted to building up the faithful. Accordingly, the medium in which A History of the Work of Redemption was cast removed it from the kind of attention that might have led to responses or placed it at the center of controversy.

Additional difficulty in tracing the influence of Work of Redemption arises from the accidents of its presentation to the public. It was developed as a series of sermons extending over half a year. There is no evidence that Edwards preached any of these thirty sermons again, individually or in subsets. Occasional visitors may have been present at one or even several of the particular occasions on which the lecture-discourses were given. But it is most improbable that, for

-- 081 --

example, any other clergyman heard many (or even any) of them. It is possible that a student (living in the Edwards household while studying divinity) might have been present for the series, but if so, no records or reports have been located.9 There is no conclusive evidence that a protégé like Samuel Hopkins (or indeed Joseph Bellamy) read the manuscript sermons. Jonathan Edwards, Jr., who transcribed them, may have been the first to read them—and that well after his father's death.

Furthermore, the Redemption Discourse was finally published some thirty-five years after its initial presentation from the pulpit, and approximately fifteen years after its author's death. At that point the initiative for issuing the sermons came from John Erskine, the New-World Edwardseans having failed to discover sufficient interest in publication of even selected literary remains of Edwards to make this much larger project feasible. The intervening period was also marked by decisive events—frontier violence as a part of the Seven Years' War, tariff struggles with England, controversies over political questions, the possibility of resistance to the English crown. No doubt in that kind of world the sermon series seemed far removed in cultural space as well as in time from the issues of moment in the last quarter of the eighteenth century. These factors (we should not exclude others) help to explain why there was a general lack of attention to—indeed a lack of interest in—Edwards' writings from shortly after his unexpected death. These circumstances affected reception of Work of Redemption at least as much as they did the other writings published in his lifetime or shortly thereafter. It is difficult, if not impossible, to isolate the influence of Work of Redemption from the question of Edwardsean influence generally.

In the context of the broader Edwardsean legacy, Edwards' specific emphasis upon the Work of Redemption experienced a variety of fates. With respect to elite culture, little was done by his followers and protégés to continue his theological preoccupation with redemption as the grand and encompassing doctrine of the Christian religion. At most Edwards' interest in the millennium received renewed attention in the 1790s, and this was but a part or aspect of the broader redemption theme. Where explicit theological responses occurred in England they were largely critical (not to say hostile) in circles beyond

-- 082 --

the evangelical camp that found his position congenial. Interestingly enough, more secular figures like George Bancroft and Harriet Beecher Stowe recognized, at a somewhat later date, that Work of Redemption had both set the framework for American collective self-understanding and fostered the remarkable flowering of religious and theological discussion of the nineteenth century.

With respect to popular culture, on the other hand, A History of the Work of Redemption clearly had enormous influence. It was a manual of Calvinistic theology suited for lay readers and popular preachers. Here it influenced in a general way the millenarianism which was diffused throughout the culture. But it also nurtured the numerous religious groups, movements, and subcultures making claims to instant or living redemption that so flourished in the antebellum years. It may not be too much to suggest that Edwards' history was as influential as any other single book in fixing the cultural parameters of nineteenth-century American Protestant culture. It securely anchored American experience in a cosmic setting, locating it by means of reference to sacred Scripture and investing it with preeminent significance for concluding the drama of Christian redemption. It legitimated the social experiment that was the new American culture.

Work of Redemption made a mark in four different stages or separate settings. First, an immediate consequence of Edwards' virtual exile at Stockbridge and early death after moving to New Jersey was the contraction of debate and discussion about his work and a general diminution of his influence. Close associates like Joseph Bellamy and Samuel Hopkins (to identify certainly the most prominent clergymen who had personal ties to him) continued in close contact with him throughout the Stockbridge years (1750–57). It is also clear that these men deferred to him and looked to him as their intellectual mentor. Furthermore, he seems to have cultivated and maintained correspondence with individuals in the Middle Colonies (a prominent example being Aaron Burr, who wooed and married his daughter Esther) as well as in Scotland (where a figure as prominent as John Erskine clearly looked to him as a significant theological figure). But the kind of leadership Edwards continued to exercise in the last decade of his life was much less focused and effective in political and ecclesiastical terms than the role he had played earlier while in Northampton. There, through the Hampshire Association, he had represented Connecticut Valley religious interests against the Bay region and had emerged as spokesman for—while yet a discriminating

-- 083 --

critic of the excesses manifested in—the Great Awakening. His social and political influence waned substantially in the last decade of his life, even if his powers as a controversialist, in more strictly theological circles, were at their height. At no point in this period did the subject of the Redemption Discourse receive extensive attention.

In the years after Edwards' death, his work had its effect in two different areas, namely, the technical (theological) and the popular. Among theologians, he set the terms in which discussions, debates, and controversies moved toward progressive refinement of certain positions, for example, the freedom of the will. He defined the issues from which friends and foes alike began to argue out positions both nearer to and further from his own. But in this process his genius seems to take leave of the discussion. His way with a disputed point, like the terms in which the will is free, embodied an openness and vitality that marked it off from the narrowly conceived technical discussions in which his successors elaborated it. Something of the same constriction can be seen when Samuel Hopkins and Joseph Bellamy discuss the millennium by focusing on a particular question or questions to the neglect of the cosmic framework of redemption which marked Edwards' consideration of the matter. So in the narrower and technical sense, the Edwardsean legacy tended to be diminished even while being carried forward in the New Divinity Movement.

Simultaneously, in its setting at the more popular level, the grand theme of God's Work of Redemption underwent another development. The broader program of revitalization that Edwards sought to root in the correct understanding of the divine ground and source of all things became so diffused that it scarcely retained any connections with its sources in his thought. By contrast with the increasingly arcane, even precious, discussions over technical points among ecclesiastics, the impulse he had done so much to initiate soon lost any particular identification with him and took on a life of its own as a virtually Lockean "new simple idea" of America, in which many innovative things were possible, and most things could flourish. It is very important to recognize this bifurcation between the two settings in which Edwards' influence developed. One was an increasingly restricted and technical legacy explicitly descending from him, while the other was a much broader cultural influence that received decisive impetus from his initiatives (and which appropriated his thought to issues and contexts he would scarcely have recognized, let alone approved). The distinction helps to explain why Edwards remains so

-- 084 --

significant for American culture even while he seems to fade and disappear from it in the course of the nineteenth century.1

Edwards' self-proclaimed technical followers dealt in a version of his position so close to caricature that it would cease to be identified with him. Only someone as knowledgeable as Harriet Beecher Stowe would be able to identify the true extent of his influence as "a man who united in himself the natures of both a poet and a metaphysician, all whose experiences and feelings were as much more intense than those of common men as Dante's or Milton's."2 She even interpreted those who radically rejected the New England system he sought to perfect—for example, the transcendentalists—as no less formed by his casting of the issues, a casting which had as one of its consequences the appearance "in the very bosom of the New England Church [of] a set of young people who were not merely indifferent to religion, but who hated it with the whole energy of their being."3 Henry May conjectures that Perry Miller's insight about the connection between Edwards' thought and even the pantheistic heresies of Ralph Waldo Emerson and Theodore Parker may have been derived from Harriet Beecher Stowe's keen understanding.4 The one shortcoming of this interpretation is that it deals with the question of his influence in high or literate culture only, failing to focus upon the depth and breadth to which he released "the whole waters of discussion."5 Beyond Emerson and Parker, Edwards' imprint can be seen on religious figures as diverse as John Humphrey Noyes, Joseph Smith, Jr., and Mary Baker Eddy. It is clear, therefore, that not only did his influence pass beyond strictly religious circles into the culture at large, but also that, even within those circles, it reached far beyond the New Divinity party.

Yet this diffusion of Edwards' legacy (as noted earlier) dissipated it so broadly that distinctive Edwardsean traits ceased to be obvious. It is extraordinary that while continued efforts were made to reissue his works, indeed to issue additional sermons and transcriptions of manuscripts, and even to begin inclusive collections, his direct influence declined. He must be counted obscure and little known, except

-- 085 --

perhaps as a negative reference point, from the mid-nineteenth century at least until after the First World War. And even then such attention as was given to him, for example, in Vernon L. Parrington's review of American literature, amounted to a perfection of the caricature invited by his protégés.6

The fourth and final stage or setting is renewed appreciation for Edwards' stature. It has come only in the course of the twentieth century and has derived from several sources. One was the germination of systematic interest in the American tradition of philosophy. Herbert Wallace Schneider in particular established a context and major lines of development for interpreting philosophy in colonial America. In this Edwards inevitably loomed large.7 A second source was in more strictly theological circles. The brilliant exposition by H. Richard Niebuhr in The Kingdom of God in America pointed to Edwards' central role in the distinctive development of American theology.8 More important yet, Perry Miller's work on colonial intellectual history necessarily singled him out as culmination and transformer of his own culture.9 Of course, numerous other scholars contributed to this renewed appreciation of Edwards; among the early studies were Joseph Haroutunian's Piety versus Moralism and Ola Winslow's painstaking biography, as well as the unusually durable collection of readings (with a fine introduction) issued by Clarence Faust and Thomas Johnson.1 By the mid-twentieth century, Edwards had emerged from a long period of scholarly neglect. This neglect had started with his untimely death and the frustration of so many early projects to publish his writings posthumously, but it was fixed by the great separation between his influence in technical and in

-- 086 --

popular circles. What will be evident is that, however much Edwards may have considered his Redemption project to be the sum and substance of his theological program, the Redemption Discourse as published was never received in those terms.

Critical Reception Of A History Of The Work Of Redemption

While Work of Redemption had its greatest influence in popular circles in the nineteenth century, from the year of its publication some writers criticized it extensively. Soon after the book appeared, the Monthly Review carried an anonymous essay which suggests the British cultural elite's view of Edwards' explication of the Work of Redemption.2 The review began with sarcastic reference to the subtitle of the book: "In a method entirely new!"


With respect to these outlines of a new body of divinity, as something will doubtless be expected from us concerning the Work, we must declare it—a long, laboured, dull, confused rhapsody; and far from being in a method entirely new, it is merely an attempt to revive the old mystical divinity that distracted the last age with pious conundrums: and which, having, long ago, emigrated to America, we have no reason to wish should ever be imported back again.3

The review clearly identified apocalyptic speculation as central to Edwards' work: "He has enriched his history with every thing that could be suggested by the most unbridled imagination."4 It also took him to task for interpolations in his treatment of the scriptural account, seeing him as an "intoxicated visionary presuming to see the will of God." The review pronounced that, "When a visionary has once sufficiently intoxicated himself with pious reveries, it is wonderful to observe how important he becomes and how presumptuously familiar with his Maker!"5 The review went on to chastise Edwards as "irreverent" for his predictions about the fulfillment of divine intentions in contingent events.6 In addition, it taxed him with "extravagances," for instance, in providing details about the life of Christ

-- 087 --

that did not exist in Scripture. Finally, he was pronounced a "poor departed enthusiast" and his treatise "nonsense."7

The author of this review has not been identified—and that is not important for our purposes. For the response does make clear just how anachronistic A History of the Work of Redemption appeared in the more sophisticated cultural climate of London. (It may also be one index of the lack of sympathy for colonials on the eve of their war for independence.) In fact, it is noteworthy that such an extensive discussion was printed. Perhaps it was as much intended to be an indirect critique of Erskine and the Scottish evangelicals (who were more sympathetic to the colonials) as it was a review of Edwards' posthumously published sermon series. There does not appear to have been, however, a comparable response on the other side of the Atlantic, either to the initial publication or to reprintings.

Another explicit discussion of Work of Redemption, while critical, is evidence for the more general influence the book had achieved in the popular religious culture within a half century. Edward W. Grinfield published The Nature and Extent of the Christian Dispensation with Reference to the Salvability of the Heathen in 1827.8 His book was dedicated to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts and undertook to provide a theological basis for Christian missionary activities: "It is the general object and design of the present work to shew, that the principles of Christianity, as a divine dispensation, coincide with the acknowledged principles of moral science."9 In particular, Grinfield argued that "Christ, in his office, as our Creator, Redeemer, and Judge, bears the same universal and impartial relation to all mankind, whether they are acquainted or not with the history of our redemption."1

Grinfield divided his tract into five parts—which in itself begins to suggest the emergence of the dispensationalist framework that, once developed, was soon extended to popular circles.2 He used this format to summarize the changing administration of divine justice through the stages of the world's history. His first period was the antediluvian, the second the patriarchal, and the third "the Jewish economy." The fourth concerned the life of Christ. Grinfield proposed that "his character

-- 088 --

and mission have a universal reference to the human race."3 Finally, as the last stage, the apostolic writings were interpreted as warranting the salvability of the heathen. He argued for the universalism of Christianity in the following terms: "I would hope, that hereafter it will be no more doubted, that the general system of doctrine which they exhibit has a universal reference to the human race; than it is now doubted, that the principles of the Newtonian philosophy hold good to the world at large, whether believed in or not by the majority of mankind."4

In connection with offering this justification for mission activity, Grinfield felt moved to add an appendix to his treatise which responded explicitly to Edwards' more traditional and orthodox position. He characterized Work of Redemption as "one of the most popular manuals of Calvinistic Theology," and noted that it was "peculiarly adapted for contrast with our argument, as it so nearly corresponds in its formal divisions, whilst it totally differs in its object and tendency."5 Grinfield reviewed Edwards' argument in some thirty pages, disagreeing with him on particular points, but seeking to appropriate his universal framework of redemption as the fundamental work of God to his own position on the "salvability of the heathen."

Grinfield's argument need not concern us in detail. His extensive discussion of Work of Redemption does, however, point to two noteworthy issues. First, the use of the book as a foil indicates that it had become widely used as a guide to "Calvinistic Theology." This evidence confirms that its great effect and influence was, as John Erskine had intended, within the popular culture and thus upon common thought and experience. Doubtless it was staple reading for clergy, and its ideas had a continuing life on both sides of the Atlantic in sermons rather than essays, or in discussions rather than in literary codifications. It may also have served laity and their reading interests well, for it explained a cultural world even as it helped to bring it into existence and sustain it.6

-- 089 --

Another issue is more technical. There is no doubt that professedly Edwards moved wholly within Calvinism—that is, his scenario for the end of history anticipated a last judgment, the destruction of the wicked, and an end to this world. So in one sense Christ's atonement was limited in its effect. Yet beside these grim notes he sounded the theme that redemption would reconcile the whole creation to God as the means of his glorification. In this he displayed an incipient universalism that appears more as a tone of exultation and rejoicing than as a clear claim, at least in his published works. This suggests that Grinfield, perhaps unbeknownst to himself, while overtly criticizing Edwards' position, was simultaneously attempting to draw out implications from Work of Redemption, implications that existed as theological possibilities more than as developed points.

A third, though brief, explicit discussion of Work of Redemption, first generally published in 1834, made even higher claims for its importance, while at the same time suggesting how a more secular and national reading of its significance had developed in the new United States. At the conclusion of the third volume of his History of the United States (which marked the summary of his discussion of the era of colonization), George Bancroft permitted himself to reflect upon the deeper import and meaning of historiography.7 He argued that "exact observation" is necessary for the "historic enquirer" but that the true meaning of the activity lies "behind" the observations: "Facts faithfully ascertained, and placed in proper contiguity, become of themselves the firm links of a brightly burnished chain, connecting events with their causes, and marking the line along which the electric power of truth is conveyed from generation to generation."8 His point is that "historic truth" should "establish itself as a science." When it does, it will "become the highest demonstration of the superintending providence of God."9

This striking position leads Bancroft to affirm a belief in "progress in human affairs." Indeed, he sees universal history as an attempt "to relate 'the sum of all God's works of providence,'" and in so quoting Edwards credits him with this decisive formulation.


In America, the first conception of its office, in the mind of Jonathan Edwards, though still cramped and perverted by theological

-- 090 --

forms not derived from observation, was nobler than the theory of Vico: more grand and general than the method of Bossuet, it embraced in its outline the whole "work of redemption,"—the history of the influence of all moral truth in the gradual regeneration of humanity.1

No doubt Bancroft was in part being autobiographical in tracing to Edwards his own discovery of an inclusive conception to undergird his conviction that the birth of the American nation was a decisive event in world history. His testimony to Edwards' influence should certainly be joined to that of Harriet Beecher Stowe. Bancroft insists that a transposition occurred; Edwards' idea had its real significance in a context not his own. His theological approach had to give way, Bancroft suggests, to the science of history. So both Bancroft and Stowe support the more fundamental point that the decisive influence of Work of Redemption appears as it is transformed in its contribution to nineteenth-century American culture. We might even argue that in this transformation it was fundamentally secularized.

A History Of The Work Of Redemption And Nineteenth-Century Religion In America

To discuss the secularization of Edwards' influence is certainly beyond the scope of this edition of Work of Redemption. He had, however, another kind of influence that must be reviewed. This was the direct or indirect effect of his embracing conception of the divine redemption of the world on more strictly popular religious developments, among them millenarianism.

For the last quarter century, the subject of millenarianism in the development of nineteenth-century American culture has drawn extensive scholarly inquiry. At the center of this inquiry is the great division that occurred in thinking about the millennium in the nineteenth-century American context—a division which produced two separate subcultures. The expressed issue separating these parties was narrow and theological, namely whether a literal return of Christ was expected before the last judgment and the ensuing millennium, or whether the return would come spiritually in the course of the millennial period. This distinction is connected to markedly different conceptions of history, the nature of politics, and the place of religion in American society.

-- 091 --

Adherents of the premillennial scheme, who expected Christ to return and reign more or less literally before the last judgment, took a dark view of human nature, emphasizing the role of evil in the world and the necessity for divine intervention to achieve its redemption. Such beliefs appealed to those unsympathetic to and critical of modern culture, whether expressed in a rational form derived from the Enlightenment or in an antirationalist Romantic version. This subculture or party tended to be populist, and those who espoused it were critical of high culture and its secularizing trends.

The alternative subculture had affinities to modern notions of progress. The millennium, as the summary period of human history, was imagined as developing out of prior epochs, serving as their culmination. There would be no radical transformation of the world because none would be needed. The return of Christ, if such were required, would come during or even at the end of the millennium. In strict terms, the return of Christ would be postmillennial. The links between this position and both rational and antirational forms of modernism are clear.

How does Jonathan Edwards, and more specifically his Work of Redemption, relate to this division within nineteenth-century American religion and culture? In an article published in 1959, Clarence C. Goen claimed that Edwards bore a particular responsibility for the development of the postmillenarian or millennialist position.2 Indeed, in Goen's view, especially through the influence of Work of Redemption, Edwards himself began the new and progressive interpretation of the millennium so important in the nineteenth century.

Serious questions can be raised with respect to the particular claim that Goen advanced. It is not at all clear that prior to Edwards interpretations of the millennium within Puritanism (either in England or America) were rigidly premillenarian in the way Goen supposed. Rather, one of the characteristics of millenarianism in seventeenth-century England, and later in the Colonies, was equivocation on just this point.3 The two claims as to whether Christ would physically

-- 092 --

return before or after the millennium featured more as complementary moods than mutually exclusive propositions in the literature of those centuries. Depending upon context and purpose, the same clerical figure, or contemporary figures in both the Old World and the New, could construe the millennium in ways that suggested one or the other without excluding the alternative. In many respects Edwards seems to continue to give voice to this equivocation, although he clearly emphasized the postmillenarian version.

It is also not so evident that the distinction between pre- and post-millennialism which became current and led to alternative and (increasingly) mutually exclusive visions of the millennium derived so directly from Edwards. Probably this distinction became fully operative as a result of the revival of interest in eschatology that occurred in the 1790s. This revival has been summarized usefully by Ruth H. Bloch in a study titled Visionary Republic.4 Her sixth chapter, "Exegesis," concerns what she terms the "eschatological revival of the 1790's," and notes the singular renewal of interest in such themes that took place in that decade. It was, for instance, marked by the republication of A History of the Work of Redemption, as well as by the reprinting of Joseph Bellamy's 1758 work on the millennium and the issuing of Samuel Hopkins' A Treatise on the Millennium—and also by actions as bizarre as David Austin's actual preparation for the great event.5 Contemporary treatises by other authors indicate the scope of interest in this issue in the very early years of the new nation. Rather than the work of one or more writers, it was probably the extraordinary general interest in the millennium during this decade that gave currency to the alternatives of post- and premillenarianism.6

-- 093 --

Goen's article can be criticized for its specific claims, but it served a catalyst for a number of studies that explored the important theme of millenarianism as it developed out of the Great Awakening and into the period of the Revolution. Nathan O. Hatch has traced the evolution among the clerical generation of the war era of an emerging "civil millenarianism" that formed a bridge from religious conviction to political action. Alan Heimert made a suggestion with yet longer-term implications that the yearning for the millennium that was expressed in the Great Awakening issued finally in Jacksonian democracy.7 Goen's posing of the problem thus offered a way to trace religious impulses of the Great Awakening into the revolutionary epoch and beyond; eschatological yearnings were seen to translate into the progressive achievements of the political order.

This literature and the subject underlying it both signal the influence of Work of Redemption in the religious and cultural development of the new republic. Its publication in the 1770s helped to fuel the transference of religious convictions into the political realm that became so important with the war effort. Edwards seemed to be speaking from the grave, even though he could not return to participate in the struggle in person. Of course, Work of Redemption spoke in a religious idiom. It portrayed God's people, revitalized through the Spirit, as a part of God's work through Christ to redeem the entire creation. From its perspective, the founding of the American republic could be at best a foreshadowing of the renewal of God's people at the millennium. While the Work of Redemption would not be completed in America, for after the last judgment God's people could expect to be taken up to heaven with Christ, the young republic might be the setting for a new life of the Spirit in the same way that the Roman empire was the context for the first bodily coming of Christ. Viewed in mundane terms, republic and empire alike might be seen as instrumental with respect to development of the spiritual realm. But seen in the framework of divine redemption of the world, of course, each would appear as but a secondary setting for a decisive infusion of the Spirit.

-- 094 --

In this context, the cultural effect of Work of Redemption was to legitimate and foster popular expressions of religious spirituality that ranged sufficiently widely to compass the Shakers as they developed Ann Lee's visions,8 Joseph Smith, Jr., and the Latter-day Saints, John Humphrey Noyes and his Perfectionists, increasingly strident dispensationalists who marked out special administrations of divine agency in specific periods of redemption, and even the remarkable Mary Baker Eddy with her claims to transcendental healing. One unfortunate by-product of emphasis upon the two versions of millenarianism has been to deflect attention from the more general and profound religious themes which Edwards' writings had a part in shaping and which coursed through the popular religious culture of the nineteenth century. In this area the impact of his popular treatise on Calvinism deserves sustained and imaginative attention.

Modern Appreciation For A History Of The Work Of Redemption

In the course of the last fifty years Edwards, and especially his Work of Redemption, have received critical recognition for their significance in American culture. No introduction to a new edition of that book would be complete without at least a brief discussion of how the magnum opus that he envisioned but never achieved has been interpreted in Edwards' reclamation from obscurity.

In his 1930 study, The Puritan Mind, Herbert Wallace Schneider indicated, albeit in brief compass, how Work of Redemption could be interpreted in terms of philosophical idealism so as to understand its extraordinary achievement and power: "In the very structure of being, God is seen to exhibit his sovereignty, and, in the philosophic heavens, the Holy Commonwealth exists eternally. Thus the social idealism which New England had rejected is sublimated and enshrined in the changeless structure of an idealistic metaphysics."9 Schneider did very little to amplify this interpretation, but it suggests how modern critical perspectives could find beneath Edwards' dramatization

-- 095 --

of the moral life a profound program in idealism that was at one with his more strictly philosophical interests.

Fuller attention was given to Work of Redemption in a quite remarkable monograph by H. Richard Niebuhr, The Kingdom of God in America. This was a supplement and sequel to The Social Sources of Denominationalism, the thesis of which was to examine "the influence of social forces on faith … by tracing the sociological pattern of race, class and sectional interests as it manifested itself in the denominations."1 In The Kingdom of God in America Niebuhr sought to deal with American Christianity as a religious movement in terms of the ideal of the kingdom of God coming to earth. In this perspective he traced the concept's development from its first appearance in early Protestantism, through its establishment in Puritanism, to its transformation in the late colonial era and the new republic, and finally into the social programs of the nineteenth century. The details of Niebuhr's overall argument are less significant for our current purpose than the role he assigned to Edwards, and especially the Edwards who spoke of redemption and framed it as the basic doctrine in Work of Redemption. Edwards and the Great Awakening were for Niebuhr the bridge by which the earlier religious emphasis on divine sovereignty passed over into an impulse to transform the common life in the nineteenth century. Thus "the coming kingdom understood as the apocalypse of the divine sovereignty was the fruition not only of divine goodness but of human badness in conflict with that unconquerable goodness."2 In this way, though treating it under the symbol of the kingdom, Niebuhr installed Edwards' theme of God's redemption of the world as at once the core of the Christian movement in America and the central meaning and significance of the culture.

While neither Schneider nor Niebuhr devoted extensive attention to interpretation of Work of Redemption, each in his own fashion retrieved it from its obscurity, at least for the elite culture. Both also placed it at the center of Edwards' life's work, even as Edwards was installed at the center of the story of the predominantly Protestant culture of America. The one emphasized his philosophical idealism, the other his theological program. But each interpreter directed attention to Work of Redemption as the foundation of Edwards' contribution

-- 096 --

to American cultural development. More explicit interpretation of Work of Redemption came in the post-World War II era.

However it is finally evaluated, Perry Miller's intellectual biography of Edwards signals the broader rehabilitation his reputation received at mid-twentieth century. Miller made of Edwards a solitary frontier figure who nonetheless wrestled with and finally made his own the elements of modernity which would be so consequential in the next several centuries. The biography is a bold and heroic delineation of that life of the mind which Miller himself supremely appreciated and found so rarely embodied. In a sense Edwards became his alter ego in relation to the tradition he plumbed so deeply. Of all Miller's chapters which treated Edwards' appropriation of the modern world taking shape in his own time, the last one, "History," can be considered the most problematic.

Miller's claim was that had Edwards lived to work through and publish the "history" of which he wrote to the trustees at Princeton, "he would … have furnished America with the first glimmerings of historical method."3 This was a reflection and extension of Bancroft's judgment. In Miller's view, this achievement would have ranked with Edwards' more readily acknowledged appropriation of Locke and his at least partial understanding of Newton—to refer to the kind of issue Miller used to index Edwards' modernity. Miller continued his discussion of Work of Redemption: "Even though lacking the scholarship to support it, [it] would still have been, in the reckoning of today, an immense enrichment of the intellectual heritage of the nation."4

Miller's argument was that while the "surface narrative" of Work of Redemption "sounds like a story book for fundamentalists," at a deeper level it recognized the elementary truth that data must be interpreted.5 Certainly Edwards followed through on this insight by adopting a Scripture-derived framework of interpretation. But in Miller's view "the real thesis of the History of Redemption is the unity of history."6 Miller certainly acknowledges its provincial cast: "It is the history of Northampton writ large," he says at one point. But Edwards brought his town to the very portals of the millennium.7

-- 097 --

So high is Miller's estimate of Edwards' achievement that he does not draw back from suggesting that comparison would be appropriate between Edwards' philosophy of history and that of a figure as important as Marx. Thus Miller saw in Work of Redemption evidence for modernity because its author understood the decisive significance of interpretation: to comprehend that interpretation is necessary is prerequisite to appreciating that the nature of human existence is inevitably historical.

At least three considerations prompt comment. First, the issue of interpretation is an ancient problem within the tradition of Christian thought. Indeed, the hermeneutical issue lay behind the typological tradition Edwards used so extensively in the Redemption Discourse. What may be new about the issue in modern historiography is recognition that there are many different interpretations of given facts. In failing to stress Edwards' continuities with his past, Miller has too strongly cast him as modern. On the other hand, he may have done so because he failed to distinguish between several kinds of interpretation. At this point Miller's argument is inadequately developed. That in some formal sense Edwards recognized the necessity of interpretation with respect to history is not an issue. What is important to explore is how much can be read into that claim.

A second point follows. We must question how strongly and consciously Edwards understood his work as historical in any modern sense. He was clearly prepared to make use of such terms as "scheme" or "plan" or "pattern" as synonyms for "history." At one level this reinforces Miller's point that Edwards believed that historical events required interpretation. But it undercuts the claim that he anticipated with a special insight the era of historicism in which Miller wanted to locate him. "History" is ambiguous, and Miller's discussion does little to make this plain or accept the constraints of ambiguity.

Finally, a third point must be made. Insofar as Edwards is concerned with patterns, schemes, or "history," it is not as subjects in themselves but as a manifestation of divine redemption—God's most basic relationship to the world. The Work of Redemption is the sum and substance of God's history or plan, and not a scheme, history, or plan in itself. In this way Miller's immensely appreciative discussion seems to distort Edwards' great objective even while paying it tribute.

If Perry Miller found in Work of Redemption intimations of the modern preoccupation with history, Peter Gay thought it "thoroughly traditional,"

-- 098 --

indeed a throwback to "religious propaganda."8 Gay makes it clear that he thinks Miller's claims perverse. Edwards' was not the stance of a true empiricist ready to write history in the advanced eighteenth-century mode cultivated by Voltaire and Hume. He saw history as "Calvinist doctrine exemplified in a distinct succession of transcendent moments."9 In this sense, Gay insists that Edwards has little, if any, of the modern sense of history that Miller found to be so pronounced in the man and to permeate his Work of Redemption. Gay sees Edwards as the ultimate link in that chain of colonial historians whose labors exemplified what he called their "loss of mastery."1

If the middle decades of the twentieth century brought appreciation for Edwards and his thought, they have also led, concurrently, to rather discrepant estimates of his Work of Redemption. By making the original sermons of the Redemption Discourse readily accessible, and restoring their form and content, this edition should make possible a more just estimate both of his actual achievement and of his projected revision. Above all, his "history" must be seen as focused on God's Work of Redemption. It stands as a theological essay, not as a human history, however grand the scale attributed to it. Our discussion of its origin and development, and the emphasis upon the structure it exhibits and the devices Edwards used, should provide resources for more adequate interpretation of this extraordinary document. But first one more line of interpretation must be addressed.

In the social setting of the late twentieth century, which has placed emphasis on subjective dimensions of culture and the role of personality, it is almost inevitable that such an influential cultural object as Work of Redemption would be read in these terms. In the last analysis, this is to interpret it as a projection into the collective realm of the Puritan morphology of redemption in the life of the individual saint. In recent years William J. Scheick has taken such a position. In "The Grand Design: Jonathan Edwards' History of the Work of Redemption," Scheick puzzles over the issue of "what Edwards intended as his new method."2 He proposes "that Edwards thought of his study as innovative

-- 099 --

because in it he treats history as an allegory of the conversion experience. History, in his view, merely manifests in large the experience of the individual soul undergoing the regenerative process." He further suggests that Edwards was possessed by the "vision of merging the notions of nature, of history, and of the saint's private self into one theological tract."3 In developing this proposal, Scheick takes notice of the imagery of the Redemption Discourse and links the whole to the preoccupation with the stages of redemption that were so important in the earlier Puritan tradition, and which we have discussed as the subjective side of redemption. At Scheick's hands, Edwards' "history" becomes "an allegory of the blessed soul."

It must be said that there is some warrant within the Redemption Discourse for Scheick's claim that Edwards was at least deliberate in making use of the analogy between the progress of redemption in the individual soul and its progress in creation. One passage to which he calls attention (located in Sermon Three) makes this plain.


And here by the way I would observe that the increase of gospel light and the carrying on the Work of Redemption as it respects the elect church in general, from the first erecting of the church to the end of the world, is very much after the same manner as the carrying on of the same work and the same light in a particular soul from the time of its conversion till it is perfected and crowned in glory. The work in a particular soul has its ups and downs. Sometimes the light shines brighter, and sometimes 'tis a dark time. Sometimes grace seems to prevail; at other times it seems to languish for a great while together and corruption prevails and then grace revives again. But in the general grace is growing from its first infusion till it is perfected in glory; the kingdom of Christ is building up in the soul. So it is with respect to the great affair in general as it relates to the universal subject of it, as 'tis carried on from the first beginning of it after the fall till it is perfected at the end of the world, as will more fully appear by a particular view of this affair from beginning to end in the prosecution of this subject, if God gives opportunity to carry it through as I propose.4

-- 100 --

Read closely, this section is less evidence for a direct parallel between the stages of salvation in the soul and in creation than it is the assertion that while the manifestations are ambiguous in each, the progress of redemption in both is fundamental and secure. The basic difficulty with Scheick's position, however, is not this slight change of emphasis in reading the text but rather his miscasting of Edwards' original intention.

Edwards was well aware of the distinction between the subjective side of redemption and the objective side. He took a great interest in how redemption affected the individual saint and sinner. Indeed, his experience with and observation of the awakenings in Northampton, and his subsequent analysis of them, underlay his own major contribution to the Puritan literature on the morphology of the religious life. But the Redemption Discourse was not concerned with the effect of redemption upon the soul of the saint—that he plumbed in Religious Affections. The objective side was the focus, that is, the divine Work of Redemption. For him, if there was an analogy, it was from that greater redemption of creation to its pale shadow in the soul, not vice versa.

Scheick shifts the emphasis of the Redemption Discourse to make Edwards address the problem of design and meaning, as if his operative criteria were only that his exposition be intellectually and aesthetically satisfying. Just as lines can be traced from his typological approach to nature into Emersonian naturalism, it is possible to propose that his interest in the coherence of redemption anticipates the kind of position that Horace Bushnell, for one, would make so much more pronounced in the next century. But while Edwards' links to nineteenth-century Romantic theology should be recognized and appreciated, his thought should not be wholly metamorphosed. This happens when his interpreters fail to respect the explicit design of his project. Thus, even though it takes its departure from the useful and suggestive analogy offered by Edwards himself, Scheick's concentration on the subjective side of redemption is flawed. Interpretation of Work of Redemption should start with the far richer and more complex literary structure of the whole in relation both to its background in the tradition and its place in Edwards' thought.

The Text Of The Redemption Discourse And Its Presentation In The Yale Edition

This edition of the Redemption Discourse is the first based directly on a transcription of the original sermon booklets since Jonathan

-- 101 --

Edwards, Jr., sent his rendering of them to John Erskine. Consequently it will appear in certain respects to be a less polished version than the Erskine edition or other versions derived from it. Modern editing practice does not permit the wholesale intervention that marked nineteenth-century presentations, that embellishment of texts that has been routinely perpetrated on Edwards'. Erskine, or Edwards, Jr., foreshadowed these elaborations when they finished incomplete sections of the text in the sermon booklets. We have seen that had its author actually completed his Redemption project, he would probably have reconceptualized it and certainly carried out substantial redrafting of the 1739 sermon texts. Those revisions would have been much more extensive than the emendations that Edwards, Jr., and Erskine, followed by Pitcher, worked upon it. The pattern of likely development is suggested by the progression seen from the sermon on "Distinguishing Marks" to Religious Affections in 1746. The recasting, refinement, and amplification would have been such as to make of the Redemption Discourse a wholly different literary product. In the absence of Edwards' own revision, we are better served by a rough but genuine Edwardsean text than by a well-meant but extensively revised version which falls far short of what Edwards himself would have produced.

The current edition is intended for both scholarly and more general use. A primary objective has been to make accessible a version that, while conveying the vigor of Edwards' preaching style, reproduces his sermon booklets as closely as possible. Accordingly, the edition, while standing in its own right, also serves as a guide to the original manuscript or to microform versions that may be available in the future.

Edwards left his Redemption Discourse unrevised in the manuscript sermon booklets which he took into the Northampton pulpit in 1739 and which are the basis for this text. He did not preach them again as a series or even rework parts of them for use in connection with other sermons. They remain almost untouched, although at a very few points there are interventions that may be by another hand, presumably that of Jonathan Edwards, Jr. Wilson Kimnach's full discussion of Edwards' literary practices in sermon crafting is helpful to those interested both in the technical details of how he composed sermons and in the form in which the manuscripts now exist.5 Edwards'

-- 102 --

practices in drafting the Redemption Discourse booklets were consistent with those he used at the same period in the preparation of other sermons. Some sections of these booklets are carefully drafted, presenting essentially complete texts as the basis for oral delivery. Others are much less polished, in the extreme amounting to a series of words or phrases interspersed with lines and spaces. Physically, however, the sermon booklets for this series are in remarkably good condition. (There is some wear at the corners of the first leaf of the first booklet.)

In preparing this edition, I have been guided in general by the principles laid down by the Yale edition for editing manuscripts. Previous editors in the series, especially Paul Ramsey in Freedom of the Will, Stephen J. Stein in Apocalyptic Writings, and Wallace E. Anderson in Scientific and Philosophical Writings, have provided valuable introductions to the presentation of Edwards' materials in this critical edition.6 Unfortunately, none of the sermons to be published from Edwards' manuscripts has yet appeared. This series of thirty sermons, however, which is logically but one expanded sermon, has requirements significantly different from those of individual sermons or short sermon series. For this reason it has been given unique treatment in certain particulars to reflect its unusual status as a proto-treatise widely circulated posthumously.7

The division of the text into sermons has been retained, including introductory recapitulations and summaries, and no attempt has been made to improve the rough sections that were left unfinished. Like Edwards' other sermon booklets, those containing the Redemption Discourse fall far short of being completed literary texts. Edwards uses contractions, symbols, and abbreviations very freely. Indeed, abbreviations of the same word may vary widely. R., Rig., Right. stand in the text along with Righteous and Righteousness. Of course, R. or r. can also stand for Redeemer, or redeem, or redemption, which may also be abbreviated as Red., Redemp., and Redempt. All words are spelled out in this edition, and notes indicate probable readings or plausible alternatives. In accordance with the practice of the Yale edition, however, some eighteenth-century contractions and spellings have been retained, for example, ben't, han't, 'tis, and 'em,

-- 103 --

where the voicing of the word, and therefore the pattern of speech, is decisively affected. In pursuing the goal of presenting a thoroughly readable text, I have modernized Edwards' spellings and also the names of authors to whom he refers, for example, Zwingli and Wycliffe. In addition, I have modernized his capitalization without notation.

I have introduced modern paragraph divisions because Edwards used none in his sermon booklets. He did, however, indicate two kinds of internal division that related to the delivery of the sermons. One is indentation, through which he signaled a new block of material or new subject matter. The other is keying lines, either between blocks or within them, to assist him in finding his place after raising his eyes as he preached. He probably inserted the lines after he had worked out his text. Paragraph divisions reflect, but are not limited to, both the blocks of material and the keying lines.

The punctuation of Edwards' texts creates a particular challenge. His practices in this respect in published sermons, tracts, and books, as well as in more finished manuscript compositions, were essentially rhetorical, and of course reflect eighteenth-century conventions. The manuscript text of the Redemption Discourse, however, has no consistent punctuation, so that I have established sentences. Edwards' delivery frequently entailed constructing a cumulative series of clauses, resulting in lengthy blocks of written material susceptible to different constructions. In punctuating such passages, my intention has in general been to present his thoughts in shorter and simpler rather than more extensive and complex written sentences, and to add only minimal punctuation.

An important task has been to handle Edwards' deletions in ways that further both readability and scholarship. Since Edwards drafted the sermons in the booklets on which this edition is based rather than composing them on scrap paper and copying them in relatively finished form into notebooks, as he did with other writings, the text includes numerous transpositions and deletions. The transpositions may be followed in the notes, which track Edwards as he moves back and forth between leaves in a single booklet. The deletions take two forms. The first are words, phrases, or even short sections struck out in the course of composition. These are omitted without comment. The second are sections which Edwards deleted with bold lines and/or by enclosure of blocks of text in large brackets. It is possible that he wished to save these for use in other writings; they have therefore been transcribed and are preserved in the notes.

-- 104 --

-- 105 --

-- 106 --

To present a coherent and readable text, this edition provides material which Edwards clearly intended to deliver from the pulpit but which does not appear in the booklets. This is necessary where he left spaces or drew in lines to indicate where he intended to quote. Where these quotations were to be from Scripture, he either cited the passage without writing it out, or indicated it by several words or phrases. Here the quotation has been supplied and enclosed in square brackets. Edwards also used this method to indicate his intention to repeat words or phrases as he built the rhetorical structure of his preaching. Such words and phrases can be deduced from the rest of the text, and I have supplied them in angle brackets. Where this edition of the Redemption Discourse does not include either scriptural quotations or other material to be found in editions derived from the Erskine edition (based on the Edwards, Jr., transcription), the correct inference is that one or other of these earlier editors inserted the material.

Where scriptural material has been provided by the editor, it has been taken from the Authorized Version, and the punctuation and orthography of that text have been followed.8 Edwards appears to have quoted Scripture from memory, and he is not always accurate. His quotations, according to the conventions of the Yale edition, stand as he wrote them, but significant misquotations are noted, as are all wrong citations. I believe that giving voice to biblical citations was an important rhetorical device in Edwards' sermon delivery; their position in this text of the Redemption Discourse reproduces his practices in the booklets, and I have set them off with commas.

Edwards' text contains no formal footnotes. The notes in this edition are all mine. The Yale edition numbers notes on a one-to-nine repeating scheme. In this text, some notes are italicized (both number and text). Italicized notes present technical material. In general, they fall into two classes. The first consists of those notes that indicate the level of subordination of the argument in the rational outline I have devised to embrace the entire thirty preaching units. Edwards' method of denoting subordination within his argument is difficult to follow. His indication of heads is clear in his text, but their enumeration leads to immediate confusion. In the outline I assign numbers

-- 107 --

and letters to the various levels of his argument. This ordering has been presented schematically in table 1 (pp. 74–77) and comprehensively in Appendix A. The numbers and letters are given in the notes to assist the reader in following the logic of Edwards' discourse. The text reproduces his letter or number; and the note indicates the logical level of subordination.

The second class of italicized notes indicates the point at which Edwards moves from one side of a leaf to the other or to a new leaf or new booklet. The number of the booklet, the number of the leaf in each booklet, and the side of the leaf, expressed by the abbreviation recto or verso, are all given (for example, 2.6r). This convention permits the scholar to locate any section of the manuscript text in either the original or a microform version. In the rush of composition and subsequent revision, Edwards frequently relocated provisionally written material, and a careful reading of these notes will permit the reader to trace his course through the booklets.

When a note contains both a booklet/leaf/side code or an element of the rational outline and other information, whether technical or not, only the note's number and the code or element is italicized. In some cases, technical discussions (for example, concerning blocks of text Edwards has moved, added, or deleted) occur in notes whose numbers are not italicized. Codes and elements included as part of the discussion are italicized.

Notes with purely substantive content are not italicized. These convey a variety of information. Many identify scriptural quotations reflected in Edwards' language but not cited. (Of course, he presumed that members of his eighteenth-century congregation would be far more familiar with the Scriptures than we can assume the modern reader to be.) Where Edwards refers to ancient or contemporary authors, quotes their work, or gives evidence of dependence upon other sources (an important issue in many of his published writings but much less so in sermons) the author or source is identified, and an attempt is made to cite the passages Edwards had in mind in his rather casual references.

These aids, both technical and substantive, are intended primarily to help the scholar. They will, it is hoped, not inhibit readers who may wish to approach Jonathan Edwards' work as a literary document. For it is my intention that this edited text should read easily and well, presenting the vigor of Edwards' prose and, above all, the splendor of his intellect.
