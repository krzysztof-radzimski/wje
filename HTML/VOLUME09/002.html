EDITOR'S INTRODUCTION

Jonathan Edwards' A History of the Work of Redemption stands apart in the body of his writings. We know that he intended to bring his thought to its summation in a volume which would give theology a new basis in the theme of God's redemption of the world. But the book assumed to be that volume was published in Edinburgh a decade and a half after his death and was a significantly edited transcription of a series of sermons he had preached in the late 1730s. Of all his major treatises, including Religious Affections, Freedom of the Will, and Original Sin, Work of Redemption alone was not delivered to the printer by Edwards himself. His other significant systematic works, the smaller essays known as the Two Dissertations, were nearing completion before his death. They were published within a decade, largely in the state in which he left them, under the supervision of his protégés. So the singularity of A History of the Work of Redemption begins with the fact that it was edited and published by an admirer removed in space and, to some degree, in time from its author.

The manner in which Edwards composed Work of Redemption is also noteworthy. The sermon series he delivered under this title was a quite extraordinary venture on his part; namely, the composition of an extended set of sermon-discourses based on one biblical text. The form was derived from that of the conventional Puritan plain-style sermon, and the series stretched out over the best part of six months. Certainly there are examples in the Edwards corpus of single sermons that required several occasions for delivery. There are also instances in which a series of sermons was grounded upon a single scriptural passage. Edwards developed one of these very shortly before he composed Work of Redemption. This series, known as "Charity and Its Fruits," presented a progressive analysis of the verses in St. Paul's Hymn to Love (1 Corinthians 13). But no extant series is like the

-- 002 --

Redemption Discourse,1 which sustained, or rather transformed, the structure of a single sermon across thirty separate preaching occasions. Although Religious Affections, Edwards' first great treatise, undoubtedly also originated in such a series, we do not have the manuscripts of the sermons, and the materials were thoroughly reworked for publication. So Work of Redemption is unique within the Edwards corpus with respect to the status of its text. No other uncompleted major treatise survives in such a "first draft."

Work of Redemption is unusual in other respects as well. Although Edwards conceived of it as a systematic discussion of the doctrine central to his mature thinking, he put the twelve manuscript booklets containing these sermons aside and as far as we can tell never preached the series or any part of it again. The only evidence that he began any significant revision is to be found in a notebook compiled toward the end of his life which probably marks the beginning of such a process. Thus both what was published as A History of the Work of Redemption and the original sermon-discourses on which it was based probably stand at some remove from what Edwards would have envisioned as its final, completed form. The fullest and most carefully wrought plan for the project on God's Work of Redemption occurs in his letter to the trustees of the College of New Jersey where, in discussing his reluctance to accept their invitation to become president of the college, he sketched an outline for the treatise.

In offering a new edition of Work of Redemption, the editor must proceed with caution and sensitivity. My introduction is divided into three parts. The first attends to the manuscript itself—its origin, characteristics, transmission, and eventual publication. In the second part I discuss the theological ideas which are at the heart of Edwards' endeavor, in particular the notion of the Work of Redemption as fundamental to the relationship between God and His creation. In doing so I deemphasize the importance conventionally given to the term history in the title, arguing instead that the work is primarily theological and that Edwards' use of the term is in that context. The third part describes how Work of Redemption was received upon its publication, how its influence spread, and how it has been reinterpreted as twentieth-century scholars have rediscovered in Edwards a major American thinker. The progression from text to doctrine and

finally to uses suggests the structure that is explicit in Edwards' sermons and that underlies his mature treatises.

1. The Redemption Discourse
The Setting Of The Redemption Discourse

By the spring of 1739, Jonathan Edwards had come into his own as successor to his grandfather, Solomon Stoddard, pastor of the church at Northampton in the Massachusetts Bay Colony. Northampton was the most important settlement in the upper Connecticut River Valley, and as a consequence its church was extremely influential. The grandson had shown himself worthy to follow Stoddard by his role in the religious awakenings of 1734, which he chronicled in his Faithful Narrative.2 This season of renewals made it evident that the impressive harvests of souls that had marked Stoddard's ministry were continuing after his death, under the stewardship of the grandson. In spite of rivalries and hard feelings, the succession was secure, and it was clear that Edwards would be a force to be reckoned with.3

Certain developments in the town of Northampton provided the occasion for the extraordinary preaching venture of the Redemption Discourse. In the course of the 1730s it had become increasingly evident that the town must replace its old meetinghouse with a new one, the third in its history.4 Contemporary accounts show that a serious decision to build the new meetinghouse was made in the middle years of the decade.5 While the new building was under construction, the gallery in the old one collapsed. No one was hurt, but this narrow escape from injury and death was taken as a sure sign that the townsfolk had acted wisely by committing themselves to building the new church. This more spacious meetinghouse also gave Edwards a larger stage on which to perform, to deliver (or "publish") his sermon-discourses—especially

-- 004 --

after interest in religious affairs waned following the 1734 revival. It is noteworthy that he preached the extended series of sermons on 1 Corinthians 13, St. Paul's Hymn to Love, soon after the move to the new building.6 The Redemption Discourse, which Edwards called "A History of the Work of Redemption," came a year later, preceded by four sermons on Genesis 3:11 preached in February 1739. That these ambitious sermon series closely followed the completion of the new structure was probably no accident.

The building of the new church was accompanied by an increasing differentiation of functions in the community. A new and separate town or county house was constructed soon after the new church,7 physically segregating ecclesiastical from governmental functions for the first time in Northampton. This signals the maturation of the town as county seat and suggests its increased influence. Of course, separating government from religion threatened the tradition of ecclesiastical domination and helped create a situation in which it was ever more necessary for Edwards to assert the importance of religion. The Redemption Discourse exemplifies this important theme in Edwards' preaching.

By 1739 Edwards was secure as Stoddard's successor and had engineered and participated in the opening of a new ecclesiastical chapter in the life of Northampton, but he had also attracted wider attention by his publications. The first of these had been the "Publick Lecture" originally delivered in Boston on July 8, 1731, shortly after he had assumed the Northampton pastorate. "Published at the desire of several, Ministers and Others, in Boston who heard it," God Glorified in the Work of Redemption stands as an earnest of the central place that redemption would hold in his mature theological reflection.8 Three years later (1734) he published another sermon, A Divine and Supernatural Light, Immediately Imparted to the Soul by the Spirit of God.9 Soon thereafter A Faithful Narrative (1736) eclipsed these sermon-discourses at least in terms of public notice—it went through as many

-- 005 --

as ten editions in a decade.1 Edwards then prepared a set of additional discourses on five subjects (these were also originally sermons) for publication in 1738.2 So by the time he turned his attention to composing the Redemption Discourse, he was well on the way to recognition both as an author and as a leader in New England ecclesiastical affairs.

This evidence for Edwards' growing stature is significant as background to the sermon series on God's Work of Redemption. It also seems clear that he was beginning to write sustained discourses of treatise length, although their initial—and perhaps only—"publication" might be from the pulpit. His 1738 sermon series is a quite remarkable descant on the text of 1 Corinthians 13. Technically the successive sermons of this series were preached on the verses in turn, so that Edwards was effectively appropriating an old and much-used device (expository preaching was thoroughly perfected in the Puritan tradition) in working through a biblical book (or even a series of books) over a long time. In this respect, then, he conceived the Redemption Discourse as a new and different kind of project, for it was based on a single verse of Scripture.

The Composition Of The Redemption Discourse

A History of the Work of Redemption originated as a series of thirty lecture-sermons that Edwards preached to the church in Northampton between March and August 1739. The collection of his manuscripts held in the Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library at Yale University includes the twelve booklets in which he wrote out these discourses. Like his other sermon booklets, they were designed to be held in the palm of his hand; each measures approximately 10.2 cm by 9.4 cm.

In general we know very little from either internal evidence or external testimony about the composition and delivery of these sermons.

-- 006 --

Five of the thirty manuscript sermon units do contain notations, presumably concerning the time of their delivery. The first sermon was dated "March 1739," the tenth "April 1739," the sixteenth simply "June 17th," and the twenty-eighth and twenty-ninth "August 1739." Beyond this, internal evidence makes it certain that at least the fourteenth and fifteenth, the eighteenth and nineteenth, and the twentieth and twenty-first sermons were composed and probably preached as linked pairs. In each case, the first unit of the pair (this possibly holds true for other sets as well) was intended for the forenoon and the second for the afternoon of the same day (undoubtedly Sundays). Otherwise the manuscript booklets do not indicate a specific day or time of preaching.

In the history of Edwards scholarship, more precise claims have been advanced about the preaching of this sermon series. Sereno Dwight, who was Jonathan Edwards' great-grandson and comprehensive editor and biographer, offered quite precise information about the distribution of the preaching occasions throughout this six-month period: "The eight first were delivered during that month [March], the eight next in the two following months, and the whole series, thirty in all, was completed before the close of August."3 Dwight's statement is consistent with the internal evidence, and the distribution he sketched may have been based on inferences drawn from the texts. It is conceivable, of course, that Dwight had access to additional information about the series that is not currently available. He may also have compiled enough information about the delivery of other sermons to give him a basis for inferring the pattern of distribution he reports. In the course of this particular six-month period (March through August), Edwards' production of sermons other than those of the Redemption Discourse was markedly lower than usual. In tabulating extant sermons he presumably composed and delivered during 1739, we find that eight date from January and February, and thirteen from the months September through December. For March through August, there are only ten sermons independent of the thirty

-- 007 --

of the Redemption Discourse.4 This evidence does not necessarily support Dwight's claims about the distribution of the series across the months in question, but neither does it contradict his statement.

Dwight may also have been familiar enough with details of Edwards' activities and travels throughout this period to have made a good educated guess about when the sermons were preached. Ebenezer Parkman's Diary provides some information about Edwards' characteristic movements.5 Parkman's relatively close observation of him in late May 1739 gives us grounds to suppose that the composition and delivery of the series caused no disruption of Edwards' normally demanding life. Parkman records that Edwards preached the public lecture in Boston (on the text 1 Timothy 2:5 [sic]) on May 24, a Thursday. On the next day Parkman dined with him at the Reverend Joshua Gee's in Boston, after which Edwards preached at "Elder Lymans." Whether he returned to Northampton on the Saturday is not certain, but it seems unlikely. On Sunday afternoon Parkman preached in his own pulpit at Westborough. (Westborough was only about a third the distance of Northampton from Boston.) On Monday afternoon (May 28) Parkman set off for Cambridge; when he returned he learned that Edwards had stayed at his home in Westborough that night. Probably Edwards was on his way to Northampton, although it is possible he was going back to Boston after having preached at Northampton on the twenty-seventh. From this we get a glimpse of the active life of a New England clergyman of the period; and we know that Edwards was, if anything, among the busiest. The larger point is that he composed and delivered the Redemption Discourse sermons during a time in which his schedule was already full. His normal Sunday sermons were the occasion of, and vehicle for, developing a series of lectures on this grand and encompassing theme.

Another of Dwight's assertions may also have come from internal evidence. He stated that Edwards "appears never to have repeated the Series of Discourses to his people."6 This claim may rest on special information available to Dwight alone, but it is much more likely that he based it on his knowledge of Edwards' literary habits. When he

-- 008 --

preached a sermon again Edwards usually noted the date; and he often thoroughly recast it.7

In the absence of more internal evidence we would ideally find external testimonies in, for instance, contemporary references to the actual delivery of this preaching series. As a hypothetical example, Joseph Bellamy, later to achieve prominence as an ecclesiastical leader, was apparently residing as a student in the Edwards household in this general period and might have heard these sermons. Our external evidence amounts to only one reminiscence, however, which was passed on to Edwards' grandson, Timothy Dwight, who cited it in Travels in New England and New York. Dwight devoted the fourth "letter" of his fourth volume to refuting an article in the Edinburgh Review which had claimed that "federal America" had "done nothing either to extend, diversify, or embellish the sphere of human knowledge." As a retort, Dwight argued that "the late President Edwards has more enlarged the science of theology than any divine of whom either England or Scotland can boast."8 Furthermore, Dwight claimed that, save for Whitefield's, Edwards' preaching was unrivaled for eloquence. In this connection he introduced a reminiscence about the Redemption Discourse:


The late Nehemiah Strong, Esq., a native of Northampton, and formerly professor of mathematics and natural philosophy in Yale College, observed to me that in early youth he heard Mr. Edwards deliver the sermons which now constitute the History of Redemption. His mind, he remarked, was from the beginning deeply interested in the subject. As it advanced, his feelings became more and more engaged. When Mr. Edwards came to a consideration of the final judgment, Mr. Strong said, his own mind was wrought up to such a pitch that he expected without one thought to the contrary the awful scene to be unfolded on that day and in that place. Accordingly, he waited with the deepest and the most solemn solicitude to hear the trumpet sound and the archangel call; to see the graves open, the dead arise, and the judge descend in the glory of his Father, with all his holy angels; and was deeply

-- 009 --

disappointed when the day terminated and left the world in its usual state of tranquility.9

Strong's testimony suggests that many might have remembered this significant series of lecture-sermons, but his is the only specific recollection. That Dwight published only this reference may mean that no contemporary written references to the series had survived even to his time.

From the outset Edwards clearly looked on the Redemption Discourse as an endeavor unlike any he had attempted before. First, all thirty sermons were organized around a single text: Isaiah 51:8, "For the moth shall eat them up like a garment, and the worm shall eat them like wool: but my righteousness shall be for ever, and my salvation from generation to generation." The elaboration of the whole series is bounded by that text and the doctrine framed from it: "The Work of Redemption is a work that God carries on from the fall of man to the end of the world." As in any Puritan sermon, there are entire sections which are "improvements" or "applications" for the benefit of Edwards' hearers. He concludes the series with a summary improvement. But a significant transmutation of the genre was taking place in spite of these and other formal elements and the constraints they placed upon the Redemption Discourse. The thirty edifying lectures had a narrative and theological unity so pervasive that, when they were finally published, their editor was able easily to strip away such sermonic characteristics as repetition of text and doctrine and reiteration of the argument. In so doing, he revealed the sustained treatise, organized by rationally ordered and largely chronological subdivisions, that we know as A History of the Work of Redemption.1 This is one reason to think (in spite of his reference to the contrary in the well-known letter to the trustees of the College of New Jersey, see pp. 62–63 below) that, apparently from the point of their conception, Edwards intended to develop the Redemption Discourse sermons into a substantial treatise for publication.

Wilson Kimnach, a close student of the booklets in which Edwards wrote out his sermons, has suggested that, at least in certain cases, the division into "preaching units," that is, individual sermons, was arbitrary, or introduced only to permit Edwards to deliver the "treatise"

-- 010 --

over thirty occasions.2 In some places, the manuscript was clearly divided into sermons only after composition, but many of the sermons were composed with a view to being preached on a particular occasion.3 Kimnach's larger point, however, is indisputable: the series is indeed a single, self-contained project. In this perspective, the development of a sermon series offered Edwards the opportunity to draft an initial version of a long-range undertaking; circumstances required its division into units that permitted delivery as sermons. Additional evidence that the Work of Redemption theme was fundamental to his mature thought appears in the "Miscellanies," that extraordinary series of essays recording the life of his mind from his college years on. There he repeatedly framed early entries around the theme of redemption and related subjects.4 The Redemption Discourse summarized, as of the spring and summer months of 1739, Edwards' extensive reflections on the doctrine he believed to be most fundamental to Christianity.

There is no evidence that Edwards gave his attention to reworking (or, as noted above, repreaching) the series. There are, for example, no extensively redrafted sections.5 In this connection, the circumstances of the time must be kept in mind. In little over a year after the conclusion of the series, George Whitefield made his first visit to New England, arriving at Northampton in the middle of October 1740. The extraordinary response to his preaching, and the itinerating which followed it, represent the social and cultural tumult later denominated the Great Awakening, in which Edwards became deeply involved. Under its influence, his intellect was increasingly devoted to developing a more discriminating understanding of the workings of the Spirit in human subjects. This preoccupation produced the extraordinary sequence of analytical studies of the phenomenon that began with his New Haven commencement sermon, "The Distinguishing Marks" (1741), progressed through Some Thoughts Concerning

-- 011 --

the Revival

(1742), and culminated in the magisterial treatise Religious Affections (1746).6 Religious Affections was itself, apparently, first worked out in the form of sermons.

These publications of the early 1740s bear on the Redemption Discourse materials at most only indirectly, so that little, if any, discussion of them is appropriate. Edwards was so caught up by the Awakening and its demands that he had little opportunity, even if he had wished for it, to go back to revision and elaboration of the series of lecture-sermons about God's Work of Redemption. At the same time, there was a direct connection with the Awakening, for that revitalization of religious life concerned what we might term the subjective side of redemption, that is, its effect upon and appropriation by human subjects insofar as they were made participants in it. For our purposes, however, his publications of this period primarily demonstrate Edwards' proclivity to develop, and his manifest skill at transforming, essentially sermonic materials into sustained treatises. Thus the final form he seems to have intended for the Redemption Discourse was first perfected in the meticulous analysis of religious behavior and belief as they originated in the affections.

The "History" Project

A letter from the Scottish clergyman John Erskine to Joseph Bellamy, written on March 24, 1755, gives us some hint of Edwards' intentions for the Redemption Discourse. In passing Erskine writes, "Tho' I long to see Mr. Edwards' refutation of the several branches of Arminianism, yet I more long to see his intended history of man's Redemption. From such a pen upon such a subject, something highly valuable may be expected. May a kind providence preserve from danger a life so important."7 Edwards had begun to exchange letters with Erskine in 1747. In the light of these comments we can safely surmise that he must have told Erskine something of his plans. But none of the letters he wrote to Erskine, at least among those that are preserved by Dwight in his Life, gives any details of what Erskine actually knew about the Redemption Discourse.8 If Edwards' extensive correspondence

-- 012 --

with Erskine survives, it has not been located and identified. What is clear, however, is that Erskine's special interest in the Redemption Discourse—an interest that eventually led him to edit it and arrange for its publication—antedated Edwards' death.

During the Stockbridge period (1750–57), Edwards began to turn his attention back to the Redemption Discourse. Three of his notebooks from this time establish, to greater and lesser degrees, that he was beginning to think about revising and reworking the lecture-sermons.9 He seems to have pursued this enterprise most actively toward the end of the Stockbridge period, especially between 1755 and 1757. Elsewhere I have analyzed the contents of the three notebooks to see how they bear on Edwards' plans to work seriously on the project and have found strong evidence that in the later Stockbridge years he began in his usual fashion to approach the project systematically.1 He seems to have thought that his knowledge of Christian history needed to be augmented, so he started to read comprehensively in the history of the early Western church, focusing his attention on the Roman popes. Concurrently, he made notes about important points other authors had made which bore on the Redemption project. Finally, in the most distinctive of the three notebooks, he jotted down thoughts about the form, the content, and the argument of the project concerned with the Work of Redemption, including variant titles and different patterns of organization. The specifics of these notebooks will be introduced later.2 For the moment their relevance is that they corroborate the statements Edwards made about the Redemption project in his letter to the trustees of the College of New Jersey.

With the death of Aaron Burr, Edwards' son-in-law and the college's president, its fragile existence in New Jersey—it had but recently settled at Princeton—was once again threatened.3 Edwards had links to the college from its early days. Indeed he seems increasingly to have felt such a kinship to the Middle Colony "New Side" Presbyterians

-- 013 --

that it to some extent superseded his ties to the Connecticut Valley churches and the Awakeners in the Bay area. It is not surprising that the college's trustees turned to him as an obvious candidate for its presidency. In offering a dispassionate analysis of his strengths and shortcomings which stands as a model for candor on the part of presidential candidates, Edwards also reviewed the current projects he thought would necessarily be delayed or abandoned if he accepted the office. In this context he offered much the clearest statement that we have about his plans for the development and completion of the Redemption project, signaling that it was uppermost in his thoughts at that point. "I have had on my mind and heart, (which I long ago began, not with any view to publication,) a great work, which I call a History of the Work of Redemption, a body of divinity in an entire new method, being thrown into the form of a history."4 So when Edwards moved from Stockbridge to Princeton in January 1758, he was again decisively deflected from the "history" project he considered a development of the Redemption Discourse of 1739. His death within two months (March 22, 1758) prevented him from ever returning to it.

The Redemption Discourse And The "Miscellanies"

One significant perspective upon the place of the Redemption Discourse in Edwards' intellectual life can be gained from his "Miscellanies."5 To this point these have not been used to interpret the Redemption sermons, although Thomas Schafer's critical work on them has led to confident dating, at least of the periods in which individual entries were copied into the notebooks. It is not surprising that the "Miscellanies" contain traces or foreshadowings of the Redemption Discourse throughout, but their full exploitation in this respect must await their publication in the Yale critical edition. The suggestions that follow are, therefore, provisional and necessarily impressionistic.

There are at least three different respects in which the "Miscellanies" bear on the present inquiry. First, from the early entries, and continuing throughout the entire series of well over a thousand, the

-- 014 --

theme of redemption as the greatest of God's works appears repeatedly. Second, two extended entries offer quite remarkable evidence that Edwards had reflected thoroughly on the themes that would define the outline of the project as a whole, perhaps as early as two to three years before he began to compose the sermons. Finally, a half-dozen or so entries, contemporaneous with or made shortly after the preaching series in 1739, include explicit references to the Redemption Discourse.

A very large proportion of the "Miscellanies" explicitly concern redemption. For example, No. 38 (probably summer 1723) is a brief discussion of the "Harmony of God's Attributes in the Work of Redemption." No. 60, titled "Hades" and written not long after, perhaps in the early fall of that year, is a discussion of the process of redemption with regard to both individuals and groups. No. 156, written when Edwards was a tutor at Yale, is devoted to the defeat of Satan and introduces themes that are developed at great length in the Redemption Discourse proper. Discussions introduced under topics as various as "New Earth," "Heaven," "Kingdom of Christ," "Hell" or "Hell's Torments," "Resurrection," "Covenants," and "Lord's Day," among many others, are directly relevant to the project. Many are even more explicit: "End of Creation," "Millennium," "Universal Redemption," "Incarnation," and, above all, "Work of Redemption."

These themes had been fully explored by Edwards before he preached the public lecture in Boston on July 8, 1731, that, when published, carried the title God Glorified in the Work of Redemption, by the Greatness of Man's Dependence upon Him, in the whole of It. They continue as subjects throughout the "Miscellanies" until the end of his life. Certain formulations gain preference during particular periods in the course of his entries in these notebooks. The "Wisdom of God in the Work of Redemption" became significant in the early 1730s (examples are Nos. 508, 510, 521, 524, 526, 554, 569, 604, 614, 616, 633, 653, 664, and 681). He continued to use this formulation throughout the Northampton years, although less frequently. With No. 802 he began to use the phrase "Progress of Redemption" as an inclusive designation (other examples are 814, 833, 834, 835, 907, and 932). This he emended to "Progress of the Work of Redemption" in the early 1740s (see Nos. 935, 946, 949, 952, and 991). To identify individual essay-entries in this fashion, however, may obscure their interconnection. Edwards weaves themes into and around the redemption motif so that, taken as a whole, the "Miscellanies"

-- 015 --

dramatically confirm what he claims so explicitly, that this was a, if not the, central focal point of his theological reflection.

Beyond this, there is clear evidence in two entries that Edwards systematically brought together the basic themes of his sermon series at least two to three years before actually composing it in 1739. No. 702 (probably 1736 or 1737) is titled "Work of Creation. Providence. Redemption." It amounts to a lengthy argument (twenty-one pages in typescript) that the works of creation and providence are subservient to the Work of Redemption. Within this framework Edwards explored many of the theses and themes that concerned him throughout the sermons he preached on the Work of Redemption. In particular, he analyzed Adam as a type of Christ, and the relationship between creation by Christ and re-creation, or the new creation in him. The incarnation is central to this argument, as is the perfection of redemption in the end of the world. In many respects No. 702 is a précis of the argument of the Redemption Discourse.

This is made all the more evident by No. 710, an equally long entry, which stands as an appendix to No. 702. It explores the implications of viewing the internal transactions of the Trinity as the energizing force behind creation as well as redemption: "The world was created that Christ might obtain a spouse." This leads Edwards to explore the secondary roles of angels and men in these transactions. It also leads him to develop the theme that bulks so large in the sermons, the respects in which the devil and his Antichrist mirror God and his Christ. Edwards' typological analysis is very important to these sections. Taken together, these two miscellanies offer the earliest evidence of his intentions concerning the Redemption Discourse and show how thoroughly he developed his grand plan of the project some time before he actually wrote and preached the sermons.

Finally, several of the miscellanies give direct evidence that, probably shortly after preaching the sermon series, Edwards gave thought to its extension and specification at certain points. In the manuscript of the sermons, he directed himself to particular miscellanies at three separate places. These three are Nos. 802, 803, and 810, generally dated in mid- to late 1739, or roughly toward the end or at the conclusion of the period in which he delivered the Discourse.6

-- 016 --

No. 802 is called "Progress of Redemption" and begins with directions: "Add this to Discourse on Isai 51.8. Sermon 18 at the end of the 2 particular under the 4. General Observation." In this miscellany Edwards develops the argument that Christ's spiritual "comings to judgment" as spoken of in Scripture anticipate what will be done "externally" at the day of judgment.

No. 803 is titled "Progress of Redemption. Christ's Coming to Judgment." Here the instruction is: "Join this to what is said concerning the state of things requiring Christ's coming to judgment at the time he will come in Discourse from Isai. 51.8. Sermon 28. The 3d particular concerning the last Apostasy." Its theme is that the greater light available to the wicked of the world at that point will make their apostasy the more heinous in God's sight. Therefore the destruction will be all the more terrible.

Finally, No. 810 is an addition to No. 359, which concerned the Scriptures in relation to redemption. No. 810 especially reflected on Job and why such a book was inserted in the canon. It seems less fully and directly linked to the Redemption Discourse than Nos. 802 and 803, but it does stand as further evidence, if such be needed, of Edwards' preoccupation with this theme at the end of the 1730s.

Two other brief adjacent essays, Nos. 806 and 807, directly relate to the Redemption Discourse. No. 807 is titled "Work of Redemption the Greatest of God's Works." It concerns the duration of the new creation (about six thousand years plus an additional thousand years in the millennium) in comparison with the old (six days plus a sabbath day). In this brief note Edwards explicitly refers to "Discourse on Isaiah 51:8 1 Inference at the close of Sermon 29." No. 806 is entitled "New Heavens and New Earth" and does not explicitly refer to the Redemption Discourse, although it directly concerns its overall theme.

From this brief consideration of the "Miscellanies," it should be clear that any future interpretation of the sermon series that formed the Redemption Discourse must include study of the large number of relevant entries in that remarkable series of notebooks. As much as anything else these essays, which Edwards compiled throughout his life, demonstrate how prolonged and deep was his commitment to develop a theology based upon the theme of the redemption of the world as a consequence of the internal dynamics of the Trinity—that is, the interrelationships of Father, Son, and Spirit.7 By the same

-- 017 --

token the Redemption Discourse stands as a systematic elaboration of a grand theme that appears at a remarkable number of places and times and under many different headings in the storehouse of his essays.

The Fate Of The Sermon Manuscripts

In moving to Princeton without his family (Sarah Edwards was to remain in Stockbridge with the children for a transitional period), Jonathan Edwards apparently entrusted the bulk of his manuscripts to Samuel Hopkins, protégé and then near neighbor in Great Barrington, Massachusetts. Writing on January 19, 1758, to Joseph Bellamy, another protégé and colleague then living in Bethlem, Connecticut, Hopkins reported that the preceding day Edwards had departed on the journey from Stockbridge to Princeton.8 Most significantly, he reported that he had left "long papers" and manuscript sermons in his possession, permitting him to go through them and make use of them while they were in his care. In Edwards' Stockbridge years, Hopkins and Bellamy had frequent exchanges with him about their respective writings in progress. Thus many of Edwards' manuscripts were undoubtedly well known to Hopkins, and he would have been a trusted and obviously sympathetic custodian for them until in due course they could be moved to New Jersey. There is no reason to suppose that the sermon manuscripts of the Redemption Discourse were separated from the other manuscripts—for instance, making the journey to Princeton with their author. It is a conjecture, of course, but a reasonable one, that they did not remain with Sarah under these circumstances; presumably the works about which Edwards was most concerned were left with Hopkins.

Edwards died in a matter of weeks, on March 22, 1758. While technically Sarah was executor of his literary remains, and was succeeded in this task at her death by the eldest son, Timothy, the actual possession of (and responsibility for) the manuscripts seems to have rested in other hands, primarily those of Samuel Hopkins.9 His role

-- 018 --

as conservator lasted for the better part of a decade. In autobiographical reflections written years later and published in 1805, Hopkins reported his memory of the arrangements made for the manuscripts and papers during the years immediately following Edwards' death. "Upon the death of Mr. Edwards, Mrs. Edwards, in consequence of verbal directions given to her by Mr. Edwards in his life time, put all his manuscripts and his library into my hands, and care: His manuscripts to be disposed of by me, and two other ministers."1 Joseph Bellamy was certainly one of the two other literary executors in this period; Gideon Hawley, another younger associate, may have been the third. There is significant correspondence between Hopkins and Bellamy in the ensuing years about these shared responsibilities.2 Inferences drawn from these letters, as well as Hopkins' own assertions, make it evident that of the three he was the most thoroughly involved with the manuscripts.


As these manuscripts were in my hands a number of years, I paid my chief attention to them, until I had read them all, which consisted of a great number of volumes, some of them large, besides sermons, of which sermons I did not read the whole. In doing this I had much pleasure and profit. My mind became more engaged in study, rising, great part of my time, at four o'clock in the morning to pursue my study, in which I took great pleasure.3

Others outside this circle, including John Erskine in Scotland, expressed an interest in Edwards' "literary remains." He wrote to Bellamy on June 8, 1759, with the thought that subscriptions could be obtained, presumably in Scotland, for two octavo volumes of "practical works."4 He also requested a description or listing of the manuscripts, presumably with a view to selecting additional items and proposing them for publication. A Dissenting minister in Ipswich, England, Mr. Gordon, also expressed interest in abridging Religious Affections for the English market. Indeed, from a letter written a year later, it is apparent that he wanted to reissue Edwards' published writings, hoping to find a market for them through the London book-seller

-- 019 --

Field.5 Thus during the first years after Edwards' death, while legal title to the manuscripts remained with the family, actual work in and with them, not excluding developing plans for their publication, was very much in the hands of others. Only when Jonathan Edwards, Jr., had graduated from the College of New Jersey and was judged suitable, in part through residing in both the Hopkins and Bellamy households and pursuing theological studies under them, did the responsibility for his father's literary materials pass into his hands.6 This development restored possession of and control over the corpus of manuscripts to the family, where it remained for more than a century.7

It is evident that Hopkins invested great energy in working with the manuscripts. By his own account, noted above, he worked through them with great love and benefit to himself. His access to them was made thoroughly secure as a consequence of Sarah Edwards' request that he write a memoir of her husband:


And Mrs. Edwards solicited me to write the life of Mr. Edwards, to be published, with a number of sermons, to be selected from his manuscripts. I considered myself very unequal to writing his life; but as by having the possession of his manuscripts, I was under better advantage to do it, than others, I engaged to do the best I could; and if it should be approved of by a number of friendly, judicious ministers, I would consent to have it published, on condition that my name should not be put to it. Accordingly it was published with a number of posthumous sermons, most of which I transcribed myself from his manuscripts.8

-- 020 --

Although Sarah died within six months of her husband, Hopkins respected her request and wrote the first substantial memoir of Edwards. His work has been basic to biographical study of Edwards ever since. But before The Life and Character of the Late Reverend Mr. Jonathan Edwards was published in 1765, including Sermons on Various Important Subjects,9 Hopkins was more than a little frustrated by erstwhile collaborators. To Joseph Bellamy he wrote with apparent testiness on January 4, 1764: "I should be glad you would transcribe some of the sermons you mention without any ifs or ands [italics original]. I and my brother have transcribed 10 sermons, which I hope to send to Boston [to the publisher Kneeland] soon, but these with the life & the 6 Mr. Searl has transcribed, will not be enough to make one Vol."1

Apparently Hopkins and Bellamy also collaborated in issuing Two Dissertations2 in the same year. The same letter requests Bellamy to "send me that on Virtue the first safe opportunity," because he is planning to go to Boston to prosecute the proposal.3 Hopkins' letter also reports having finished transcribing the "Last End."4 From this we can infer that it was Bellamy's transcription of "The Nature of True Virtue" that he asked for. In these efforts, Hopkins discharged his editorial labors in a conservative fashion, showing respect for the manuscript text and seeking to preserve it from excessive editorial intervention in a fashion less often followed by later editors.

The Publication Of The Redemption Discourse

At some point during this period, possibly during the winter of 1765 and the spring of 1766, Jonathan Edwards, Jr., took physical possession of the manuscripts. He also began to function as literary executor of his father's papers. Certainly by March of 1767, when he was a tutor at Princeton, Edwards, Jr., had replaced Hopkins (and Bellamy, and perhaps Hawley) as the agent responsible for them. At this point Bellamy, and probably Hopkins, too, effectively passed out of the picture. In the fragmentary materials which bear on the location and status of the Edwards materials in this initial period, no evidence has been found to suggest that serious and sustained attention

-- 021 --

was ever given to the question of whether the Redemption Discourse should be transcribed and published. It is clear that the response to the proposals for publishing Edwards' works had been disappointing, at least on the western side of the Atlantic. Perhaps because of the lack of interest in the Colonies, Edwards, Jr., seems to have sought to capitalize on the rather livelier interest in his father's library remains in Scotland especially, but to some degree also in England. We have noted John Erskine's correspondence with Edwards (which Edwards valued so highly, particularly during the Stockbridge years when he was so physically isolated and Erskine sent him current books) as well as with Bellamy. Although we do not have direct evidence, it is most likely that Erskine now turned to the son, recently become literary executor, and urged upon him his own interest in seeing the Redemption Discourse published. In his "Preface" published in the first Edinburgh edition5 and dated at New Haven on February 25, 1773, Jonathan Edwards, Jr., referred directly to Erskine as removing the obstacle presented by "the difficulty of getting any considerable work printed in this infant country hitherto." According to Edwards, Jr., Erskine "engaged a bookseller to undertake the work, and also signified his desire that these following discourses in particular might be made public."

The question of who transcribed the manuscripts that were published by John Erskine in Edinburgh in 1774 as A History of the Work of Redemption now becomes extremely important. If they were transcribed by Jonathan Edwards, Jr., there is no reason to suppose that Erskine, publisher of the first printed version and its avowed editor, ever saw the originals. Every subsequent edition would then have been based upon a printed version initially edited from a transcript, and not from the manuscripts themselves, with what loss of nuance and authenticity may be imagined.

First we must consider Erskine's possible role in the transcription. If he was responsible for encouraging and facilitating the publication of the sermon series on the Work of Redemption, did he also have a hand in transcribing them? From the fact that he unequivocally claims to have edited the sermons into the form of a treatise, it is possible to infer that he arranged to transcribe them. His advertisement permits this reading: "The manuscript being intrusted to my

-- 022 --

care, I have not presumed to make any change in the sentiments or composition. I have, however, taken the liberty to reduce it from the form of sermons, which it originally bore, to that of a continued treatise; and I have so altered and diversified the marks of the several divisions and subdivisions, that each class of heads might be easily distinguished."6 Dated April 29, 1774, some fourteen months after Jonathan Edwards, Jr.'s, preface, this advertisement might suggest that the transcription (as well as the editing) of the Redemption Discourse was accomplished in Scotland. But circumstantial evidence casts doubt upon this interpretation.

First, Jonathan Edwards, Jr., himself published a set of his father's sermons at the end of the decade. In the preface to the book Sermons on the Following Subjects, published at Hartford in 1780, he offered the following statement: "As to the sermons themselves, they were not transcribed with any view to a publication in this country. In the year 1773, I was desired by a gentleman in Scotland, to transcribe a number of the author's sermons on some of the most plain, practical and experimental subjects, that they might be printed there."7 (This is dated December 21, 1779.) It is highly unlikely that different understandings and approaches to transcription, editing, and publication would govern these two ventures (the publication of Work of Redemption and Sermons) so closely related in kind, context, and time.

Second, Edwards, Jr.'s, reasons for selecting these particular sermons closely echo Erskine's grounds for recommending A History of the Work of Redemption to its potential readers: "Though the acute philosopher and deep divine appears in them, yet they are in the general better calculated for the instruction and improvement of ordinary Christians, than those of President Edwards's writings, where the abstruse nature of the subject, or the subtle objections of opposers of the truth, led him to more abstract and metaphysical reasonings."8 Thus the parallel characterizations with regard to the Sermons Edwards, Jr., published in 1780 and the Work of Redemption Erskine published in 1774 suggest they were related projects produced by similar methods.

Comparable considerations apparently also led Erskine to publish Edwards' Twenty Sermons in Edinburgh in 1789, and a selection of his

-- 023 --

Miscellaneous Observations, also in Edinburgh, in 1793.9 The language of the prefaces to these works suggests that the same method of collaboration was used: Erskine took the initiative in soliciting Jonathan Edwards, Jr., to publish materials, but it was the latter who transcribed them and forwarded the transcriptions to Erskine.

Thus it is likely that this method was also used for the manuscript published as A History of the Work of Redemption. We can, then, reasonably surmise that while Erskine clearly exercised considerable initiative in editing the Redemption sermon materials into treatise form, it is almost certain that Jonathan Edwards, Jr., transcribed the booklets, providing the manuscript which Erskine used in preparing copy for the publisher.

This interpretation of the evidence is corroborated by charges leveled at Jonathan Edwards, Jr., some years later, when it came his turn to be separated from the church he served as pastor.1 Roger Sherman, Connecticut judge, member of the Continental Congress, and signer of the Declaration of Independence and the Constitution, was a prominent (and dominant) figure in the White Haven church served by Edwards, Jr., although he was not close to its everyday life because of his frequent and prolonged absences from New Haven. When, in 1790, the proceedings which led to Edwards, Jr.'s, departure were under way between the pastor and his church, Sherman inquired of other members about the grounds for the dispute. David Austin, who was then emerging into prominence, offered the observation that Edwards, Jr., had devoted inordinate time to "business" affairs in his study: "has he not been imployed a great proportion of his time there in business of a pecuniary nature? has he not imployed himself in transcribing a volume of his father's sermons & printing them at Hartford? In transcribing the History of Redemption & preparing a copy to be printed in Scotland?" Sherman's response took the form of defending the appropriateness of these activities on the part of Jonathan Edwards, Jr. It did not challenge the essential correctness of Austin's description, nor did it question the accuracy of the assertion about Edwards, Jr.'s, role in transcribing the Redemption Discourse

-- 024 --

course booklets.2 This suggests that Edwards, Jr.'s, work as transcriber of his father's manuscripts was common knowledge among the son's associates.

This reconstruction of how the Redemption Discourse was transcribed and readied for publication has recently been confirmed by the discovery of evidence in Jonathan Edwards, Jr.'s, hand. In a letter dated April 28, 1792, Isaiah Thomas, who reprinted the Erskine edition of A History of the Work of Redemption at Worcester (1792), wrote to Edwards, Jr., expressing surprise that the son might have any legal claim to it. In drafting a rather irritated reply on the same sheet, the son presented the basis for his claim in the following language: "I was at the pains of transcribing and publishing that volume at a time when the laws of our country secured nothing to authors or proprietors of manuscripts." In his response Thomas rejected Edwards, Jr.'s, claim. Thus the son's role in transcribing the Redemption sermon series in New Haven during the early 1770s is clear. There is, further, a strong presumption for thinking that the original manuscript booklets never crossed the Atlantic and that their editor and publisher, John Erskine, worked solely from the son's transcription.3

In general the son was respectful of the integrity of his father's manuscripts, as Hopkins had been. Occasionally he undertook to rework some of Edwards' prose. This intervention was especially pronounced in the case of his transcriptions of some "Miscellaneous Observations." At a very few points his handwriting may also be identified in the Redemption booklets themselves.4 It seems likely that Edwards, Jr., did not have a collaborator in the transcription of this series of sermons, although it is remotely conceivable that he made use of earlier drafts by Hopkins—and possibly others. The difficulty of Edwards' hand, the absence of a ready market for additional published materials by him, and the sheer length of the manuscripts, however, all make such collaboration or dependence improbable.

We have already noted that John Erskine's advertisement to his edition, dated Edinburgh, April 29, 1774, reports that the younger Edwards entrusted the manuscripts to him for editing. We return now to Erskine's treatment of the materials he was to publish as A History of the Work of Redemption. He clearly exercised the prerogative of editor in reducing the sermons to a coherent treatise. In this he followed the logical structure Edwards gave to the whole, dividing the Work of Redemption into three great periods, and further subdividing each of these either into relevant subperiods or according to analytic principles. (He used the analytic method especially with the middle period, comprising Jesus' life with its purchase of redemption, and in the projection of events that would complete the last period beyond Edwards' own time.) Erskine suppressed the marks of the preaching occasions, for example, the repetition of text and doctrine or recapitulation of the preceding sermon(s), which Edwards often provided. In doing so, he did readers a disservice, for these introductory sections were often preludes to Edwards' further development of his argument and had relevance to his thesis as well as some literary interest. On the other hand, Erskine did not delete such substantial sermon-derived sections as the improvement at the end of the first period (which comprised Sermon Thirteen) or the application at the end of the second period (in Sermon Seventeen)—and certainly not the application of the whole (which begins in Sermon Twenty-nine and takes up the whole of the concluding, and thirtieth, sermon).

At a less general level, presumably it was Jonathan Edwards, Jr., who exercised the prerogative of completing certain sections that his father had left as little more than outlines of his argument. Often this occurred when he was compiling scriptural warrants for a position or when he was developing an interpretation of a particular verse. Indeed, if we assume that the original manuscripts (Edwards' sermon booklets) remained with his son in New Haven, Erskine may never have known how relatively unfinished and incomplete some sections of the Redemption Discourse actually were. A History of the Work of Redemption. Containing, The Outlines of a Body of Divinity, In a Method entirely new was published in Edinburgh for W. Gray in 1774. There are no grounds to suppose that any subsequent version rested on additional study of the original manuscripts, or for that matter, on familiarity with Jonathan Edwards, Jr.'s, transcription of them.

The Published Versions Of A History Of The Work Of Redemption

John Erskine's 1774 edition of A History of the Work of Redemption has frequently been republished in English.5 In 1782 it was reissued in Edinburgh and reprinted at Boston as well. It was first issued in New York in 1786. Before 1800 this same version was reprinted three more times in Edinburgh (1788, 1793, 1799), and also in Worcester, Massachusetts (1792). In addition, an augmented version, known as the "Pitcher edition" after its editor, George Pitcher, was brought out in London in 1788. This included historical, critical, and theological notes along with a section on "The Life and Experience of the Author." The Pitcher text has some minor variations from Erskine's, but there is no basis for supposing that it rested on a fresh transcription of the manuscripts or even reference to them or to Jonathan Edwards, Jr.'s, transcription. In all respects the Pitcher edition seems to derive from the Erskine text in an attempt to improve it. There is no possibility of independent reference to the original. The same applies to the Pitcher edition as brought out by Swords in New York in 1793. This later edition (Pitcher and then Swords as derived from it) suppresses Erskine's advertisement, offers a much fuller syllabus than Erskine's table of contents, appends extensive notes, and provides an index of references. But none of this elaborate paraphernalia derives from Edwards or has any basis in his manuscripts (sermon booklets or notebooks).

In general Pitcher's departures from the Erskine version take the form of occasionally eliminating a redundant sentence. A selective analysis of variations between these editions shows that the Erskine text is always fuller and always stands closer to the original manuscript. It is proof of the complete dependence of the Pitcher version on the Erskine version that it follows Erskine in sections of the text that Edwards, Jr. (or Erskine) undertook to complete or otherwise finish, sections left in rough form in Jonathan Edwards' preaching booklets. At most it could be claimed that Pitcher had access to the transcription that Erskine edited, but it is more reasonable to suppose that Pitcher's version derived wholly from Erskine's.

In addressing the issue of variations between the Erskine and Pitcher versions of the texts, the editor of the Worcester edition of

-- 027 --

Edwards' Works explicitly chose to use Erskine's corrected 1793 text (the fourth Edinburgh edition).


This [the 1793 text] is according to the form to which the manuscript was reduced by Dr. Erskine, and appears to have been printed with uncommon accuracy, probably under the eye of the Doctor. This is preferred to the London Edition, published in the year '88 by Mr. Pitcher. The Editor of this Edition [Pitcher], though he appears to have been conscientiously careful to preserve the sentiments of Mr. Edwards, has so varied the style, that we cease to rely that what we have is the composition of the original author.6

While this judgment about the relative merit of these versions is undoubtedly correct, the characterization of the Pitcher edition overstates its degree of independence from Erskine's edition, insofar as the author seems to imply that Pitcher had benefited from access to the original manuscripts.

In the nineteenth century the original Erskine edition was reissued numerous times, especially in Edinburgh and Worcester, Massachusetts. Both the Religious Tract Society (London) and the American Tract Society (New York) also kept it available in print. The Pitcher edition was reissued twice in London. Beyond this, of course, A History of the Work of Redemption was a standard part of the various collected editions of Edwards' works, beginning with that issued at Leeds in 1806–1811 (8 vols.). The most important collection came out at Worcester in 1808, and numerous others followed.

Finally, A History of the Work of Redemption was translated. It was brought out almost immediately in Dutch at Utrecht (1776). In the nineteenth century it was issued twice in Welsh (at Bala in 1829; later, undated, from New York, by the American Tract Society). A translation into French was published at Toulouse in 1854, and one into Arabic at Beirut in 1868. Without question A History of the Work of Redemption proved to be one of the most widely distributed of Jonathan Edwards' writings. No doubt its greatest influence was in the popular culture as a "historical" construction of evangelical consciousness rather than as a strictly philosophical or theological treatise like Religious Affections or Freedom of the Will. In this sense it informed

-- 028 --

the nineteenth-century evangelical understanding of the world and served as a framework within which progressive refinement of theories of dispensations took place.

2. Redemption as God's Greatest Work
Redemption As A Theological Preoccupation In The New England Puritan Tradition

Redemption was a fundamental theme in New England Puritan thought from its beginnings. Thomas Hooker's writings exemplify this preoccupation in his early published sermons.7 This venerable patriarch had been a leader in the migration from old England in the 1630s, and also in the subsequent departure of a small group that removed to the Connecticut River Valley from the Massachusetts Bay patent. Modern critical scholarship has but followed him in confirming the centrality of redemption by emphasizing the questions of "visible sainthood" and "preparation for salvation." Jonathan Edwards' initial publication took redemption as its topic: God Glorified in the Work of Redemption, by the Greatness of Man's Dependence upon Him, in the whole of It was preached as a public lecture in Boston in 1731.8 A brief review of his "Miscellanies" has indicated that he repeatedly returned to the subject throughout his life, but especially in the early years of his maturity, at Northampton. His composition of thirty sermons as "A History of the Work of Redemption" was entirely congruent with the broader tradition in which he stood, and it was also the development of a theme already central in his own work.

What is noteworthy about the Redemption Discourse is not so much the subject but the approach to it which Edwards chose to develop. Earlier tradition was largely given to close analysis of the "application of redemption"—the phrase was used by Hooker—in the soul of the Christian.9 The great theological doctrines of the incarnation of the divine as human in Jesus as the Christ and the Savior's atonement for human sin so as to achieve salvation for the faithful were taken for granted in this literature. The center of concern was how this

-- 029 --

objective given of the spiritual economy might be rendered real in the lives of the individual saints who were its subjects—the application of redemption translated into a concern for the morphology of conversion and became a preoccupation with visible sainthood.1 It entailed a scrupulous analysis of the steps to salvation, especially in the lives of individual saints, but not excluding salvation's collective expression.2

These interests substantially overlapped with those of the adherents of Pietism, which developed in Continental circles in the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries.3 The Pietists were deeply influenced by English Puritanism's concern with the application of redemption. And a mutual interest continued on the part of German-speaking Pietists and English-speaking Puritans throughout the seventeenth and into the eighteenth centuries. These traditions interpenetrated in the middle Colonies of the New World in Edwards' lifetime, and this circumstance must be seen as background to the Great Awakening in that region.4 What distinguished these cognate traditions was their respective emphases and focuses. The Puritan tradition initially developed and nurtured the Reformed commitment to renovate the common life as a whole as well as individual Christians and their purified churches; the Pietist tradition carried forward a more typically Lutheran attitude of greater indifference toward the social and political context within which redeemed individuals and their religious groupings lived.

The side of the Puritan tradition concerned with the application of redemption and visible sainthood continues strongly in Edwards. In his revival preaching he was preeminently the skilled "doctor of salvation," simultaneously holding forth the possibility of redemption and the terror of everlasting damnation. Thus "Sinners in the Hands

-- 030 --

of an Angry God" deserves the attention it has received as an exemplar of his preaching.5 But it was preaching of one particular kind—that directed to securing the redeemed in their faith through holding forth with exceptional vigor the dreadful alternative to it. He also took a vital interest in the application of redemption, and this theme stands behind the 1734 awakening chronicled in his Faithful Narrative, as well as behind the Great Awakening. His reflections issued in a series of increasingly discriminating analyses of the spiritual life, culminating in the masterful treatise Religious Affections.6 Here the full range of Edwards' analytic skills was marshaled to review the experience of the subjects of redemption.

The application of redemption provided the focus for much of Edwards' mature career. For example, the energy he devoted to that ambitious transatlantic revival movement, the Concert of Prayer, was precisely the direct outcome of his concern, although it also showed his continuing sense of (and clearly enhanced appreciation for) the connection between the New England and at least the Scottish traditions.7 In addition, he seems to have been aware of the Continental Pietism of Spener at Halle and the role of Zinzendorf in both Europe and the Middle Colonies. Another example is his reversal of Stoddard's relatively open position on membership in the church, a direct expression of Edwards' continuing conviction that "closing with salvation" was a matter of utmost urgency. It is imperative to insist, however, that while he focused great energies on the application of redemption, he did not make the transition in his own work that would come about in the next century. This was an increasing preoccupation among Romantics with redemption viewed subjectively.8

Within the tradition of nineteenth-century religious thought in America reaching from Edwards, this transition or turn can be perceived

-- 031 --

developing in both orthodox and heterodox ways. The thought of a Horace Bushnell, to take the first case, has developed far toward a modern position that since language as a cultural product is the medium in which religious claims are made, any religious claims advanced are necessarily rooted in the human subject.9 While Bushnell remained committed to the comprehensive and traditional Christian confession, for him it was essentially just that—a compelling description of the phenomenal world, not a secure disclosure of the noumenal realm. On the other side, Ralph Waldo Emerson was no less preoccupied with redemption in the experience of its subjects. But for him the traditional Christian symbols were equated with, or reduced to, symbols derived from nature, and the life of the spirit was construed in thoroughly heterodox terms.1 But for us the central issue is that Edwards' concern with redemption represented his appropriation of central elements of the New England religious tradition that would, in turn, be developed further by others.

As important as it is to observe that Edwards' concern for redemption as the most basic religious category was carrying forward a prominent strand of New England culture, we must note that he also turned these concerns in a different direction entirely. Here he proposed systematic attention to the "objective" side of the issue: redemption seen in relationship to the whole of creation as the means to comprehend the relationship of the world to God. His continuing reflection on these questions led him to carry the question even further back, into conjecture that the human understanding of the Work of Redemption established the nature of the Godhead as irreducibly trinitarian. The late essay "The End for Which God Created the World," posthumously published in Two Dissertations, addressed the question of the limits of human understanding of redemption.2 But it was already an implicit issue in the Redemption Discourse, though not as fully developed. As has been suggested, the "Miscellanies" show its presence even earlier. In effect, Edwards' argument was, so to speak, that if creation is a stage, the purpose of which is to permit

-- 032 --

the drama of redemption to be played out, the outcome of the drama (and thus the reason for creation) is God's self-glorification. He does not agonize at length over why the created world exists, for its purpose is to effect the redemption of mankind, the objective side of the process so thoroughly inquired into on its subjective side both in the tradition in which Edwards stood and in his own work. A framework such as this suggests why his "history" of the Work of Redemption was so central to his intellectual life and how he approached this major project. It also helps to explain why he always intended it to be a summary theological statement.

Edwards defined the Work of Redemption as taking place between "the fall of man and the end of the world," or last judgment.3 The series of events constituting this "history" properly was given in narrative or dramatic terms. But although his project depended profoundly on the Bible and grew out of it, we should not suppose that Edwards' sources for his discussion were confined to it. It is also wrong to suppose that his efforts are to be understood as an exercise in bibliolatry. For him, the Scripture offered an incomplete and complex presentation of the "history," and, moreover, the scriptural version could be easily misread. At the same time, Scripture provided accounts of events that Edwards thought critical in God's Work of Redemption. Examples are the redemption of Israel out of Egypt and the securing of David's kingship over Israel. Edwards augmented the incomplete "historical" sections with Scripture prophecy, in and through which the narrative account of the redemptive scheme was interpreted and fleshed out. This gave him not only a correct understanding of events narrated in Scripture history, but also permitted interpretation of events that transpired outside its purview (examples being the significance of the rise of the Roman empire and the contribution classical pagan philosophy made to the scheme of redemption). Prophecy also disclosed events yet to come that would complete God's Work of Redemption (the destruction of Antichrist and the last judgment). So although there is a sense in which the "history" of the Work of Redemption depends upon the Scriptures and certainly could not be comprehended by humans without them, the Scriptures do not contain its entirety. Indeed, the Scriptures might be said to have as their divine purpose or intention the work itself.

-- 033 --

It is crucial to identify precisely the social location and the nature of the account of God's Work of Redemption which Edwards finally created. His "history" is addressed to a community which is potentially redeemed, as an exhortation to its members to be saved, to "close with salvation." These sermon-discourses seek to engage their hearers and readers. If creation is the stage and God's Work of Redemption the drama being played out upon it, Scripture is the established text of what has taken place in the drama and the partial text of what is to come. To continue the analogy, Edwards is part aspiring producer, part theater critic. His sermon hearers and treatise readers are at once unwittingly acting, attending the performance, devouring reviews, and yearning for consummation of the project. This analogy, like all analogies, breaks down at this point, and such a comparison with the stage might in any case have struck Edwards as at best frivolous and at worst blasphemous. But it does help to suggest to the modern critical reader the complex relationships between Godhead, Scripture, redemption, Edwards' text, the hearers, and finally the readers that his project comprises. We must focus on the text of the Redemption Discourse to understand what makes it so effective, while recognizing that Edwards' preaching of his "history" was part exposition of the Work of Redemption, part production of its story, and part critical commentary upon it.

To analyze the Redemption Discourse, appreciation for the sermon form is crucial. Beyond extensive attention to this question we will make and emphasize a distinction between, on the one hand, the narrative base of the text (which recounts events in serial order) and, on the other, various levels of meaning invoked to interpret events that took place in biblical times, have taken place since then, or are yet to take place, in relationship to the redemptive process. It is important to emphasize that events in this narrative base already carry an interpretation—these are not neutral occurrences, now available for the first time. Rather, these occurrences are already very familiar, and their significance is established, like the rescue of one righteous man and his family from a flood so that they can be bearers of salvation, or the release of a people under captivity in Babylon, or the fall of Jerusalem before the might of Rome, or the embrace of Christianity by an empire. These events are basic themes already established, but in their perfection, the whole is enriched by skillful anticipation of later themes and extensive elaboration of earlier ones as the consummation of all things approaches. Like the playing of a

-- 034 --

piece of music or the production of a play for that matter, the whole adds up to so much more than the parts taken in isolation.

Edwards' devices for inventing meanings or elaborating interconnections were relatively conventional. In effect, he availed himself of a series of literary strategies which involved claims about the revelatory power of isolated units in the narrative base. The strongest claim is the assertion that at several points Christ actually appeared to Israel before his incarnation. Slightly less strong claims concern a much broader class of materials: prophecies. A weaker level of claim, still very important, is made by typology. Here the individual or event is a figure that, while retaining its own integrity in the narrative base, yet discloses another reality. As we shall see, Edwards uses this method fully, but he feels free to apply typology less rigorously as well. Still weaker forms of elaboration of the basic narrative account occur in claims concerning images or shadows of fundamental reality.

To move into a systematic analysis of Edwards' literary strategies necessarily misrepresents them, because it treats as separate elements that are fused in his text. But it ought to be evident that, already in the thirty sermons preached in 1739, he had moved far toward the conception of a treatise or sustained essay on the fundamentals of the Christian religion, which he would organize in terms of the objective side of the redemptive process, namely, God's Work of Redemption. The sermons stand without the ambitious development he intended for them. But they stand also as a literary achievement in their own right, without the revision, refinement, and extension he would have given them.

The Redemption Discourse And The Transformation Of The Sermon Form

While Jonathan Edwards worked within the traditional structure of the sermon, he was also engaged in transforming it. Sermon series, like "Charity and Its Fruits" preached the preceding year, were traditionally developed around the exegesis of a series of verses, a scriptural chapter, or even a whole biblical book. The preaching unit was defined through the rhetorical conventions of the Puritan tradition. A scriptural text, a verse (or part thereof) was explicated so as to develop it in relation to its setting and to clarify it in terms of content. Then a doctrine or rational statement of the principle conveyed through the text was enunciated. The sermon developed "observations" or "reasons," that is, rational elaborations of the doctrine,

-- 035 --

drawing upon a range of sources both within the tradition and outside it. References to classical authors were included, as well as historical precedents, allusions to current events, and commonsense observations. This served as the core of the sermon. Finally, the implications of the whole for the auditors were pointed out; the sermon was "improved" for their benefit. This marvelously efficient instrument provided a framework within which the preacher could suit his delivery to circumstance and develop variations as warranted by occasion. Its tripartite structure suggested the conventional threefold psychological model in which human nature was thought to involve reason, will, and emotions.4 In making use of this form, Edwards was simply appropriating a recognized tradition that had proved effective since evolving to roughly this stage among Puritans in the late Elizabethan years.5

Edwards explicitly framed the Redemption Discourse using these basic elements of text, doctrine, and application or improvement. He did not isolate a distinct section of "reasons," although three "propositions" or specifications of the doctrine guided the development of the whole. His rather full explication of the doctrine in Sermon One, after he had briefly introduced the text, may indicate that the conventional structure had undergone a modification in which rational elaboration was drawn into and conflated with text and doctrine. Where this occurs, some have proposed, we have an adaptation of the rhetorical form that indicates a significant change in assumptions about human psychology.6 That may certainly be an important insight into Edwards as preacher and a significant aspect of the Great Awakening

-- 036 --

in American culture. At least with respect to the Redemption Discourse, however, it is more important to view the substantial part of these thirty sermons—that not explicitly devoted to text, doctrine, or application—as an elaboration of the rational content that makes it the central argument of the whole. In this framework, the "history" of the Work of Redemption precisely fulfilled the role conventionally played by "reasons" in Puritan preaching: sustained and compelling arguments for the doctrine adduced from Scripture and redemptive events, assembled as authoritative and directed to the will. According to this interpretation, Edwards was developing the sermon structure to the point at which the central core of rational argumentation was virtually a treatise in its own right, though of course he would introduce it by text and doctrine and conclude it by improvement.

We have noted that Edwards' text and doctrine are both fully explicated in the course of the first of the thirty sermons. Thereafter he devotes eleven preaching occasions to a close discussion of the dispensations through which redemption was advanced from the fall of man to the coming of Christ. This development of God's Work of Redemption takes place under the general text and doctrine, with additional "observations" about the last dispensation. Their improvement or application is concentrated in the thirteenth sermon. Nevertheless, the eleven sermons stand as a rational statement that has a distinctively historical cast. Under the same text and doctrine, three more sermons offer a rational theological analysis (rather than a historical discussion) of the period of Christ's humiliation or life; improvement of this section is made in Sermon Seventeen. With Sermon Eighteen, Edwards begins to discuss the Work of Redemption from Christ's resurrection to the end of the world and its eventual return to God. The same text from Isaiah and the same doctrine frame this section. A short concluding section of Sermon Twenty-one is explicitly an improvement or application, as is all of Sermon Twenty-five. The last part of Sermon Twenty-nine and all of Sermon Thirty are set out as an improvement of the whole.

At this level of analysis it is clear that text, doctrine, and improvement—conventional elements of the traditional sermon form—were significant in Edwards' sermons on the Work of Redemption, probably comprising between 20 and 25 percent of the manuscript materials. They provide a form and set a framework that governs the whole. But it is equally clear that the rational core of the series, if we interpret this element as central to a sermon discourse, is his sustained

-- 037 --

analysis of how redemption has been, and is being, worked out by God. Thus the substance of the sermons—perhaps 75 percent—stands as a largely narrative account of the redemption of the world by God, indeed a "history" of it. In general, the thirty sermons have been read as if they were that history, and there is no doubt that had Edwards revised them, the rational account in a largely narrative mode would have been at least as pronounced. The revised "history" would have appeared even more treatiselike. (Indeed, the treatise Religious Affections can be read beside the "Distinguishing Marks" as essentially an elaboration and refinement of the central rational section of that one sermon.7) But it would be a grave error to assume that the narrative sections of the Redemption Discourse, which bulk so large in the sermons (and which might well have had increased prominence as a result of revision), in fact constitute a "history" in a modern sense. Instead, the theoretical framework for the narrative and rational sections is derived from the text and doctrine, and the application and improvements were intended to engage the human subject so as to further the Work of Redemption. The degree of subordination of the narrative account within the doctrinal framework is made clear in the outline of the sermon series given in table 1.8 The Redemption Discourse had controlling theological premises and was intended to eventuate in religious practice or conversion. In this sense, in spite of his transformation of the sermon form, Edwards anchored the Redemption Discourse securely within that tradition.

Edwards' choice of a text from Isaiah, "For the moth shall eat them up like a garment, and the worm shall eat them like wool, but my righteousness shall be for ever, and my salvation from generation to generation," seems an improbable starting point for his ambitious project. In his "Blank Bible," where he noted the significance of particular verses, his single entry concerning Isaiah 51:8 reads, "This verse is a proof of the indestructibility of the Church."9 But at least two comments are in order. First, it was a central tenet among conscientious Protestants from the days of Luther that the Old Testament contained the Christian gospel implicitly. Edwards' development of a

-- 038 --

most general statement about the divine Work of Redemption on the basis of a text from the Old Testament which embodied homely metaphors is not especially noteworthy. Second, he chose a text that, not uncharacteristically for a statement attributed to God, contrasted God's everlasting righteousness and resolute intention to achieve salvation for his people with the fragility and transitoriness of earthly goods. This assured a dialectical framework for the development of the series.

The formulation of the doctrine makes Edwards' intention certain: "The Work of Redemption is a work that God carries on from the fall of man to the end of the world."1 Thus we can understand God's deliverance of his people by understanding his Work of Redemption alone, and that process includes "all God's works that were properly preparatory to the purchase [of redemption by Christ's incarnation, death, and resurrection] or as applying the purchase and accomplishing the success of it."2 Indeed, as the great fruits of redemption are eternal—extending beyond the end of the world—so the plans for it were laid in the "confederation" of the persons of the Trinity in "a design and a covenant of redemption" before its creation.3 So the myriad mundane events Edwards reviews to describe the stages of the process have their true import in terms of the cosmic drama in which evil will be vanquished and each person of the Trinity glorified. The very end, intention, or purpose of God in the creation of the world and its return to God is the central theme of the whole. The homely quality of the text did not detract from, but made all the more striking, the extraordinary range and reach of the doctrine framed upon it.

For Edwards God's Work of Redemption has three major segments (see table 2). These are separate and distinct periods which mark out different stages in its "history." The first segment runs from the fall of humanity to the coming of Christ. The second is defined by the great christic event, including the coming of Jesus, his ministry, death, and the resurrection, which achieves the redemption of humanity. In the third and last stage, the redemption that was so purchased will be realized. The logic is that preparation leads to purchase in and through the life of Jesus as the Christ, which is then applied in and carried to completion through the life of the church.

-- 039 --

The three periods are but subdivisions, however, of one integral process, the Work of Redemption, that is, God's most fundamental relationship to the world. But each period does have special characteristics. The first, for example, comprehends six distinctive administrations or dispensations, a term that Edwards uses less technically than is the rule in later evangelicalism.4 The second is treated as one event, although in two distinct theological perspectives. The third is composed of four separate and overlapping movements or progressive administrations in which the earlier events are taken up in the later and completed in the last.

The first of the three great periods is discussed in Sermons Two through Twelve. It is defined by his interpretation of the Old Testament, which he thought not only pointed to the coming of Christ, but defined the Christ who would come. At the most general level this was simply a conventional Protestant version of the Christian interpretation of these materials: the scriptures of ancient Israel established the necessity for a messiah actually to complete the redemptive story embodied in them. The interpretive framework Edwards appropriated to set out and make this case was more distinctive. His scheme depended on a figural and, more specifically, typological theory of scriptural interpretation. This enabled him to find coherence where chaos might otherwise be thought to reign.

Sermon Thirteen is cast as an improvement of the first great period of the redemptive story. With Sermon Fourteen Edwards turns to analyze in three sermons the second great period, that of Christ's humiliation. With Sermon Eighteen he shifts his attention to the third and last period, which reaches from Christ's resurrection to the end of the world. The Work of Redemption will be completed as time is brought to a close—but for Edwards the outcome will continue, beyond time. Four great events define the stages of this period and accomplish the inauguration of Christ's kingdom. These four are not so much separate and discrete, however, as cumulative; each successive event incorporates those that went before and issues in yet a further development. This makes the achievement of Christ's kingdom gradual, and the result, Edwards argues, is that God's glory is the more visible and his triumph over Satan the more glorious.

With the judgment day, the last, summary event will bring "history"

-- 040 --

to a close. Christ will appear in glory with the angels, and the elect will be saved. The dead will be raised, and the living will be changed at the last trumpet. Finally, the whole church of saints will be caught up in the air, gathered together with Christ, leaving the wicked earth and Satan behind to be judged. While Christ, the saints, and the angels ascend toward heaven, and the church enters eternal blessedness, the world will be set on fire. The church in glory will realize what was foreshadowed by the church under grace. In its delivery to the Father, time will be transcended and redemption accomplished.

The whole series of sermons concludes with an extensive improvement (part of Sermon Twenty-nine and all of Sermon Thirty). But Edwards' elaboration of the "history" as a rational analysis of God's Work of Redemption assumed a life of its own. The metamorphosis of the sermon form was virtually complete, for text, doctrine, and application had all become subordinate to the rational elaboration. The sermon form was stretched across thirty preaching occasions and turned inside out. This form acted as a chrysalis: within it the process of change that held the promise of a treatise occurred. In A History of the Work of Redemption as published, this transformation is fixed and the metamorphosis stopped. Had their author lived to rework these materials, there is no doubt that they would have issued as a treatise comparable to The Freedom of the Will or The Great Christian Doctrine of Original Sin.5

Typology In Protestantism And Puritanism And In The Redemption Discourse

We have noted the grand scheme, pattern, or "history" of the Work of Redemption as Edwards sketched it out in his thirty sermons of 1739. It was a rational analysis and account of the divine action in the salvation of fallen mankind. Its terminus a quo was set by the fall of mankind, and its terminus ad quem by the end of history, signaled in the last judgment and the salvation of the church. In comprehending human existence it stands as the most fundamental doctrine with respect to the divine relationship to humanity and the world, including all other doctrines. In characteristic fashion, Edwards argued that while other divine activities, for example, in creating the world, might, strictly speaking, have preceded and been presupposed in that of redeeming humanity through time, redemption was in fact

-- 041 --

their purpose or final end. So he viewed the doctrine of providence as subordinate to that of redemption. Indeed, if redemption was superordinate to creation and providence, it stood as subordinate only to God's self-glorification. This conferred a significance upon redemption that explains the high claims he made on its behalf. In the perspective of traditional Christian teaching, of course, especially concerning sin, humanity offers woefully inadequate grounds for any claim to redemption. By contrast, God's self-glorification, implying his design to redeem humanity, explained the significance Edwards attached to the project.

This high Calvinist position stands behind Edwards' work and makes it intelligible, and the actual "history" he sketched in broad outline is equally derivative. The early Christian community had worked through the issue of how Christianity understood itself in relation to ancient Israel, the chosen people of God. Theoretically, Christianity might have deemphasized its sources in Judaism (indeed, its origins as a Jewish revitalization movement) and developed much more as a Gnostic cult or mystery religion in the culturally plural setting of the Roman empire. This would have required maturing Christianity to interpret its claims in mythic terms, or through philosophical terminology. In some respects, of course, Gnosticism can be seen as such a version of the Christian movement. But the orthodox tradition, defined in councils and through creeds and secured through institutionalization, went the route of emphasizing that Christianity was the fulfillment of those particular expectations for salvation fostered in ancient Israel, expectations preserved in rather different versions within Jewish rabbinic circles. So in sketching the content of the scheme of the first great period of the divine redemption of humanity in terms of the Christian appropriation of the sacred traditions of Israel (its own Old Testament), Edwards stands securely in a tradition of at least fifteen hundred years. How he derived that content from the scriptures of ancient Israel, however, resulted from no such consensus within Western Christianity.

The interpretation Edwards gave to the second great period of his scheme, the center of history, when Christ, the second person of the Trinity, was incarnate, was no less traditional and orthodox. His emphasis was entirely upon the Christ figure. Unlike the preoccupation with the human Jesus as a historical figure which developed in the nineteenth century, culminating in sentimental portrayals of Jesus as an everlasting contemporary and a moral exemplar, Edwards' interest

-- 042 --

was in the second person of the Trinity and the central salvific exchange attributed to Christ. That this Christ became human, and radically enough human to achieve the salvation intended, was the important point. It was the actuality of the incarnation—the reality as God-man—that mattered, not the details of the life, or even, for that matter, its integrity as a human life, except insofar as that was required for the incarnation to be genuine. In this respect also he was arguing from a very traditional perspective. The salvific work of Jesus as Christ was achieved through satisfying the demands of the divine law by which humanity stood condemned. From his infancy, through his private life and public ministry, culminating in betrayal and death, the Savior's utter obedience completed the purchase of redemption. As human, Jesus fulfilled the law and satisfied its every demand, thus manifesting the central Christian virtues; as divine, Christ thereby completed the purchase of redemption. This construction of the work of Jesus Christ begins so early in the Christian movement that it is embedded in its scriptures. Modern interpretations which significantly qualify or enlarge it chiefly came to flower only in the nineteenth- and twentieth-century liberal theological programs. In his interpretation of the second great and central period, then, Edwards must be counted as basically in accord with tradition.

It is in the interpretation of the third, and yet to be completed, period that Edwards is less securely orthodox and traditional. In the broadly catholic tradition, East and West, emphasis was given to the church as an institution that would preserve the means of salvation and offer them to humanity. In this view, the church would itself be taken up in the last judgment and united to God forever, an emphasis echoed by Edwards. He reflected even more directly, however, the alternative position espoused by some medieval heretics and the Protestants that the biblical prophecies made available the divine plan for concluding the sojourn of humanity on earth. In particular, this approach entailed claiming to understand the significance of the bizarre symbols and striking imagery in Daniel and especially in the book of Revelation. In looking for a blow to Satan and expecting a millennial period of felicity for the saints before a final apostasy and the last judgment at the end of time, Edwards espoused positions that had, in their form at least, been codified only in his recent past. Whereas his interpretations of the first two periods were conservative, his construction of the third was comparatively innovative.

-- 043 --

This search beneath the surface narrative to identify different substructures for the three basic periods suggests that additional probing might disclose a fundamental level, at which there is a unity of the whole. In Edwards' view, what links secured the events of Christian history to the stages of Israel's experience? How did he relate the compelling clarity of the central christic period to the mixed and Confused first period, or to the frankly ambiguous third? To raise this issue directs attention to the basic terms by means of which he achieves unity: first, a reliance upon, and appropriation of, the figural tradition of interpreting the Old Testament and the New; second, the specification of this tradition in typology; and finally, the expansion of claims made in its name. This dynamic substratum supports both the discreteness of the several periods and the narrative unity of the whole in the Redemption Discourse.

As early Christianity laid claim to the ancient Jewish scriptures as its own, the issue of their interpretation necessarily came to the fore. Erich Auerbach has been especially influential in calling attention to the importance of figuralism as a means of understanding a development that deeply affected Western literature. He proposed that figural interpretation "establishes a connection between two events or persons in such a way that the first signifies not only itself but also the second, while the second involves or fulfills the first. The two poles of a figure are separated in time, but both, being real events or persons, are within temporality."6 The linkage between them has nothing to do with temporal or causal considerations, that is to say, juxtaposition or connection in space or time. Rather,


Both occurrences are … linked to Divine Providence, which alone is able to devise such a plan of history and supply the key to its understanding.… The temporal and causal … connection of occurrences is dissolved; the here and now is no longer a mere link in an earthly chain of events, it is simultaneously something which has always been, and which will be fulfilled in the future; and strictly, in the eyes of God, it is something eternal, something omni-temporal, something already consummated in the realm of fragmentary earthly event.7

-- 044 --

In such a perspective we see at once that while the three periods of Edwards' "history" of the Work of Redemption were separate and discrete, they nevertheless had a deep unity and formed a whole in terms of the figural tradition and its representation of reality. More exactly, Edwards made use of a specific development of that tradition in terms of typology, an ancient literary strategy that had become especially significant again since the sixteenth century in Protestant, and in particular English Protestant, circles.8

One hallmark of the Protestant revolt in early sixteenth-century Northern European cultures was rejection of the kind of exegesis that had flourished under Roman Catholicism. The medieval context produced a multilevel approach to the interpretation of Scripture, particularly as a means of dealing with its great variety (and indeed the outright disjunction between its parts). In general, allegorization was the means to this end. If a mundane event stood for or symbolized something else, the way was open to deal effectively with the hard places of Scripture. Many versions of this approach had developed over the centuries. The most widely known formulation posited four levels in the biblical text—the basic literal or historical level, the allegorical (a reading usually heavily Christological), the tropological (a figurative and/or moral level of meaning), and the anagogical (interpretation of the text in terms of an ultimate or spiritual sense). That this approach—and those more complex ones that attained less currency or recognition—was ingenious and effective is beyond dispute. What concerns us, however, is the reaction against this manifold interpretation of Scripture on the part of Luther, Calvin, and their followers. As if with a single voice the Protestants called for a return to the literal or historical interpretation of Scripture as the only construction that God had intended.9

The reasons why the early Protestants took this position are many, diverse, and interesting. In part, theirs was a reaction against what

-- 045 --

they saw as the unwarranted elaboration of the fundamentals of the Christian religion. To them this paralleled the growth of religious orders and the extension of institutional sacraments, as well as the open-ended development of ritual. The result had been a fecund liturgical life for the church. Against this background, the Reformation was a call for a return to basics, for a revitalization of original Christianity. Further impetus for simplification came from the Protestant commitment to end the hierarchical reign of priests in the church and to elevate all Christians to be ministers of grace to each other. In such a program of reduction there was little room for the extremely complex approach to exegesis embodied in the manifold interpretation of Scripture. So Luther, Calvin, and their collaborators called for a return to Scripture's literal sense. While that attitude did not rule out the figural framework that we have already identified (for instance, Luther found the whole Bible—and he most explicitly included the Old Testament—to be the cradle of Christ), the first generation of reformers used such literary strategies with caution and restraint.

The second generation of reformers in Switzerland, however, while maintaining the commitment to exegetical literalism, began to explore in more detail characteristically hard passages such as Canticles, certain of the Psalms, and apocalyptic sections of Scripture. In these cases belief in their literal-historical fulfillment raised difficult questions. Theodore Beza, for example, emphasized that many Old Testament persons were shadows, similitudes, or figures of Christ and his church; David, for one, became the "figure and shadow" of Christ.1 Such similitudes were also thought to refer to contemporary events through the agency of the Holy Spirit. Heinrich Bullinger drew attention to apocalyptic passages in Daniel 2, Matthew 24, and Luke 21, which he thought foretold events of his own time. Furthermore, Revelation provided particular insight into the drama scheduled for the end of time and believed then to be taking place.2 Thus the second generation of Protestants gave more emphasis to a typological interpretation, especially of the Old Testament, through which, among other things, Christ could be read as its fulfillment.

-- 046 --

Subsequent Swiss dogmaticians explored the possibilities opened up by this systematic development of typology. Johannes Wolleb (Wollebius), for instance, explicitly used it in his Compendium Theologiae Christianae of 1626. The Old Testament's ceremonial and its political order were believed to prefigure Christ on earth.3 Late in the century, Johann Heidegger made much of the Old Testament as providing types, pronouncing in his Medulla Theologiae Christianae (1696): "Generally speaking types are things which impinge upon the senses, ordered by God, to represent to the mind by some similitude a thing of greater moment pertaining to religion."4 Francis Turretin (Turrettini), the Genevan systematizer who deeply influenced New England theology, emphasized the progressive disclosure of divine intention. His three ages of the Old Testament were in effect stages moving toward the perfect manifestation of the messiah.5 In their development of Reformed theology Swiss theologians in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries increasingly used typology—but always within a conservative framework. The types pointed to Christ and were there fulfilled. These writers seem to have restrained any inclination to expand typological reference.

Among Scottish and English theologians, however, such an expansion of typology did begin. Scottish Covenanters especially tended to read their immediate struggles and goals in terms of apocalyptic symbols in the Bible. Here there was more than a hint of legitimating their own political actions through biblical types. The English civil struggles of the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries provided an even richer field for the luxuriant growth of claims both that scriptural symbols, events, and passages enabled the protagonists to legitimate themselves and that, indeed, they were living out (realizing) the true import of biblical revelation. This development has been helpfully chronicled by Mason Lowance in The Language of Canaan. More particularly, a significant step was taken by William Perkins, the influential Elizabethan Puritan, who made room for something like a "double literal sense." This position was then expanded far beyond

-- 047 --

Perkins' original position, in the flowering of English Puritanism in the seventeenth century.6 A volume by Samuel Mather of Dublin written late in the seventeenth century certainly demonstrates significant development beyond Perkins' assumptions. But Mather's is still a much less expansive use of typology than we see in Edward's writings.7 The particulars of this story have been discussed extensively by Lowance.8

The significant point is that, in the two centuries that intervened between the first-generation reformers and Edwards' mature career, the rejection of the multiple senses of Scripture—that is, the general rejection of allegory as a tool for interpretation—remained secure as a "Protestant principle." But its place was taken, in part at least, by typology, a construction of figuralism that yoked Old Testament events and individuals to Christ as their antitype. In this way the unity of the Bible continued unchallenged, for the promise of the type was fulfilled by the great antitype, Christ. Most notable, however, was a marked expansion of the tradition of typological interpretation that occurred over time. This development enabled later Protestants, especially English Puritans and their New England descendants, to find sufficient latitude to work imaginatively with biblical materials.

As liberating as typology was in principle for confirmed literalists, it also set a problem of how to work with the prophecies of the Old Testament that manifestly were not fulfilled in the coming of Christ. Those in the New Testament that presupposed his first coming, but pointed on the end of the world, also posed difficulties. This apocalyptic literature seems to predict events and make promises that could relate only to the end of the world. Numerous others before Edwards had taken these passages as predictive. He went a step further than many and was prepared to extend typology to utilize New Testament passages and events in the life of the Christian church as means to interpret more fully the future that the apocalyptic passages ostensibly disclosed.

Even beyond this, Edwards was prepared to find types in the natural world. His antitype, in nature as well as in apocalyptic literature, was Christ. This meant that his understanding of nature was finally

-- 048 --

determined by a Christocentric construction of the world. Without alienating him from Puritan scripturalism, it made his thought hospitable to a naturalism akin to Emerson's. Edwards did not experience these different sources of religious authority as in tension with each other—and we should not project upon him our modern sense that the worlds of culture and nature are dichotomous. But the combining of these different sources, literary and natural, made of his "history" of the Work of Redemption a remarkable combination of themes ordinarily thought by moderns to be disjunctive.

In general, as we have seen, Edwards made use of three sources for construction of his "history" of the Work of Redemption.9 One source was "Scripture history," by which he meant the Old Testament accounts detailing the creation of the world, the fall of mankind, the call of Abraham, the special relationship God developed with Israel—in sum, the "historical" account of Israel's life. In the same fashion, the New Testament canon offered accounts of the coming of Christ and the early years of the church.

The canonical scriptures also provided a second source, namely, the prophetic literature. Some of the prophetic material, preeminently that of the Old Testament, pointed to (and was fulfilled in) subsequent episodes in the life of ancient Israel, for example, in the captivity in Babylon. Much more of it seemed to point to (and be fulfilled in) the incarnation—or even the Christian church, especially in its early years, as recorded within the scriptural canon. Finally, some prophecies pointed to the course of redemption in Christian history. In Edwards' view, such prophecy was a means to identify the final significance of events not only within but also outside the life of the church—as in the triumph of Antichrist in the ecclesiastical tyranny exercised by the bishop of Rome as well as in the victory secured by Satan in the rise of Islam. Of course, insofar as Edwards believed that the last times remained in his future, the prophetic material from Daniel and Revelation was enormously significant in discerning the "signs of the times."

A third source for Edwards' "history" was in the secular literature that augmented scriptural accounts. Examples are the various narratives about the period between Israel's return from Babylon and the coming of the messiah and about the development of Roman dominion in the church. Edwards believed that this material was very

-- 049 --

important—indeed central—to fleshing out a "history" of God's Work of Redemption. Accordingly he emphasized the spread of philosophy in antiquity (which was preparatory to the coming of Christ) and attached great significance to the political stability of the Roman empire (a pale shadow or image of the truly stable Christian kingdom that was coming into the world). Of course, noncanonical accounts also provided evidence about the workings of movements counter to the course of redemption.

To identify the kinds of materials with which Edwards worked, however is only to recognize that they required interpretation in order to achieve that "history" of the Work of Redemption he aspired to frame. At this point the principle that was basic to his reading of Scripture became central to his literary endeavor and acted as the basis of its dynamism. As we have seen, he accepted and wholeheartedly embraced—indeed developed significantly—the typological approach to Scripture which came to him from Puritan-Protestant figural sources. The pattern of type and antitype provided the basic structure for the initial period in his "history," the preparation for the redemption that is achieved in the second period through purchase by Christ. This is easily recognized, and Edwards' was a conventional use of the approach. A conservative practitioner would have stopped using typology at this point, because it was essential to the message that Christ fulfill all the Old Testament types—if not in the earthly life, then in the resurrection and in his anticipated coming in glory. At one level Edwards operated with these assumptions. But he also moved beyond and transformed them, adapting a figural, even typological, framework for understanding the third period, that of the application of redemption. For him Old Testament types were fulfilled not only in Christ but also in those who were believers. The life of the redeemed was for him typified in the Old Testament, and he applied the types of the Old Testament much more generally than conservative exegesis allowed.

Furthermore, Edwards detached the typological framework from its exclusive linkage to scriptural sources. He explicitly located in the events of history (not only in canonical scriptural accounts) patterns constraining both the redeemed and the rejected of God. In other words, his typology broadened to the point that paradigmatic events outside Scripture had a figural relationship to the rest of history. Again, this moved him decisively beyond a conservative typological hermeneutic.

-- 050 --

Finally, Edwards stepped altogether outside history to find patterns of redemption in nature as well. Of course, he believed that these patterns were most fully disclosed in scriptural revelation, but he seems to have given them status as types even apart from that. He believed that God's redeeming relationship to humanity was imaged or shadowed in nature in rich detail, and that it presented the basic truth of human dependence upon divine glory. This step clearly marks a point of connection with Emerson. For Edwards, nature and history were not alternative sources of knowledge about the Godhead, but each one interpenetrated the other. At his hands, scriptural figures became fused with and elaborated by the images and shadows of nature, and the boundary between them ceased to exist. What remained was an interdependence through which nature and Scripture joined to reveal divine glory. It is not too much to claim that in his Redemption Discourse he so transformed conventional typological assumptions that the discourse became as much a celebration of the God of nature as a hymn to the Lord of history.

The Literary Structure Of The Redemption Discourse

Two aspects of the Redemption Discourse's narrative base immediately stand out, those that unify and those that differentiate. Unitive aspects are given in the definition of the project. Edwards' formal topic was God's Work of Redemption, that is to say, the achievement of the redemption of the world as the greatest of God's works. Unity derived directly from the divine intention that was thought to undergird that work and make it the goal of creation—a goal possibly required by the logic of the trinitarian Godhead. This unifying factor was deeply woven into Edwards' "history" through the central role of Christ. (He saw the Spirit active throughout the whole as well.) Further, the boundaries given to the subject—the fall of man and the last judgment—rendered it coextensive with human consciousness of historical existence.

Edwards' identification of master models or patterns of replication throughout the series also provided unity. These include such things as the pattern of a river, constituting with its tributaries an integrated system; the pattern of the great tree, extending from deep roots to remote branches; the pattern formed by wheels within wheels, identified with Ezekiel's vision as a biblical source but suggesting as well clocks and possibly the gears of elementary machines; the construction of buildings, from objective through design to execution; and

-- 051 --

the pattern of raising an army and conducting war. Edwards advanced these great models or dynamic systemic unities taken from the world of nature and human communities as analogues for the whole of the divine Work of Redemption, and also as a means to identify parts within it.

Edwards' notebook of essay-entries known as "Images or Shadows of Divine Things" provides important background to his use of these master models for comprehending God's relation to the world.1 A series of entries which have been dated to late 1738 and early 1739 (when he was composing the Redemption Discourse) indicate that he was systematically thinking through these analogues.2 No. 77 concerns the "wonderfull analogy between what is seen in RIVERS.… [and] how all things tend to one, even to God, the boundless ocean." No. 78 observes, "We see the reverse in TREES.… all comes from one common stock and is distributed into innumerable branches." No. 81 develops the analogy between a great empire and God's conduct of history: "The Roman triumph was a remarkable type of Christ's ascension." No. 89 likens "The providence of God [to] … a wheel, or a machine composed of wheels." That these entries coincide with use of these analogues in Edwards' account of the Work of Redemption not only indicates the coherence of his intellectual life but also suggests how his theological and literary reflections directly informed composition of the Redemption Discourse.

No less does Edwards emphasize the differentiation of the narrative base. That base has a remarkably rich and variegated texture. The "history" of the Work of Redemption necessarily falls into the three periods already discussed. The first is one of preparation and extends from the fall of man to the coming of Christ. The second, so much the shortest in a human framework but much the most fundamental in theological terms, is that of the incarnation of Christ, during which the purchase of redemption was secured. The third extends from Christ's resurrection to the end of the world, and during it the Work of Redemption is finally and fully accomplished. While Christ and the Holy Spirit are both central in all three periods, their relation to each is different. The differentiation of the Work of

-- 052 --

Redemption carries down into the special characteristics of each period.

The first period is defined by giving a Christian rendering to the scriptural account of Israel's history. Edwards' six stages in this history provided a narrative that progressed toward the coming of Christ. Each stage was discrete, but each also incorporated what had gone before and focused it anew in a clearer delineation of the Work of Redemption. The theological achievements of each stage set it off and suggested interconnections between stages.

From the fall of humanity to the flood, a beginning was made on the whole work, and the end or purpose was also revealed. Christ began his role as mediator. The gospel was disclosed. Central salvific ceremonies began (especially sacrifice as a mode of worship). God began to save souls. Mankind received the Spirit repeatedly. The first saint (Enoch) was chosen; he prophesied of the coming of Christ and was at last translated to heaven. Finally, apostasy narrowed to one family the possibility for the continued existence of humanity. In Edwards' view this first dispensation disclosed the meaning of the whole. The five subsequent stages confirmed and amplified this fundamental pattern.

From the flood to the calling of Abraham there was a fuller disclosure of the redemptive process. The theme of destruction of the enemies of God's church mingled with that of preservation of the family from which the Redeemer would come. In Noah the covenant of grace between God and man was renewed, while in the destruction of the Tower of Babel and the dispersal of the people God's interest in true religion and hostility toward false were manifested.

The third dispensation, from the call of Abraham to the youth of Moses, introduced new redemptive themes and included moves to separate the forebears of Christ from the rest of humanity. So, too, the renewal of the covenant with Isaac and Jacob, and also the preservation of the Redeemer's family in Joseph and through the Egyptian bondage, testified to the focus of the whole redemptive process in Israel. The destruction of Sodom and Gomorrah further signaled God's general wrath. Edwards argues that in important respects this dispensation marked the beginnings of God's church, which must be in but not of the world.

With the fourth dispensation, the invisible church was rendered visible through Israel. In the era between Moses and David, God decisively selected Israel and rejected all other peoples. That nation

-- 053 --

was blessed with law and the written word of God. It was preserved through its wilderness wanderings and freshened (revived) through the Spirit. Finally it was installed in its promised land, preserved against its enemies, and blessed with a succession of prophets.

In the dispensation which ran from David to the Babylonian captivity, Israel's life was fully elaborated as that of a visible kingdom of God, completing the demonstration of this basic pattern for the securing of human redemption. The dialectical counterpart was Israel's defeat and captivity—an outcome which made it clear that this means of redemption was inadequate. The selection of kingly rulers (David and Solomon), the erection of a holy city (Jerusalem), and the augmenting of Scripture (the historical books) all had made it evident that a great work would be accomplished through this holy priest-people. But Israel's decline and defeat showed that, however complete the pattern, redemption had not yet come. The dispensation ended with clear and full prophecies of Christ by Isaiah and the other writing prophets.

In the final dispensation, from the captivity to the coming of Christ, it was evident that Israel remained important to the redemptive work, for example, through preservation of the family through which the Redeemer was to come, through the receiving of prophecies (especially Daniel), and through infusions of the Spirit (Ezra). This dispensation was marked as well, however, by the preparation of the Gentile world for the Redeemer's arrival. The destruction of the Persian empire and the emergence of the Greek led to a universal language by means of which Israel's treasure could be offered to the world. The florescence of learning and philosophy paved the way for the dissemination of the message of redemption. And the peaceful rule of Rome quieted the turmoil of the world so that the Redeemer might come.

Edwards set out the grand design of the first period of his "historical" account in these terms. We will return to it to note how he uses literary strategies to increase the significance of this period. But the organization of each of the remaining two periods of the "history" differed from that of the first.

The middle period, while much the briefest and in some respects least rich phenomenally, is the central point, the pivot on which all turned. The temporal compass of the second period is the life of Christ, from his birth to his death. In treating this period, Edwards took it as a whole and did not use temporal distinctions to control his

-- 054 --

account. For example, he might have explored the episodes of the private life which preceded the events of the public ministry. Instead, he framed the account in two sections. The first considered the incarnation, and particularly its theological necessity. Edwards essentially argued that the course of redemption traced in the first period required this Christ as its outcome. Christ as God-man was the natural completion of the preparation for redemption in Israel's history.

The second section of Edwards' treatment of the middle period concerned how it was that Christ purchased redemption. What transactions accomplished this outcome? Edwards argued with relative brevity that through suffering and humiliation Christ satisfied divine justice, while through obedience and righteousness he merited happiness. So redemption rested upon both fulfilling justice and earning happiness. These two aspects are linked, indeed fused, in the discussion. Redemption was not to be understood as simply a return to the status quo ante, the conditions obtaining before the fall. Rather, Christ's purchase entailed a new creation, a new life in and with God. The work of Christ was seen as latent in the preceding period—that of the Old Testament—and patent for the following one—the life of the redeemed community in the Christian era.

As important as this central period was—it is the nexus of the whole—Edwards treated it in three sermons only, and he applied it in a fourth. What deserves attention is his high and conventional Christology. In this he stood securely in the Calvinist tradition, and his Christocentric position is evident. He avoided the minutiae of federal theology, although he did explicitly identify covenantal aspects of the Old Testament materials. Finally, he did not attempt to exploit the human figure, Jesus, in subjective terms. While Christ manifested certain virtues as a part of his obedience, these were not significant as a pattern for faithful believers. Rather they were the means of securing the Work of Redemption. Edwards' second period was thus entirely dominated by his theological construction of the work of Christ in the purchase of redemption.

From Edwards' perspective, redemption was assured by the close of the second period. The third period runs from the resurrection to the end of the world. In it he returned to a framework that is broadly based on events and organized as a narrative. But his account of the accomplishing of redemption was not simply a counterpart or mirror image of the preparation for it so thoroughly traced during the first period. While it does have a strong sequential cast, his exposition

-- 055 --

of this period made use of certain other patterns as well. First, the resurrection of Christ established that the purchase was done, and its completion or accomplishment (centering in the church) would necessarily follow. For Edwards this was best understood through an analogy: as Christ, the God-man, came through and into Israel, the resurrected Christ would come to and for the church. Second, this construction served to shift the narrative to focus on the church as Christ's kingdom. Edwards brings into play a twofold distribution of the church's life. With the ascension of Christ the church would continue under grace until his return: with the appearance of Christ for the last judgment, the church would enter its life in glory.

This twofold distribution in turn yields to a fourfold set of events, the first three a triad of suffering for the church under grace, the last a state of peace coincident with the church in glory's preparing to take leave of and transcend the earth. The four events have a complex relationship beyond their order in time. Each of the first three foreshadows the fourth. Each improves on any that precede it. Divine providence operates so as to connect them with each other. Finally, together they form one great comprehensive event, the completion of redemption.

The first event, the destruction of Jerusalem, linked the establishment of the church to the end of the Old Testament dispensation given to a priest-people, a nation holy unto God. This was the comprehensive thrust of redemption through the gospel. The second event was the end of the heathen Roman empire, which opposed the gospel with superior knowledge and power. Edwards was so bold as to liken the revolution under Constantine to the coming of Christ. The third event will be the fall of Antichrist and the end of Satan's earthly kingdom. This event brings history to its climax and concludes Edwards' present time. He analyzes that event under four heads. First, Satan both corrupted the church with heresies and had some success in restoring paganism. Second, Antichrist rose within the church in the West in the growth of the papacy, paralleled in the East by the growth of Islam. The true church had a marginal existence under these circumstances until, third, the Reformation reasserted the claims of the gospel. Edwards viewed the intervening two hundred years, to his own time, as a mixture of gospel successes and failures. The fourth division extends from his present time to the fall of Antichrist. This period will climax with the coming of the kingdom of heaven on earth, a state of peace and prosperity for the redeemed

-- 056 --

community which will last a thousand years, then be abruptly terminated by a final great apostasy before judgment day.

The fourth event will be a summation of all these foregoing events as the church comes into glory. Christ will appear with angels, the dead will be raised, and saints will be spiritually transformed as, with Christ, the church is delivered into eternal blessedness. Concurrently the wicked will suffer their fates. Thus God's Work of Redemption will be completed, and its "history" will end.

As strong and compelling as this narrative base turns out to be in its unitive and differential aspects, it does not exhaust the text. The literary strategies already identified are extraordinarily significant in giving texture and a complex content to the whole, and they transform what might be taken simply as a predominantly linear narrative into an analysis that comprehends all of time because it makes the temporal process a function of a far grander whole. In crucial respects eternity actually is brought within time (at points during the "history" and at its completion), and time is endowed with significance by being taken up into eternity. Above all, the Work of Redemption as the objective or divine side of the process invests the subjective or human side with cosmic significance.

Edwards appropriated many different literary devices to effect interconnections within and between the fundamental segments in the narrative. The frequent implicit and explicit use of these devices gives the text great richness. The significance of the analysis of literary strategies is only in part that it is required for a full understanding of the text. Appreciation of these strategies is also necessary to reach an understanding of how, and why, Edwards' "history" proved so influential. Its special achievement was that it set up exceptionally long-lived and significant resonances within the popular culture.

The broadest literary strategy knitting together the periodized segments of the "history" is emphasis upon resemblances between various episodes in the Work of Redemption. In this way Edwards argued for the coherence of the divine administration of the world. At a very general level this strategy includes emphasizing the linkage between the simultaneous growth or expansion of Satan's power and the purification of Christ's church and kingdom. This pattern is implicit in the aftermath of the fall and appears again and again right up to the last apostasy at the end of the millennium. A comparable pattern, repeatedly identified by Edwards, is that of revival, which occurs in the periods when Edwards charts influxes of the Spirit.

-- 057 --

Like the wave action of an advancing tide, Spirit infusions recede and spiritual gains give way as consolidation occurs before a new advance takes place. Edwards makes even a segmented, differentiated process express one divine administration.

As already mentioned, Edwards repeatedly uses the notion of shadows and images as a more specific literary device to connect events in the various periods of his study. An example or two will make this point. He holds that the Jewish church was at its highest external glory under Solomon. As such it "shadowed forth" the kingdom of Christ. This concept serves to connect the pre- and post-incarnational periods and to suggest how the latter, like the former, would be brought into being out of the materials of the Christian dispensation. In a comparable argument, he proposes that the earthly achievement of peace under the rule of the Roman empire is the (pale) image of the harmonious world of the millennium. Through use of such analogies he suggests the coherent impulse lying behind the whole of the drama of redemption and binding together otherwise unrelated actions. His preoccupation with shadows and images is attested by the separate notebook he kept, identified as an independent collection only forty years ago.3 It is clear from this notebook that he thought the natural world, no less than historical events, capable of construction in this way. Turned in a philosophical direction, of course, this meant that he thought the Godhead was making use of the created world to communicate with the human subjects of redemption. At its limit this insight became the epistemological anchor for understanding God's work of redemption.

Edwards identifies another degree of interconnectedness within the Scriptures by typological exegesis. Recent literary criticism has shown that this strategy developed, especially within the English Puritan tradition, as a means of elaborating upon the literal sense of Scripture which remained when the Protestant reformers abandoned the theory that there were many levels of meaning within the biblical text.4 The conventional scheme posited, as we have seen, additional levels or frameworks of interpretation—allegorical, tropological, and anagogical. This fourfold pattern or variations of it, which sometimes established numerous other levels as well, were taken by the Protestants as evidence for the corruption of the Christian tradition by the Roman-dominated

-- 058 --

church. But in directing attention back to the literal or historical sense of Scripture, of course, the Protestants faced the challenge of bringing order to the richness and confusion of the writing of ancient Israel that they took to be sacred.

By insisting that the Old Testament foreshadowed Christ, Puritans took the decisive step of positing that Christ could be found in these materials. And through return to a mode of interpretation as old as Christianity itself, typology, the Protestant tradition (especially in England and Scotland) embarked in its own way on the projection of meaning upon the Scriptures. In particular, typology posited individuals and events in the Old Testament as types of Christ—distinct, if latent, images—that were developed or revealed by Christ himself. This device served to uncover meaning in the Old Testament and to elaborate and enrich the New. In rigorous versions of typology, types referred only to Christ. Looser constructions permitted reference to the church or redemption-related incidents. Edwards clearly accepted this general framework, and he devoted great energies to arranging typological readings of the Scriptures.

At the looser end of the typological spectrum, Edwards interpreted the flood broadly, as a type of Christian baptism. While baptism is rendered a ceremony through the antitype of Christ's submission to John the Baptist, Edwards' reference was clearly to its location in the church as a continuing institution. He held that God's action at Mount Sinai was a type of the rule of law and, as such, was taken up in the law-gospel polemic derived from Paul. In this its typological function was negative—it showed a rejected means of salvation. Again, the exodus was offered as a type of the church, as was Jerusalem. In these instances, of course, the identification of the church with Christ is very strong, but still, in a technical sense, the antitype was not Christ but his corporate extension, the church. Joshua and the conquest of Canaan were held to be types of redemption. This is a still broader antitype. Finally, Edwards proposed that the skins in which Adam and Eve clothed themselves in the Garden of Eden to hide their nakedness were types of the righteousness of Christ in which Christians clothe themselves.

In these and numerous other cases, Edwards identified Old Testament incidents and events as types of redemption through Christ. Reference to Christ is not absent—its presence is critical to establishing the antitype—but the antitype points more specifically to aspects of the church, or the life of Christians, than to Christ and his work

-- 059 --

itself. In many ways these loosely constructed types were only very little more specific than the images or shadows that have already been discussed. They do appear sufficiently distinctive as a category, however, to merit special attention. More than the shadows or the images, their reference to an aspect of the redemptive process is tied to the role of Christ as Redeemer.

Strict typology is also used in the Redemption Discourse. The references to sacrifice in Genesis are clear types of Christ, who completed his task precisely through himself becoming the final sacrifice. This antitype is also implicit in Noah's sacrifice and the paschal lamb. Again, the temple Israel built in Jerusalem was explicitly a type of Christ's human nature, but it also served, in a broader construction, as a type of the church and of heaven. Similarly, deliverers of ancient Israel in pre-Davidic times were explicit types, for Christ as antitype was the great deliverer of Israel.

Edwards accepted certain conventional typological formulations. He proposes at one point that there are essentially three kinds of types of Christ. One kind, the institutional, has sacrifices as the great types and Christ with his church ordinances as the clear antitype. A second kind is providential: it consists of sequences of events that find their true meaning in Christ. Here the great type was the redemption of Israel out of Egypt, which finds its antitype in Christ's redeeming his church. The third kind is personal. Here David represented the fullest or most exclusive type of Christ; David as the king of Israel who brought integrity, peace, and prosperity to Israel. So Christ as antitype fulfills the Davidic type—as well as those personal types represented by Joshua, Moses, and Solomon among others.

Through strict typological exegesis even more than through the looser version, Edwards builds up interconnections between the Old Testament and the several periods of redemption that go beyond even resemblances, shadows, and images in developing levels of meaning critical to portraying the Work of Redemption. Although the narrative is linear, the layered applications of typology make contiguous, indeed contemporaneous, signals and signposts of the Work of Redemption that otherwise would be discrete.

As we might expect, Edwards uses the type-antitype framework extensively with respect to the first four of the periods into which the Old Testament is divided. From the fall through the establishment of Israel and the enrichment of her life, virtually all events can be construed as pointing directly to Christ. By the same token, the New

-- 060 --

Testament accounts of Christ are implicitly antitypical. Even when not made explicit, the types (shadows, images, and so forth) that have been identified give definition to the whole in outline and in particular. With the sixth Old Testament period, the captivity of Israel in Babylon, more emphasis is given to another literary strategy, the role of prophecy in pointing to Christ—and beyond, to the end of the world. Prophets are types of Christ in foreshadowing the full disclosure of the eternal Word and world. But they also foreshadow Christ and his specific coming. These two functions are closely related: through the prophet (for example, Ezra) the Spirit of God is poured out, an action which stops with Christ's incarnation. The principle of explicit witness through the Spirit punctuates the earlier periods of the Old Testament. In the very first period of the Old Testament the Spirit is poured out on Enos. In Enoch a prophet of Christ is translated in the body to heaven. The principle of Spirit witness or presence becomes increasingly important in the third period (from the resurrection of Christ to the end of the world). There the Spirit is the great agent through whom the Work of Redemption—which Christ has finished—is developed and completed. In his exposition of this period, Edwards stresses how "history" confirms the prophetic schema which is fully given again in the early church, especially in Revelation. With the last stage—from Edwards' present to the end of the world—his account becomes wholly dependent upon the Spirit's utterances. The logic of this position is that prophecy becomes as pronounced in the last great period of the "history" as shadows, images, and types were in the first, but nowhere does he insist that these instruments are confined to certain periods or dispensations.

We have already taken note of the role prophecy plays in the Old Testament dispensation as the manifestation of the Spirit. No less do types occasionally appear after Christ, but now they are used more loosely. Strictly, a type can point only toward an antitype in whose coming it is dissolved or fulfilled. But, to cite one example, Edwards identifies the revolution of the church in Constantine's era as a type of the day of judgment. Occasional instances make it clear that he uses typology as much as other devices throughout all three periods of his "history."

The literary strategy that completes this series ties together the whole "history." Edwards very occasionally declares that, beyond any outpouring of the Spirit, Christ was actually present among his people in manifestations to Israel. No less does he assume that Christ is

-- 061 --

present in his church among the faithful in the Christian era. In this assumption Edwards curves the straight line of his scheme around itself, so that Christ becomes present throughout "history." The basic images (the flowing of a river, the growing of a tree, the interconnection of wheels, the building of a structure, the conduct of a military campaign) express this same point in other ways. Christ is redemption. Redemption is Christ.

The Redemption Discourse must be recognized as, at least implicitly a much more thoroughly developed literary document than has normally been thought. It may be more usefully compared with classical literary constructs than with traditional historical accounts like Cotton Mather's Magnalia on the one hand or Bancroft's History of the United States on the other. In this sense Herman Melville's Moby-Dick may stand in as close a kinship to it as any other American text. The literary characteristics of the Redemption Discourse also help to explain the near-paradox one can see in conventional assessments. The enormous range and reach of the Discourse and its impact upon popular culture have been clear, but the materials of the book itself have appeared to be conventional and, philosophically speaking, scarcely worthy of a Jonathan Edwards. Viewing the Redemption Discourse as a literary construct, however, makes sense of the discrepant estimates of its importance.

The Projected Revision Of The Redemption Discourse

The literary characteristics of the Redemption Discourse clearly suggest that we can see it as a proto-treatise. Its form indicates that Edwards intended from the outset so to develop it. Recognizing its strengths as a literary document, and in particular the strategies its author deployed in its design and composition, compels us to ask how he would have revised and recast the project had he lived. There is no doubt about its centrality to his sense of his work as an author. Wilson Kimnach, who has studied Edwards' literary techniques intensively, believes he viewed it as the culmination of his work.5 Furthermore, he offers the intriguing suggestion that Edwards had actually sorted through his general collection of sermons with a view to identifying those he would use in this large project. Kimnach's proposal rests on the presence of a symbol he takes to stand for "Redemption" on several hundred of the well over one thousand

-- 062 --

manuscript sermons.6 If correct, this is further evidence that, in his last years, Edwards was marshaling his resources to construct a major treatise on the Work of Redemption.

It does seem evident that this project was for Edwards a central (if not the central) intellectual concern at the time of his decision to accept the presidency of the College of New Jersey. His letter to the trustees makes that clear. Indeed, in the course of that letter he provided the most comprehensive formulation of his plans that we have. It is worth reprinting this paragraph:


I have had on my mind and heart, (which I long ago began, not with any view to publication,) a great work, which I call a History of the Work of Redemption, a body of divinity in an entire new method, being thrown into the form of a history; considering the affair of Christian Theology, as the whole of it, in each part, stands in reference to the great work of redemption by Jesus Christ; which I suppose to be, of all others, the grand design of God, and the summum and ultimum of all the divine operations and decrees; particularly considering all parts of the grand scheme, in their historical order.—The order of their existence, or their being brought forth to view, in the course of divine dispensations, or the wonderful series of successive acts and events; beginning from eternity, and descending from thence to the great work and successive dispensations of the infinitely wise God, in time, considering the chief events coming to pass in the church of God, and revolutions in the world of mankind, affecting the state of the church and the affair of redemption, which we have an account of in history or prophecy; till at last, we come to the general resurrection, last judgment, and consummation of all things; when it shall be said, It is done. I am Alpha and Omega, the Beginning and the End.—Concluding my work, with the consideration of that perfect state of things, which shall be finally settled, to last for eternity.—This history will be carried on with regard to all three worlds, heaven, earth and hell; considering the connected, successive events and alterations in each, so far as the scriptures give any light; introducing all parts of divinity in that order which is most scriptural and most natural; a method which appears to me the most beautiful and entertaining, wherein every divine doctrine will appear to the greatest advantage, in the brightest light,

-- 063 --

in the most striking manner, showing the admirable contexture and harmony of the whole.7

In addition to this statement we have the three working notebooks that Edwards used in apparent preparation for revision. All of them were elementary efforts and suggest that the project was still fluid in his mind as the Stockbridge period came to a close. A thorough analysis of these notebooks, including attention to the dating of the most important, is published as Appendix B. The shortest (Notebook B) represents little more than such random jottings as an author might keep as he began to prepare for a serious and sustained attack on a topic.8 The most finished (Notebook C) is basically composed of quotations or references Edwards had culled in the course of his reading; these are materials that he apparently wished to have available, especially for his treatment of the papacy.9 This notebook discloses very little, if anything, about his reflections on the whole project, except that it suggests he wanted to flesh out his discussion of the Christian era. In particular, he seems to have intended a more comprehensive discussion of the corruption that, he believed, had overwhelmed the Christian church after the Constantinian settlement.

The remaining notebook (Notebook A) is of considerable significance for the insights it gives into Edwards' plans about the project in the last years of the Stockbridge period.1 This is much the roughest of the three notebooks, and perhaps it is best described as a record of his reflections upon rereading his thirty lecture-sermons with a view toward revision. In its pages he recorded ideas as they came to him and marshaled scriptural citations that seemed relevant. He also tried out and recorded his thoughts about the structure and organization of the revised project, frequently under a heading he often used in this type of writing, "method." Through these and related entries it is possible to gauge his approach to developing the Work of Redemption project.

The first substantial entry in Notebook A represents Edwards' initial step toward an outline for the whole.2 He clearly posited a first

-- 064 --

-- 065 --

-- 066 --

part composed of as many as five chapters. Each chapter would concern some general condition or significant aspect of the whole. Chapter 1 would concern God as "the great author and end of the Work of Redemption." Chapter 2 would discuss the "eternal contrivance and design of the Work of Redemption." In chapter 3 he would discuss "the general nature of the Work of Redemption" as "the greatest of God's works." Chapter 4 would address the question of "preparation for the Work of Redemption," for example, the "creation of angels, their trial, fall of some of them, the fall of man, etc." He projected that the fifth and concluding chapter of the introductory section would treat "the beginning of the Work of Redemption," but thought that this might better belong to the "second part." He thought that the second part should be concerned with "the preparation for … redemption and visible accomplishment of the work." This would take many chapters. Edwards broke off the draft outline with the comment that the first part might be titled "the original and foundation of the Work of Redemption," and the second "the performance of the Work of Redemption."

In a series of later entries, Edwards continued the outline, with additional observations.3 He reiterated the basic triadic structure of periods that was fundamental to the lecture-sermons in 1739. The life, death, and resurrection of Christ remained pivotal. He also reflected on the cumulative nature of the whole—"all things in the foregoing periods [are] preparation for the next." Further, he identified five scripturally warranted models of and for the Work of Redemption; these are the master models he explored in "Images or Shadows of Divine Things." One was "the revolutions of the wheel of a chariot"—the basic image was of machinery, perhaps especially the clock, although its scriptural derivation was from Ezekiel's vision of the wheels. A second was "the erecting of a great building," a favorite image for Edwards, in which he emphasized the organization of the whole from the architect's plans to the assembling of materials and workers and on to the completion of the building. (This undoubtedly reflected his own experience with the construction of the new meetinghouse in Northampton, which antedated the delivery of the sermon series.) The third model was "the growing of a tree," while the fourth was complementary, "the flowing of the water in a great river." In this model he laid great emphasis upon the many

-- 067 --

tributaries that come together to form a river as it makes its way into great ocean, an image doubtless stimulated by his life along and in relationship to the Connecticut River. Finally, his fifth model was the "carrying on a war," with emphasis upon the complexity of its organization and the importance of timing the different elements. Of course, in historical terms, the Roman empire was the great exemplum of this model.

Still reflecting on the second part of this projected organization, Edwards posited a chapter "with some things in general concerning the manner which the work or this fulfillment to it were carried on from the beginning of the world to the end. Here to show how carried on earth, and how in the invisible world, how among angels and man, how in heaven and hell." He also reflected on the proportion of the population that would be subject to God's commands in the glorious times of the millennium; it would represent "a hundred or perhaps a thousand to one of all other times put together." These conjectures are amplified and codified by Joseph Bellamy in his sermon on the millennium,4 as well as by other New England authors. Which author is dependent on the other is not at all clear; perhaps it is futile to attempt to chart certain lines of dependence in the case of writers with strong mutual interests and the opportunity for frequent interaction like Edwards and Bellamy.

Through additional random entries, it is clear that in many respects these notes on method are more the clarification, specification, and proposed amplification of the argument of the Redemption Discourse as already framed than significant departures from it—or proposed revisions that would transform it. That Edwards intended to make explicit much that was already implicit is clear, as is his commitment to make extensive additions to the corpus of the document. But his plans in the later Stockbridge years certainly did not include the development of a radically different project, as has sometimes been alleged by those commenting on his interest in history. Such a conventional estimate seems to have rested on a systematic undervaluation of the Redemption Discourse's literary complexity. The letter to the trustees, the last statement we have from him about it, can also be read as analytical reflection about a project that had been implicit in the Redemption Discourse twenty years earlier.

-- 068 --

This judgment is confirmed by secondary sections in his notebook which return to the question of method or address the organization of specific sections. In one section Edwards suggests that he should argue for God's preoccupation with the work of creation under six particulars from "a continuing appointing it for eternity" to "the consummation of it." At this point he referred to a "miscellanies entry" about the "various steps and degrees" of the new creation.5 In developing this theme, he introduced a sevenfold periodization for, or set of steps in, the new creation that was potentially a new organization for the third period (from Christ's resurrection to the end of the world). This is certainly a development, if relatively minor, of the summary formulation given in the 1739 sermon materials. The seven periods or steps are: "1. the work begun at Pentecost. 2. destruction of Jerusalem. 3. destruction of heathenish empire in Constantine's time. 4. the reformation. 5. the fall of Antichrist. 6. the overthrow of Judaism, Mahometanism and heathenism through the world. 7. the consummation of the end of the world."6 While no further direct development of the scheme occurs in the notebook, Edwards was apparently thinking of replicating in the new creation the pattern he identified so fully in the old. These seven periods would mirror the creation plus six periods which he set out as the proper divisions of the scheme, or dispensations, from the fall of man to the coming of Christ. A slightly later entry suggests he considered developing the theme of balancing a new against the older covenants.

Reflections of this kind about the structure of the whole work are buried within notes which more characteristically concern scriptural texts to be used to advantage, and readings and authors that merit notice. Nonscriptural references in particular seem random—"see Owen on Hebrews 11:5, page 24, place marked in the margin."7 They do not indicate that Edwards was engaged in a sustained reading program on the redemption theme or offer evidence that he had identified a particular foil, like John Taylor in his work on Original Sin.8

As Edwards continued to make entries, he gave attention to subordinate points or technical details. Some concerned theological matters. As one example, he commented on the behavior of the angels

-- 069 --

at the birth of Christ. Others concerned assumptions to be made, for example, about the pre-Mosaic era in Egyptian history. In these sections there is evidence that he had consulted a number of conventional historical accounts like "Dr. Winder … his History of Knowledge."9 In a series of entries he recorded useful information he gleaned from Winder, but he also detailed points of disagreement and how he would appropriate such work to his own purposes.1

These entries are followed by notes about the development of particular sections, which in turn yield to more general reflections on method—the burden of which is the importance of making explicit, or opening to view, the procedures he will follow in each of the three great sections of his work.2 These and related comments do not amount, however, to a proposed radical recasting of the whole. They simply indicate Edwards' awareness of the organization or literary structure already implicit in the 1739 Redemption Discourse. One entry explicitly links together this project and the essay "The End for which God Created the World": "First part concerning the cause of the Work of Redemption and of the Trinity that the happiness of God chiefly consists in love to himself. See my rough draft of discourse on the Last End of the Creation after part the papers sketched in at the left hand page second of those papers."3 The significance of this entry is at least twofold. First, it places in perspective the limited focus of the earlier essay. Moreover, it establishes the point that Edwards did not view that essay as preempting his larger project on the Work of Redemption—for example, by describing its themes philosophically. The earlier essay was to be seen instead as a kind of prolegomenon, exploring the reasons for the grand summary inquiry he still intended to complete.

An additional perspective on Edwards' work with the Redemption project is given by entries reflecting on the title. We know from his correspondence that the word history remained useful in his thinking about the project. But it is also evident that he considered other terms and titles that would more appropriately capture his intended meaning. One was "the scheme and progress of the Work of Redemption."4

-- 070 --

Two other versions appear toward the end of the notebook: one is "A Scheme of a Treatise concerning Redemption, with practical improvements"; the other is "The Nature and History of the Work of Redemption."5 He varied this latter title subsequently by referring to the work as "The Treatise of the Nature of Redemption."6

This last evidence leads to two conclusions. The first is that Edwards had not exactly settled or determined what title he would give to the project. He thought of such possibilities as "Work of Redemption" and "Nature of Redemption," among others. The second conclusion concerns the term history. While it is clear that he intended the treatise to be analytical, he did not identify his approach exclusively or even secondarily as historical, or in terms that establish that he was committed to historical writing as preeminent; two of the formulations he seriously considered as alternatives to history were scheme and nature. Beyond doubt, the Redemption project remained primarily a theological program in his mind. He most certainly did not conceive of it as an exercise in the writing of history in a more modern sense. At issue here is not whether the development of universal historiography in modern European cultures finally derived from Christian cultural assumptions about the world, but how far this particular treatise by Jonathan Edwards should be interpreted as if he were consciously, or even subconsciously, working toward such an ideal. The evidence seems clear that he had not moved as far along the road toward modernity in this respect as some interpreters have claimed.7

Apparently Edwards had reflected on the project to the point of imagining its format. "Let there be large marginal notes at the bottom of the page, and the reflection and doctrinal observations and disputations be in them."8 He seemed to favor using Deuteronomy 33:26 in place of Isaiah 51:8 as the "text to be prefixed to the whole treatise" and used to anchor the "doctrine."9 He continued to envision both a "historical part" and a "practical improvement"—that is, he believed he should retain sections for the benefit of his readers comparable to that application of the sermons that was a part of the delivery to his Northampton congregation, especially the summary improvement in

-- 071 --

the last half of the twenty-ninth and the whole of the thirtieth sermon.1 The final entry is a further reflection on method that recapitulates in part, and echoes in full, prior notes under this heading.

Any discussion of what Edwards would have done in developing the Redemption sermon series into a mature treatise must be inconclusive. Our evidence suggests that the Redemption project was much on Edwards' mind in his last years even if, with respect to details, it remained protean. Its overall shape and internal direction seem to have been clear to him, in no small part because they maintained continuity with the 1739 sermon series. Questions about organization, and a myriad of details respecting development, still had to be worked through. Contrary to an assumption that has been rather widespread, however, it is not likely that the revised work would have departed radically from the substance of the sermons. As a treatise it would most probably have been a development of the original Redemption Discourse, bringing forward into the light much of what had been latent in those initial lecture-sermons for the pulpit. Edwards' late reflections on the project provide us with another justification for turning back to the sermons, and finding in the twelve preaching booklets a substantial and far more complex and sophisticated literary structure than has generally been appreciated. The notebooks Edwards made at the end of his life as much suggest the importance of reevaluating the nature of the 1739 text as they witness to the frustration of his plans to recast and develop it further.

A general conclusion is that, if the literary characteristics or strategies discussed above are recognized as already present in the Redemption Discourse, Edwards' plans had not, at the time of his death, developed very far beyond that series of sermons as it stood when completed, certainly not to the degree ordinarily assumed. Recognizing the literary characteristics of the original sermons leads us to place less emphasis upon the presumptive changes and recasting that the passage from the letter to the trustees implies. This is not to deny that A History of the Work of Redemption so perfected would have been significantly developed and enriched, but only to posit that it would have been recognizably derived from the 1739 sermon series, perhaps (as has already been suggested) in something of the way that Religious Affections extended, refined, and amplified the position its author initially worked out in the commencement sermon at Yale in 1740 and

-- 072 --

published in the next year as "The Distinguishing Marks."2 In that case the concerns and consequences he set out remained identifiable, although their statement was refined and his analysis much extended and deepened. This seems to be the kind of development he would have given to the Redemption Discourse.

The Logical Structure Of The Redemption Discourse

The form of Edwards' Redemption Discourse is deeply influenced by its origin as material prepared for preaching. It is divided into thirty preaching units—which Edwards terms sermons—even though it could be argued that technically the thirty taken together stand as one complete sermon. At a deeper level the structure of the whole directly reflects the literary usages of Puritan preaching, with its conventional elements of text, doctrine and reasons, and application. The internal development of each part of this structure follows the principle of the hierarchical ordering of heads relentlessly. This pattern is carried into far more than the usual major divisions of the sermon form. John Erskine used a subordinate level of these heads to organize his 1774 edition of A History of the Work of Redemption, as can be seen from his table of contents.3 Erskine's editorial decision obscured the significance of the sermon elements which are the framework of the series—however much Edwards had adapted their conventional relationships. Furthermore, by elevating to prominence the intermediate and more chronological levels of organization, Erskine to some extent veiled the degree of subordination of heads. One can see this clearly from tables 1 and 2.

In table 1, major heads at the left of the page indicate how the sermon structure orders the whole of the treatise. In this connection note the insertion of improvements or applications so as to punctuate the development of the doctrine and subordinate propositions. In Erskine's reduction of the manuscript with an eye to suppressing divisions between preaching units and subordinating the conventional elements of the sermon, he generally moved to the second and third levels of organization. This effectively deemphasized the place of the opening text and especially the formal priority of the doctrine in its main divisions. Table 2 elevates this second level of structure so

-- 073 --

that the "historical" or sequential stratum of organization stands out. Indeed, taken at this level the dominant pattern is that of the three great periods. Of course the "parts" of the second and third periods are less clearly defined in temporal terms than those of the first, although the third period has a temporal orientation at the third level of subordination—which concerns the church under grace that will be succeeded by the church under glory at the end of history. In any case, the overwhelming impression created by the published version was that of a historical treatment of the subject. So Erskine's suppression of the sermon form obscured Edwards' formal subordination of biblical and postbiblical materials to the edifying logic which derived from his use of that form in his original exposition.

In particular, Erskine's treatment deemphasizes the thoroughness with which Edwards used the hierarchical principle of organization of material, or analysis by dichotomy, within the periods or parts. This is not so significant in the first period, for through the first four subperiods he used only one degree of subordination in discussing his material. In the fifth subperiod (from David to the Babylonian captivity), however, he used two degrees, while in the sixth (from the captivity to the coming of Christ), he used three. In the second and third periods, he used branching analysis even more extensively. In the second period (the incarnation) he reached to four further degrees of subordination to secure his points. Nine degrees of subordination beyond the broadly chronological were required for his analysis of the last period (from the resurrection of Christ to the end of the world).

Of what significance is this to an understanding of the Redemption Discourse? The use of a branching structure, or the pattern of subordination of parts within the logical framework, is an index of the degree to which the Discourse was governed theologically. The point is not simply that Edwards' subject was the encompassing divine action in relation to the world. His controlling interpretation derived from theological premises rather than from empirical observation guided by hypotheses or from an explicitly developmental historical framework. This is the sense in which the Discourse, in spite of the title under which it was published, is profoundly unhistoriographical in any modern sense. In an earlier analogy I have suggested that Edwards was deeply committed to understanding the human saga as the production of a play that God had authored. His interest, moreover, was very much in the play's text as well as in its production. He

-- 074 --

did not hold, with some modern criticism, that the play as produced is independent of the playwright, or that through its production the text takes on a life of its own. He did not have historiographic interests uppermost.

This is a different dimension of the argument that the Redemption Discourse was a profoundly theological project. The whole has been easily misunderstood when read in editions deriving from Erskine's.4 Having allowed secondary and tertiary levels of organization to emerge as controlling, Erskine deemphasized the continued branching of the discussion. Hierarchical subordination as a principle of organization was deeply congruent with specifically Ramist logic and ought to be seen in part as an expression of the dichotomizing tendencies in that mode of analysis. At issue in this discussion of how thoroughly the Redemption Discourse is organized through heads, however, is less the source or implication of the method than the degree of its control over the text. Branching analysis gives form to the whole. To put it most boldly, doctrine controls interpretation at all points. The result is a remarkable blending of rational analysis and biblical reference in which anything remotely resembling modern historiographical interests is hard to find.
