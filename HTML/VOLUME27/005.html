Jonathan Edwards PREDESTINATIONThis section contains a great number of relocated passages, as directed by JE through cue signs and prompts, and made more complicated by JE Jr.'s markings. An attempt has been made to follow JE's intentions, but the final order of some of the passages must remain conjectural.

Mr. Grove, in his discourse on Wisdom the First Spring of Action in the Deity,Henry Grove, Wisdom the First Spring of Action in the Deity (London, 1734), p. 2:
It is not a thing indifferent, whether the end propos'd by God in creating intelligent beings, shall be the display of his sovereign uncontroulable power in their everlasting misery, without the least thing done by them to deserve it; raising their expectations by express and solemn promises, and then disappointing them .. .. The sun in the heavens is not more visible by its own light, than the truth of this maxim, that whoever knowingly and wilfully hurts another, without cause, is guilty of injustice; and the greater the hurt, the greater the injustice. objects thus against the doctrine of REPROBATION, p. 2: "It is not a thing indifferent, whether the end proposed by God in creating intelligent beings, shall be the display of his sovereign, uncontrollable power in their everlasting misery, without the least thing done by them to deserve it; raising their expectations by express and solemn promises, and then disappointing them .. .. The sun in the heavens is not more visible by its own light, than the truth of this maxim, that whoever knowingly and willfully hurts another without cause, is guilty of injustice; and the greater the hurt, the greater the injustice."

That place, Matthew 20:21-23, "Grant that these my two sons may sit, one on thy right hand, and the other on thy left, in thy kingdom.. .. It shall be given to them for whom it is prepared of my Father," affords an invincible argument for particular, personal predestination.

'Tis an evidence that the Apostle, in the 9th of Romans, has not respect only to an election and dereliction of nations or public societies, because one instance that he produces to illustrate and confirm what he says, is the dereliction of a particular person, even Pharaoh, Romans 9:17. So 'tis an instance of God's mercy to a particular person, even Moses, when he says to Moses, "I will have mercy on whom I will have mercy, and will have compassion," etc. Romans 9:15.

The words cited were used by God on occasion of, and with relation to, his mercy to a particular person, even Moses; and the language in that verse, and the next, is suited to particular persons, as v. 16, and v. 18, and vv. 22-23. And the Apostle shows plainly, vv. 27, 29, that 'tis not an election of nations or public societies, but a distinction of some particular persons from others of the same society; as it was a distinction of particular persons in preserving some, when others were destroyed by Nebuchadnezzar's armies, and in returning some from captivity and leaving others. This was a showing mercy to one public society in distinction from another.

So in ch. 10:4-5, where the Apostle plainly continues to speak of the same election, it was not by a national election, or election of any public society, that God distinguished the seven thousand that he had reserved, that had not bowed the knee to the image of Baal 1 Kings 19:18, Romans 11:4.

To consider particularly how God is said to be willing that all men should be saved, etc. How he has the same love, all that goodness, that is requisite in order to this, so that those same qualifications, if imparted only in a small communication and image of 'em in one that is not omniscient, and to whom does not belong the sovereign disposal of all things, it will actually have such an exercise. As in the image of God's goodness in the saints, and when God becomes man, in the man Christ Jesus. WILLINGNESS, INVITATIONS, etc.

GOD'S DECREEING SIN. "It ought to be observed, that as God foreknows the wickedness of which free agents will be guilty, so his decree with respect to their wickedness, is no other than a decree to permit them to act freely; and surely there is not the least violation of their freedom in this. This is in effect God's decreeing that they shall commit this or that wickedness, on supposition of God's foreknowing that they will certainly be guilty of it, if suffered to follow their own inclinations." Greene, Friendly Conferences on Taylor's "Key," pp. 88-89.Pub. 1756; no further information found on this title.

N.B. It appears probable, by Greene's Friendly Conferences, a pamphlet Mr. BurrRev. Aaron Burr. lent me, published in 1756, that Mr. Locke's explanations of the texts in Paul's Epistles, which speak of election and predestination, are now pretty generally embraced by the Arminians, both of the Church of England and dissenters.

"John 6:37, 39, 'All that the Father hath given me shall come to me; .. . And this is the Father's will which sent me, that of all which he hath given me I should lose nothing, but should raise it up again at the last day.' What is this, the being given to Christ to be raised up again, and, as v. 4, to everlasting life, but the election of particular persons to salvation? And since it is the Father's will that, of all that he has given to Christ, he should lose nothing, this election must be so absolute as to insure their salvation." Greene's Friendly Conferences.

To improve that argument, that election is said not to be of WORKS. Romans 9:11, "that the purpose of God according to election might stand, not of works, but of him that calleth"; v. 11, "neither of them having done either good or evil"; and Romans 11:5-6, "Even so at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace. And if by grace, then it is no more of works: otherwise grace is no more grace. But if it be of works, then it is no more grace: otherwise work is no more work"; 2 Timothy 1:9, "who hath saved us, and called us with an holy calling, not according to our works, but according to his own purpose and grace, which was given us in Christ Jesus before the world began."

To observe particularly how invincible a proof of the Calvinist doctrine of election that place is, in Romans 11:5, "Even so, then at this present time also there is a remnant according to the election of grace." Dr. Doddridge observes upon it, that some explain this of having chosen grace, i.e. the gospel.Doddridge, Family Expositor. But that term is very unnatural, and neither suits the phrase, nor the connection with the former clause or with the next verse, in which the Apostle comments on his own words.

Responsio. Tenandum hic Intellectum divinum in se specatum, sive respecta ad Voluntatem, Radicem esse meræ Possibilitatis Rerum; dum deus intuendo semetipsum sive Perfectiones suas, adeoque cognoscendo seipsum, seit quicqui mullam Contradictionem involvit, sive sit Veritas absolute necessaria, sive contingens, sive libera. Sed hactenus Res nondum futurae sunt; sed nonnisi mare possibiles. Voluntas autem dei Radix Futuritionis est; ut propter divinæ Voluntatis Pradeterminationem, certa rerum series, et Rerum possibilium systema aliquod certo extitunum sit; sed tale quole indivino Intellectu, antecedenter ad decretum representatum suit; ut ea in illo libera maneant quætunc cen libera in divino Intellectu representata fuere, et contingentia quæ contingentia fuere. Quando vera divino voluntas Nexum diquem rerum ad actum producendam, adeoque futurum decrevit, divinus Intellectus illam rerum seriam non amplius ceu mere possibilem, sed etiam ceu futurem sibi representat. Concedum autem Adversaris ud saltem negare non poterunt, simplicem rerum ceu mere possibilium indivinio Intellectu Representationem antecedenter ad Decretum, Rebus nullam plane inferre Necessitatem, dum hactenus, dum hactenus, nullum ad divinam voluntatem respectu habito, ne quidem definitum sit, num res illæ ad Actum sint producendæ nec ne. Voluntas Autem tales res illas producere deceruit, quales in divino Intellectu representatæ fuere; ut nulla, respectu rei ipsius, per decretum mutetur Qualitas sive Affectio, nisi quod futura est. Unde nulla in Rebus earumque intrinseca Natura per decretum facta mutatione, condant oportent Adversarii, condem, juxta nostram de decretis doctrinam, intrinsecam omnium rerum menere Naturam, qualis antecendenter ante decretum, sive divinæ voluntatis Actum, in divino Intellectu representata suit. Cum autem sola Intellectuer divini Representatio, nullo ad ejus Voluntatem respectu habito, Rebus sive Rerum Essentiis, nullam inferat Necessitatem; Voluntatis ore actus, sive dicretum nihil plane in intrinseca rerum Natura, sive earum Essentiis muter. Sed eas tales sin at quales antea fuerent; sequitur nec Dei Voluntatem Absolutam Rebus liberis ac Contingentibus, inferre Necessitatem. Post quam autem divina Voluntas decrevit Rerum oliquam Seriem ae Nexam, antea ceu mere possibilem in divino Intellectu representatum, ad Actum producere, Intellectus divinus illud rerum systema, sve seriom illam non amplius ceu mere possibilem, sed ceu futuram sibi representat, eamque præcognoscit ac prævidet; Quæ Procognitio sive Prævisio non magis res illas absolute necessarioes facere potest ac id decretum facere demonstravimus, dum et hæc Prævisia in re decreta nihil plane muter. Unde et hic Adversarii debent fateri, Res ex decreto præcognitas propterea necessarias non fieri, nisi illarum Essentia jam antea feurint necessaria Adeoque deus omnes res tales futuras præcognoscit quales eas producere decrevit, quales sibi divinus Intellectus, jam antecedenter ad decretum ceu mere possibiles sibi representavit. Atqui Representatio illa, nullam Rerum Contingentium ac liberarum Necessitatem absolutam involvit. Ergo, nec earum ex decreto Præcognitio sive Prævisio.

Si dicunt Adversarii, Deo in Literis S. Præscientiam adscribi conditionatam a Creatinarum Actionibus liberis pendentem. Respondemus, Omnine Præscientiam divinam, respectu Creaturaru, sive objective consideratiam conditionatam esse — dum in toto rerum Nexu omnia sunt hypothetica ut si hoc fit; etiam illud fiat; ut tota series, ceu totodam Media, sive Conditions, sibi subordinata sit, ut hoc eveniat propter hoc; Adeoque tota rerum futurarum Præscientia conditionata est; dum Præscientia unius Rei semper presupponat alteram Rem. Nihilominus tamea certo futura sunt omnia, qua deus præicit, licet conditionata maneant. Non ergo Adversarii habent cur nos accusent, nos conditionatem Præscientiam negare.

And is it not "the wisdom of every agent, before he sets about a work of any compass, to fix in his design, as far as he can, all things that any way relate to it. Now all God's works, from the beginning of the creation to the consummation of all things, are one whole, one entire grand scheme, whose ultimate end lies at a vast distance from the beginning, and all the intermediate operations, as so many parts conspire to it in a regular connection. How then can it be consistent with his most perfect wisdom, to leave any of them to an after-thought, when he had forethought sufficient to provide for all alike?

"Once more, God's foreknowledge of all futurities evidently infers his purpose to be some way conversant about all of them." Does he, for instance, certainly foreknow, though in a manner inconceivable by us, all that free agents would do in every situation and circumstance? Whenever therefore he wills to bring them into any state of existence, and into any circumstances, surely he must also will to suffer the foreseen consequences to take place. Thus God foreordains whatsoever comes to pass, by purposing to do himself whatever is necessary or proper for his own power to perform, and to permit all things else.

Let it not be thought that God's simple permission of things, requires not his will to be at all concerned about it: for with his foresight he had the power of prevention. Therefore, whatever he permits, he chooses to permit, rather than to prevent, at least in the whole view of things, partly for the sake of the original constitution being judged so right in itself, as not fit to be changed because of consequences; which, however wrong, would still be owing to the default of his creatures, and partly for the sake of the ends, worthy of God, everything permitted by him would be overruled to.

"For as infinite wisdom must have perceived, if any consequences of such a constitution would be absolutely inconvenient; God's will, as directed by it, would doubtless have determined the constitution otherwise as he was free, and able to make it what he pleased, that so another course, better answering his wise designs, might flow from it."

And since he would not knowingly suffer anything utterly inconsistent with his own glory, as he had power to hinder it, he has "no doubt, fixed such bounds and limitations to all his creatures, that nothing shall be produced by any of them, which may not have a proper place and use in the sum of events." He has settled also

particular subordinate ends to individual events, and a general good end on the whole, which they shall all together subserve.. .. "Of him," we are told, "and through him, and to him are all things," Romans 11:36. "The counsel of the Lord standeth forever, and the thoughts of his heart to all generations," Psalms 33:11. They reach at once over all transactions of the successive ages of time, and are invariably pursued and executed through them, and at the end of them.. .. "God worketh all things according to the counsel of his own will." God's working here seems to mean not only his own strict and proper agency, but his ordering and disposing even the actions of rational creatures, and all events whatsoever with which his own operations are perpetually intermixed to ends worthy of himself. And this he does according to the counsel of his own will, i.e. his steady will and unalterable purpose beforehand.. ..

It appears, from principles already established, that everything which happens, has a place in the divine decrees, in a manner suitable to its nature. PREFACE. Many have a notion concerning some things in religion, that if they be the truth, yet it is not best that they should be known.

What the devil did to afflict Job, was the exercise and fruit of his devilish dispositions, and his acts therein were devilish. And yet, it is most apparent that those acts and effects of the DEVIL towards JOB were appointed of infinite wisdom for holy ends, but not accomplished by God any otherwise than by permission.

There were many absolute promises of old that salvation should actually be accomplished, that itJE Jr.'s insert. should be of great extent, or extending to great multitudes of mankind: as that the seed of the woman shall bruise the serpent's head Genesis 3:15; "in thee and in thy seed shall all the families of the earth be blessed" Genesis 28:14, and innumerable others—Psalms 22:30, "A seed shall serve him; and it shall be accounted to the Lord for a generation"; Isaiah 53:10, "He shall see his seed"; Psalms 2:6-8,MS: "6. 7." JE actually quotes from v. 8. "Ask of me, and I will thee the heathen for thine inheritance," etc.; Psalms 110:1-3, "Sit thou at my right hand. .. Thy people shall be willing in the day of thy power," etc. And if there were absolute promises of this, then there were absolute purposes of it: for that which is sincerely, absolutely promised, is with an absolute purpose of fulfilling the promise. But what can be devised, that there should be an absolute, determinate, infallible, unchangeable purpose that Christ should actually save vast multitudes of mankind, and yet it not be absolutely purposed that he should save any one single person, but, with regard to every individual soul, this was left undetermined by God, to be determined by man's contingent will that might determine for salvation or against, there being nothing to render it impossible concerning anyone that his will would not finally determine against it?

Observe these prophecies are not merely predictions, but are of the nature of promises, and are often so-called. "Which he hath promised by all the mouths of all his holy prophets since the world began" Luke 1:70, etc., etc.

God takes care to fulfill his own promises, but according to thisI.e. the Arminian. scheme, 'tis not God that fulfills these promises, but men left to themselves, to their contingent wills, fulfills 'em; man's will, that God don't determine, but men determine themselves, in exclusion of God.

All the promises of God are yea and amen 1 Corinthians 1:20, and God himself makes 'em so to be. He takes care of that matter.

Concerning that grand objection of this doctrine's supposing PARTIALITY in God, and a being very dishonorable to him, a being quite contrary to God's extensive and universal benevolence to his creatures: show how the Arminian notions and principles in this matter lead directly to deism, and that, on these principles, 'tis utterly impossible to answer Tindal's objections against revealed religion, especially in his 14th chapter.Matthew Tindal, Christianity as Old as the Creation, ch. 14, "Dr. Clark's discourse of the unchangeable obligation of natural religion, and the truth and certainty of the Christian revelation; considered. And from thence is shown, how inconsistent soever with the design of that discourse, that nothing can be a part of religion, but what is founded on the nature, and reason of things."
