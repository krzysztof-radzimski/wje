The Means and Ends of Excommunication
But now I have written unto you not to keep company, if any man that is called a brother be a fornicator, or covetous, or an idolater, or a railer, or a drunkard, or an extortioner; with such an one, no not to eat. I Corinthians 5:11.

The church of Corinth in the primitive time was very famous for the gifts and graces of the Spirit of God as well as the numerousness of its members. This church was first planted by the apostle Paul; he was as it were the father of it, that had converted its members from heathenism to Christianity, as he puts 'em in mind in these epistles. 1 Corinthians 4:15, “For though ye have ten thousand instructors in Christ, yet have ye not many fathers: for in Christ Jesus I have begotten you through the gospel.” We have an account of the Apostle's planting this church in the eighteenth chapter of the Acts. This church was doubtless excellently regulated by him when he was present to have an immediate inspection of their affairs. But, in his absence, many corruptions and disorders crept in among them.

And among others, one of the members of their church had been guilty of a very heinous kind of wickedness: he had committed incest in one of the greatest degrees of it, in having his father's wife, which, the Apostle observes, was infamous even among the very heathen.

And the church of Corinth had tolerated him in it, so as, notwithstanding, to suffer him to continue in their communion. And this chapter is wholly on this subject. The Apostle reproves this church for conniving at this wickedness, as they had done in not excommunicating the person that had been guilty of it; and directs them speedily to cast him out from among them and deliver him to Satan, and bids 'em purge out such scandalous persons, as the Jews were wont to purge leaven out of their houses when they kept the Passover.

And in the text and two foregoing verses, he does more particularly explain

-- 69 --

their duty with respect to such vicious persons. He enjoins it on them as their duty not to keep company with such; but then shows the difference that ought to be made in their carriage towards those that are of vicious lives among the heathen, that had never joined with them, and those that had been their professed brethren, beginning with the foregoing verse: “Yet not altogether with the fornicators of this world, or with the covetous, or extortioners, or with idolaters; for then must ye needs go out of the world.”

In these words, we observe two things, viz. the duty and the object.

1. The duty enjoined, of which two things are expressed:

(1) The behavior required. Negatively expressed here: “not to keep company.”

(2) The manner or degree: “no not to eat.”

2. The object, who is designed by two things:

(1) That he is one that appears to be vicious. “If any man that is called a brother be a fornicator, or covetous, or an idolater, or a railer, or a drunkard, or an extortioner.” We are not to understand only these particular vices, but these and any other gross sins, or whatever carries in it visible wickedness.

'Tis evident that the Apostle here and in the context intends that we should exclude out of our company all those that are visible wicked men: for in the foregoing verses he expresses his meaning by that, that we should purge out the old leaven; and explaining what he means by leaven, he includes all visible wickedness. Verse 1 Corinthians 5:8, “Therefore let us keep the feast, not with old leaven, neither with the leaven of malice and wickedness; but with the unleavened bread of sincerity and truth.”

(2) The other thing by which the object of this behavior or dealing is characterized, is that he be one that is called a brother, or one that has been a professed Christian and a member of the church.

Doctrine.
Those members of the visible Christian church that are become visibly wicked persons, ought not to be tolerated in the church but should be excommunicated.

In handling this subject, I would speak:

I. Of the nature of excommunication; and,

II. Of the subject; and,

III. Of the ends of it.

-- 70 --

I. I would say something of the nature of excommunication. 'Tis a punishment executed in the name and according to the will of Christ, whereby a person that has heretofore enjoyed the privileges of a member of the visible church of Christ is cast out of the church and delivered unto Satan.

It is of the nature of punishment inflicted. It is expressly called a punishment by the Apostle in 2 Corinthians 2:6. Speaking of the excommunicated Corinthian, he says, “Sufficient to such a man is this punishment.” For though it ben't designed by men for the destruction of the person that is the subject of it, but for his correction, and so is of the nature of a castigatory punishment— at least so far as it is a punishment inflicted by men— yet 'tis in itself a great and dreadful calamity, and the most severe punishment that Christ has appointed in the visible church. And though the church is to seek only the good of the person in it and his recovery from sin, there appearing upon proper trial no reason to hope for the person's recovery by gentle means, yet it is at God's sovereign disposal whether it shall be for a person's humbling or their dreadful and eternal destruction, as it always is one or the other.

In this definition that has been given of excommunication, two things are chiefly considerable: first, wherein this punishment consists; second, by whom it is inflicted.

First. I would show wherein this punishment consists. And 'tis observable that there is something privative and something positive in it.

1. There is something privative in it, which consists in being deprived of a benefit heretofore enjoyed. And this part of the punishment is expressed in Scripture by being “cast out” of the church. So this punishment in the Jewish church was called “putting out” of the synagogue (John 16:2). The word “synagogue” is a word of the same signification as “church.”

So this punishment in the Christian church is called “casting out” of the church. So the apostle John, blaming Diotrephes for causelessly inflicting this punishment. 3 John 10, he “casteth them out of the church.”

So the privative part of this punishment is sometimes expressed by the church's withdrawing from them. 2 Thessalonians 3:6, “Now we command you, brethren, in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ, that ye withdraw yourselves from every brother that walketh disorderly.”

And the privative part of the punishment that there is in excommunication consists in this, viz. a being cast off from the enjoyment of the privileges of God's visible people.

The whole world of mankind is divided into these two sorts: either those

-- 71 --

that are God's visible people, and so are within the visible church of Christ; or those that [are] without the visible church and are of the visible kingdom of Satan.

It is a great privilege to be one of God's visible people within the visible church of Christ and to enjoy the benefits of such, and is abundantly so spoken [of] in Scripture; and it is very doleful [to] be without this visible kingdom, or to be cut off from the privileges of such, and to be excluded as those that are to be treated as of the visible kingdom of Satan.

The privileges that are to be enjoyed in the visible church of Christ, that excommunicated persons are cut off from, are of these four kinds: first, from the charity of the church; second, brotherly society; third, fellowship of the church in worship; fourth, internal privileges of visible Christians.

(1) They are cut off from being the objects of that charity of God's people that is due to Christian brethren. They ben't cut off from all charity of God's people, for they ought to love all men. There is a love of God's people due to the heathen and others that are not in the visible church of Christ. Our love should be like that of our Father which is in heaven, who is kind to the evil and the good. But I speak of that brotherly charity due to visible saints.

Charity, as the Apostle represents it, is as it were the bond by which the several members of the church of Christ are united together, and therefore calls it “the bond of perfectness.” Colossians 3:14, “Put on charity, which is the bond of perfectness.” But when a person is justly excommunicated, it is like a physician's cutting off a diseased member from the body, and then the bond that before united it is broken.

A scandal is the same as a stumbling block. When a member of the visible church is guilty of scandal, a stumbling block is laid before others, in two respects:

1. It is a dishonor to God, and a bad example, and is a stumbling block as it is occasion of others falling into sin. And,

2. It is a stumbling block in the way of the charity of fellow Christians towards the offenders. As long, therefore, as the scandal remains, it stumbles the charity of others; and if it finally remains after proper endeavors to remove them, at last it breaks their charity. And so the offender is cut off from the charity of the church.

He is so, first, from charitable opinion; second, honor; third, complacence.

a. As he is cut off from their charitable opinion and esteem. So that the church can't any longer look upon them as Christians or have that charitable

-- 72 --

opinion of them, and so rejects them. Therefore excommunication is called “rejecting” (Titus 3:10).

This implies a not approving them, or disapproving them in Christian charity. The church can't any longer charitably look upon them as saints or worshippers of God, and can do no other on the contrary than look on them as enemies of God; and so does openly withdraw their charity from them, ceasing to acknowledge them as fellow Christians or fellow worshippers, and treats them no more as fellow worshippers of God henceforward than the heathen.

b. They are also cut off from that honor that is due to brethren and fellow Christians. To be a visible Christian is an honorable character, and a great deal of honor is due to such as are so on that account. But excommunicate persons forfeit this honor.

Christians ought not to pay that honor and respect to 'em that they do to others, but should treat 'em as unworthy of such honor, that they may be ashamed. Christ tells us that they should be unto us “as an heathen man and a publican” (Matthew 18:17), which implies a withholding from them that common respect and honor that we pay to others. There doubtless, therefore, should be a great difference between the respect that we show such, and what we show others; we ought to carry it so as to let 'em see plainly that we don't count 'em worthy of it, and so as tends to put 'em to shame.

c. They ought to be cut off from that brotherly complacence that is due to Christian brethren. Those that are visible Christians, or that we are obliged in charity to receive as saints, there is a great deal of love of complacence that is due to them on that account. This complacence excommunicate persons forfeit.

Love of benevolence or good will is due to 'em still, as 'tis the visibly wicked. We should wish well to 'em and seek their good.

Excommunication itself is to be performed as an act of benevolence or good will. We should seek their good by it, and 'tis to be made use of as a means of their eternal salvation. But complacence and delight in them as visible Christians is to be withdrawn and, on the contrary, they are to be the objects of displicency and abhorrence.

When they are excommunicated, they are avoided and rejected with abhorrence as visibly wicked. We are to cast them out as an unclean thing that defiles the church of God. In this sense the Psalmist professes hatred of those that were visible enemies of God. Psalms 139:21-22, “Do I not hate them, O Lord, that hate thee? and am I not grieved with those that rise up against thee? I hate them with perfect hatred.” Not that he hated them

-- 73 --

with a hatred of malice or ill will, but with displicency and abhorrence of their wickedness. In this respect, we are to be the children of our Father which is in heaven, who, though he loves many wicked men with a love of benevolence, yet he don't love them with a love of complacence.

Thus excommunicate persons are cut off from the charity of the church.

(2) They are cut off from the society that Christians have together as brethren. I speak now of that common society that Christian brethren have together. Thus we are commanded to withdraw from such, to avoid 'em, to have no company with 'em (Romans 16:17), to treat 'em as heathens and publicans. God's people are not only to avoid society with visibly wicked men in sacred things, but, as much as may be, avoid them and withdraw from them as to that common society which is proper towards Christians. Not that they should avoid speaking to 'em on any occasion— all manner and all degrees of society are not forbidden— but all unnecessary society, all such society as holds forth complacence in them, or such as is wont to be among those that delight in the company one of another.

We should not associate ourselves with them, nor to make ourselves companions. Yea, there ought to be that avoiding of their company which shall show great displicency or dislike, or such as there is wont to be in men towards persons that they very much dislike.8

And particularly we are forbidden such a degree of society with them, or appearance of associating ourselves with them, as there is [in] making them our guests at our tables or being their guests at their tables; as is manifest in the text, where we are commanded to have no company with such, “no not to eat.”

That this has not respect to eating with them at the Lord's Supper, but a common eating, there are two things that make evident:9

1. 'Tis evident by the words that this eating that is here forbidden, is one of the lowest degrees of keeping company that is forbidden. “Don't keep company,” says the Apostle, “no not to eat.” As much as to say, “No not in so low a degree as to eat.” But this would be ridiculous sort of language for eating with them at the Lord's Supper, which is the very highest degree of visible Christian communion. Who can suppose that the Apostle would speak such nonsense as this: “Take heed and have no company with such a man, no not so much as in the highest degree of communion that you can have”?

-- 74 --

2. The Apostle mentions this eating as away of keeping company, that they might not have with an excommunicate brother, that they might have with the heathen. He tells 'em not to keep company with fornicators, but then he tells 'em he don't mean the fornicators of the world, that is, the heathen. “But,” says he, “now I have written unto you not to keep company, if any man that is called a brother be a fornicator, or covetous, or an idolater, or a railer, or a drunkard, or an extortioner; with such an one no not to eat.”

This makes it most apparent that the Apostle don't mean eating at the Lord's table, for so we, as they, might not keep company with the fornicators or of the heathen any more than with an excommunicate person.

[Ques. 1.] And here a question may arise: How far the church are to treat excommunicate (persons) as they would heathens, or those that never have been of the visible church; {whether or no} they are to treat them as heathen excepting in these two things?

I answer, there is to be a difference.

a. The church is to have a greater concern for their welfare still than if they never had been brethren, and therefore ought to take more pains to reclaim them and to save them by admonishing them and otherwise than they are obliged to take towards those that have been always brethren. This seems manifest by that of the Apostle, 2 Thessalonians 3:14-15, “And if any man obey not our word by this epistle, have no company with him, that he may be ashamed. Yet count him not as an enemy, but admonish him as a brother.”

That consideration— that he has been a brother heretofore, and that we han't so finally cast him off from that relation, but that we are still hoping and using means that he may be such an one— obliges us to concern ourselves more for the good of their souls than of those that we never had any concern with, and so to pray more for them and take more pains with them by admonishing them.

And the reason of the thing shows the same: for this very ordinance of excommunication is used for that end, that we may thereby obtain their good. And surely we should be more concerned for the good of those that have been our brethren, that are now under the use of means used by us for their good, than for those that we never had any concern with. Thus there should be more of the exercise of love of benevolence than {for those we never had any concern with}. But then,

b. On the other hand, as to what concerns love of complacence, they ought to be treated with greater displicency and disrespect than the heathen. This is plain by the text and context, for the Apostle {says}, “For what

-- 75 --

have I to do to judge them also that are without? do not ye judge them that are within? But them that are without God judgeth. Therefore put away from among yourselves that wicked person” [1 Corinthians 5:12-13].

And 'tis also plain by the reason of the thing: for those that have once been professed Christians, and have apostatized and cast off that visibility, deserve to be treated with more abhorrence. [Their sins are] more aggravated {and are} more to the dishonor of religion, more abhorred of God. And therefore when Christ says, Matthew 18:17, “Let him be unto thee as an heathen man and a publican,” ['tis] not meant as Christians ought to treat heathens and publicans: for they might eat with heathens and publicans. Christ eat with such; and the Apostle gives leave to eat with such, and in the context gives leave to keep company with such as they may not.

But Christ's meaning must be to treat them as the Jews were wont {to treat Gentiles}, and as the disciples had been always taught among the Jews, and brought up, and used to treat {Gentiles}. They would by no means eat with publicans and sinners, would not eat with Gentiles [or] fornicators. Peter therefore durst not eat with the Gentiles when the Jews were present (Galatians 2:12).

Ques. 2. What kindness or respect may and ought to be shown such persons?

a. There are some things by which the members of the church are1 obliged to show kindness to 'em, and those things are chiefly two: to pray for them and to admonish them.

b. Common duties and offices of humanity ought to be exercised towards them, such as relieving them when they are sick or under any other distress, allowing them those benefits of human society and help that are needful for the support of life.

c. Duties of natural or civil relation are still to be performed towards [them]. Excommunication don't release children from obligations of duty to parents, nor parents from parental affection and care towards children; nor are husbands and wives released from duties proper to their relations, and so of brothers and sisters and other domestic relations.

(3) They are cut off from the fellowship of the worship of the Christian church. The notion of the visible church of Christ is that part of mankind that, as his people, are united in the worship, or that do in agree in upholding his appointed worship.

-- 76 --

And the notion of a particular visible church of Christ is a particular society of worshippers, or of visible saints, united for the joint worship of God, according to his institutions or ordinances.

And one great and main privilege, therefore, that they have is the fellowship they have in the worship which God has appointed in his church. They therefore that are excommunicated are cut off from this privilege of the fellowship of the worship of God's people. They have no communion with the people of God in any part of their worship. They can have no fellowship with them in baptism, or the Lord's Supper, or in prayers, or singing God's praises.

He that is the mouth of the worshipping congregation, in offering up public prayers, he is not the mouth of those that are excommunicated. He is the mouth only of the visibly worshipping society; but they are cast out of the worshipping society. The church may and ought to pray for such, but they can't have fellowship with such in prayer. The minister, when speaking in prayer, ought to pray for those that are shut out of the society of God's visible servants or worshippers, but he don't speak in their name: for he speaks only in the name of the united society of visible saints or worshippers. For God's people to put up prayers in their name would imply a receiving them into charity, or charitably looking upon and receiving them as servants or worshippers of God. But as was observed before, excommunicated persons are cast out of the charity of the church in this respect. The church has no longer charity for them as servants and worshippers of God, but looks on them as visibly wicked men and enemies of God, and treats 'em as such.

So when a congregation of visible saints join together in singing of praises to God together— as the Psalmists says, Psalms 34:3, “Let us extol his name together”— they do it only as joining with those that are, in their charity, to be looked upon as fellow servants and fellow worshippers of God. They don't do it as joining with heathen. God's people don't say to open enemies of God remaining such, “Come, let us exalt his name together”; but they say it to their brethren in God's service.

(4) There are privileges of a more internal nature, that those that are members of the visible {church enjoy}, that excommunicated persons are cut off from.

They are in the way of covenant blessings, being God's covenant people. {They} have more encouragement to come to God, to seek any mercy they need, by prayer.

God's visible church are the people among whom God has set his tabernacle, and where he is wont to bestow his blessing. They that are excommunicated

-- 77 --

are in a sense cast out of God's sight, or from God's face, into a land of banishment, as Cain was. Genesis 4:14, Genesis 4:16, “Behold, thou hast driven me out this day from the face of the earth; and from thy face shall I be hid; and I shall be a fugitive and a vagabond in the earth; and it shall come to pass, that every one that findeth me shall slay me.… And Cain went out from the presence of the Lord, and dwelt in the land of Nod, on the east of Eden.”

They are not in the way of those smiles of providence, those tokens of God's favor and that light of God's countenance that those that are within {the visible church} are, nor have they God's covenant to plead as they have.

Thus for the privative part of the punishment. I proceed,

[2.] Secondly, to the positive part, which is expressed by being delivered to Satan, in the fifth verse of the context. By which two things seem to be signified:

(1) A being delivered over to the calamities that they are subject to, that are the subjects of the visible kingdom of the devil.2 As they are thrust out from among God's people, so doubtless they are at least to be looked upon, in most respects at least, as in the miserable deplorable circumstances, that those are in that are under the visible tyranny of the devil, as the heathen are; and in many respects to suffer the cruel tyranny of the devil, in a manner agreeable to the condition they are in as thus cast out into his visible kingdom.

(2) It is reasonable to suppose that God is willing to make the devil the instrument of those peculiar severe chastisements that their apostasy deserves. As they deserve more severe chastisement than the heathen, and are delivered to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, so we may well suppose either that God is wont to let Satan loose, sorely to molest them outwardly or inwardly, so by severe means to destroy the flesh and humble them.

As he suffers the devil to take possession and dreadfully to harden, and so to destroy them forever, so, though what men are to aim at is only the destruction of the flesh, yet whether it shall prove the destruction of the flesh and the eternal and more dreadful destruction of them, is at God's sovereign disposal. So much for the nature {of the punishment}.

Second. I come now to show by whom this punishment is to be looked upon as being inflicted.

-- 78 --

1. When it is regularly and duly inflicted, it is to be looked upon as done by Christ himself. That is supported in the definition, that it is according to his will and to his direction in his Word. And therefore he is to be looked on as principal in it, and it is to be looked upon as really from him, as truly as if he were on earth, etc.

2. As it is done by men, 'tis only ministerially. They don't act of themselves any more than in preaching the word. When [the] word [is] preached, 'tis as Christ's word, as his ambassadors speaking in his name. So here, {they} act in his name, by his willing and not their own, as of his will and not their own. Indeed, 'tis only a particular applying the word of Christ. And therefore 'tis promised that when [it is] duly done, {their act is} confirmed in heaven; i.e. Christ will confirm [it] as acknowledging it to be his own act. And he will, in his future providence, have regard to what is done as done by himself. He will look on the person and treat him as cast out and delivered to Satan by himself, and if he don't repent, will forever reject him and damn him (Matthew 18:18, John 20:23).

II. The subjects of it.

They are those that have been members {of God's visible church} and are become visibly wicked. Visibly wicked persons ought not to be tolerated but should be cast out, as the very nature of the visible church shows, which is a society of visible saints or visibly holy persons.

Such as have been members don't become visibly wicked but by these two things:

First. By gross sin.

The saints may be guilty of other sins, and very often are without any just stumbling block to public charity.

Second. That they continue impenitent in it after proper means used to reclaim. Merely being guilty of gross sin, unless with some peculiar circumstances, is reason sufficient for us to look on another as wicked men.

III. The ends. The special ends of this are three:

First. That the church may be kept pure and God's ordinances not defiled. This end is mentioned in the context: that the other members themselves may not be defiled. 'Tis necessary that they thus bear a testimony against sin.

Second. That others may be deterred from wickedness. That others may fear.

Third. That they may be reclaimed, [that their] souls may be saved. [After] other, more gentle, means have been used in vain, then we are to use severe means to bring 'em to conviction and shame and humiliation, by being rejected and avoided by the church, treated with disrespect, disowned

-- 79 --

by God, delivered to Satan, his being made the instrument of chastising them.

This is the last means, with concomitant admonitions, that the church is to use for the reclaiming those members of the church that become visibly wicked; which, if it bent effectual, what is next to be expected is destruction without remedy.
