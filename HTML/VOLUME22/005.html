Preface to the Period

-- 3 --

In 1739, Edwards had history on his mind. Part of this history was local and personal— “history” as everyone understands the term. The great Northampton revival of 1734, which Edwards had led and chronicled in the internationally acclaimed Faithful Narrative, was a recent memory well on its way to becoming the new paradigm in the transatlantic history of revivalism. A Faithful Narrative not only vaulted Edwards to the forefront of revival practitioners and theorists, it also served as a template for all subsequent accounts. One of especial promise, written by the brash English itinerant George Whitefield, chronicled in autobiographical form Whitefield's own sensational revivals in the fields and urban centers of England.

In time— a very short time— Whitefield's dazzling evangelistic performances would play to colonial audiences everywhere, including a mighty “awakening” that would engulf Northampton. But in early 1739 Edwards knew only that his recent past was far more glorious than his present, and he was not well. Timothy Cutler, Edwards' former rector at Yale College and now an Anglican minister in Boston, described Edwards' “ill health” and doubted that he would live past the age of forty.1 Beyond his physical distress, and perhaps related to it, was a perceived loss of spiritual vitality within his congregation. Less discerning observers might not have been concerned. To all outward appearances, life in Northampton went on in good form: a new and much grander meetinghouse had been constructed with major assistance from the town's wealthiest families; Edwards himself received the highest pastoral salary in the land outside of Boston; and new members continued to be admitted to the church, albeit in reduced numbers.

But Edwards was no ordinary observer, and in each mark of vitality he saw signs of decay and approaching judgment. The meetinghouse was too much a tribute to affluence and privilege, its seating determined by wealth rather than spirituality. The construction of a new town house meant that Northampton's politics would be conducted outside of the

-- 4 --

meetinghouse, symbolizing a fracture between church and state that boded ill for both: politically, factional tensions between “court” and “country” parties would increase without the restraining influence of the church;2 ecclesiastically, the power of the church would be diminished as a civic— and civilizing— influence. Although Edwards' salary was generous, he had not won it without a fight, and it was often resented by many of the merchant “river gods” around him. Most ominous, church membership masked a deep-seated spiritual apathy and indifference to eternity where only years before devotion had burned bright and hot. How in practical terms, Edwards wondered, was the present to be redirected, and history— the history of 1734— reclaimed?

Speculative History

To this practical sense of history and yearning for a revival of Northampton's recent spiritual past Edwards added a more speculative preoccupation with history. This speculative interest in history had been long in the making and he was to wrestle with it for the rest of his life. Edwards sensed that the days of systematic theology were numbered. For theology (that is, orthodox theology) to survive, it would need to don new habiliments. By 1739 and even earlier, Edwards had begun to suspect that “history” was, in fact, larger than the antiquarian terms by which it was then known; indeed, it was larger than theology itself. In his evolving thought, carefully recorded and organized in reams of notebooks, commentaries, and sermons, history was emerging as nothing less than a container for the synthetic whole of theology and, indeed, of God's innermost self-revelation.3 Gone were parochial notions of history as genealogy or the simple chronicle of human achievements and sequential events. Gone, even, the larger but still theologically restrained notion, familiar to Puritans always, of history as the chronicle of “God's Wonder-Working Providence” on earth. Edwards had an even grander conception of history, capacious enough to contain all these ideas— and more.

-- 5 --

Earlier Protestant thinkers such as Philipp Melanchthon, John Calvin, or William Ames had thought of theology as the ultimate canvas on which to record the being of God and his relationship to his creation. Whatever distance the Reformers may have traveled from Rome, they had still retained a Thomistic sense of systematic theology as the queen of the sciences. Edwards substituted history. His history was not the history of William Bradford or Cotton Mather, a mere recording of New England towns and their ministers under the nationalistic gloss of Magnalia Christi Americana. Nor, as John F. Wilson convincingly demonstrates, was it history in the emerging “Enlightenment” sense, the “scientific” history of politics and great men based strictly on empirical observation with no recourse to supernatural revelation. By comparison, Edwards' method was, in Wilson's terms, “profoundly unhistoriographical in any modern sense.”4

Alongside these modern senses of history, however, lies another, more mythic sense of history that is best labeled “metanarrative.” The modern model of history, which we might label “historiographical” or, in Edwards' term, “actual” time, is simple chronology— history measured in minutes and years and recorded in written records. But as anthropologists and biblical scholars remind us, it is also possible to order and understand history in mythic terms, or, as Edwards put it, as “virtual” time— history as seen from God's time— transcending temporal perspective. Central to this model are separate but overlapping senses of time, as in the creation myths common to all religions. But, as we shall see, Edwards had an even larger story in mind: a narrative of “redemption.” To frame the metanarrative of redemption, Edwards would employ historiographical time from every source he could lay his hands on, both sacred and secular. But he would also subject it to the most important time in the narrative, namely, divine time. For all his piety, perhaps because of his piety, Edwards was not afraid to see time from God's vantage point. He would take his narrative where his predecessors had been afraid to tread. If a majestic-enough story could be constructed, Edwards speculated, it could contain all the doctrines and philosophical underpinnings of systematic theology in a more compelling— and popularly accessible— format. In other words, a metanarrative method could do all that systematic theology, and for that matter historiography, could do and more.

Edwards' history incorporated philosophy, theology, and narrative into a synthetic whole. Earlier he had established the proposition that

-- 6 --

“heaven is a world of love,” a metaphysical state infused with the innermost being and character of the Trinity. So too, he proposed, earth was a world of pulsating divine energy, and hell a perversion of love that set in motion the cosmic supernatural conflict between God and Satan with earth as the prize. What if the story of all three— heaven, earth, and hell— were integrated into one narrative, superior to systematic theology for its drama and to earthbound historiography for its prophetic inspiration?

While Edwards was intrigued by the idea of a narrative history, this does not imply that he was uninterested in theology or even that he would not identify himself as a preacher or theologian if forced to choose. In fact, Edwards often referred to his work as “divinity,” and produced several treatises, most notably Original Sin, Concerning the End for Which God Created the World, and The Nature of True Virtue, that take on the aspect of a systematic theology. It is rather to say that Edwards early on came to sense— especially in his sermons— that the most effective way to realize the theologian's goal of knowledge of God was to abandon the synchronic methods of formal theology and “throw” the truths of “divinity” into the diachronic form of a history.

It is one of the greater ironies in the transmission of “Edwardsean” theology that virtually all of Edwards' proteges missed the point of his creative genius. While treasuring his philosophical treatises and arresting attacks on Arminianism, none of them, as Wilson observes, continued Edwards' “theological preoccupation with redemption as the grand and encompassing doctrine of the Christian religion.”5

Never in American intellectual history has there been a more glaring example of misplaced identity. When Edwards' foremost student, Samuel Hopkins, took it upon himself to write a systematic theology as a tribute

-- 7 --

to the supposed opus that Edwards never lived to complete, he created, in fact, the antithesis of what Edwards had hoped to achieve and in the process denuded Edwards' thought of its sublimity and mystery. The reason Edwards never wrote a systematic theology to contain all his concepts and doctrines was not that his life was cut short prematurely (John Calvin, after all, composed his first draft of the Institutes of the Christian Religion at age twenty-six) but that early in his career he turned away from the idea of writing a “Rational Account” and toward something different. History, not rational or systematic theology, would be the canvas on which his budding ideas on theology, metaphysics, typology, psychology, language, and, most important, religious affections would find form and structure.

At the same time that Edwards discovered his true artistic genre, he located the organizing theme for this history as “salvation,” or “redemption,” the most sublime theme of all, grander even than creation. If Edwards' vision bore a close similarity to Scripture itself, that should not be surprising to modern scholars. Scripture narratives, as Erich Auerbach and Hans Frei have shown, represented a new literary form, a “realistic narrative.”6 Edwards would have understood the findings of these modern scholars instantly. In some ways his vision was even grander than Scripture, for it would incorporate all revealed scriptural truths and then interlace them with ongoing narratives from church and “profane” history.7

By 1739 Edwards had found a context in which to place and interpret all the turbulent events looming in the next two years. He would understand revivals on three levels interacting with three histories: the history of heaven and redemption; the history of earth, from the fall to the approaching millennium; and the history of hell and the horrors of damnation. In one way or another, each of these themes was to inform his preaching during the awakening. He strove mightily to bring his hearers into contact with these worlds and, by implication, with their own place in the history of planet Earth.

-- 8 --

Long after the events of 1740–43, which vaulted him to even higher international acclaim, the narrative vision remained. In earlier volumes of this series, John F. Wilson and Wilson H. Kimnach have traced the enduring appeal of these speculations throughout Edwards' life. Over the years, through voluminous reading and careful notetaking, the history of redemption became his capstone ambition. In his regulatory notebook “Subjects of Inquiry,” written in the 1750s, he made the following methodological notations:

Show how parallel, in many instances, historical evidence of a past age, by the testimony handed down to us, is to the evidence we have of what is present of the existence and estate of a distant country or nation that we have never seen.

And consider what may be argued from this, that we see, ourselves, to what degree truth is maintained in narration of things past, in our age, and so may argue how it will be through many such ages. For the ages are all continuing. The last half of our age is the first half of another, and so all are interlaced as it were. We argue in the same manner as we [do] concerning the truth of narrations concerning distant places; so far as we travel, we have opportunity to see with our own eyes how far truth is kept in its carriage through such a distance, etc.8

By 1757, on the eve of his premature death, the grand vision had formally matured into nothing less than a Descartes-like new “method” for conceiving religion and thinking about God. When in that same year the Board of Trustees of the College of New Jersey invited Edwards to be their new president, he hesitated primarily because his history was not complete. In a sense, all his great treatises, written in six-month bursts between 1750 and 1757, were ingredients to be digested and embodied in the tripartite narrative. Those who see “the end” of Edwards' scholarly career as his all-fronts assault on Arminianism and Deism miss the point. Those treatises were intended only to remove so much heterodox debris from the highway, so that the way would be cleared to his true destination: the narrative history of redemption. In his often-cited letter to the Board of Trustees, he revealed his ambition: “I have had on my mind and heart (which I long ago began, not with any view to publication) a great work, which I call A History of the Work of Redemption, a body of divinity in an entire new method, being thrown into the form of an history, considering

-- 9 --

the affair of Christian theology, as the whole of it, in each part, stands in reference to the great work of redemption by Jesus Christ; which I suppose is to be the grand design of all God's designs, and the summum and ultimum of all the divine operations and decrees; particularly considering all parts of the grand scheme in their historical order.”9

As he continued to describe his project to the Trustees, Edwards made plain how his New History would differ from prevailing notions in several particulars. First, it would be a history in which theology was subordinated to history, rather than vice versa. Second, and more shocking, Edwards went on in his letter to observe that his New History would be one that transcended space and recorded time, a history telling the simultaneous stories of three realms: “This history will be carried on with regard to all three worlds, heaven, earth and hell: considering the connected, successive events and alterations, in each so far as the Scriptures give any light; introducing all parts of divinity in that order which is most scriptural and most natural: which is a method which appears to me the most beautiful and entertaining, wherein every divine doctrine, will appear to greatest advantage in the brightest light, in the most striking manner, showing the admirable contexture and harmony of the whole.”1 The word “method” carried with it steep claims, indeed, the highest claims.

Although Edwards wrote the letter to the Trustees in 1757, these themes were clearly on his mind in 1739, and even earlier.2 From his private notebooks, the “Miscellanies,” and the “Catalogue” of reading, we can discover an ever-mounting interest in history, both historiographical and mythic. Edwards' “Catalogue” documents his wide-ranging fascination with the history of the world and its many gods. In his own New England he evidenced scant interest. But the world was another matter. In 1737, for example, he records his desire to read An Universal History from the Earliest Account of Time to the Present: Compiled from Original Authors and Illustrated with Maps, Cuts, Notes, Chronological and Other Tables. The work had just been published in London and included indispensable materials for Edwards' project. His notes on the book, copied from an advertisement in a learned journal, reveal that it includes “an exact account of the migrations and conquests of every people, the successions and reigns of their respective princes, their religion and government, trade, customs

-- 10 --

and learning; the whole immediately extracted from original authors.… N.B. This work is so generally esteemed that it is translating into several languages in different parts of Europe (London Magazine on April 1737, of which The Republick of Letters gives a very agreeable idea, vol. 18, p. 185).” And in two separate entries dating from the late 1730s, Edwards endorses Samuel Shuckford's The Sacred and Profane History of the World (London, 1728), “greatly commended in The Republick of Letters; and spoken of as an excellent thing by [Philip] Doddridge.”3

Literary periodicals such as New Memoirs of Literature or, more important, The Republick of Letters, were indispensable guides to all that was being written about earthly time and history. Edwards avidly read them and acquired copies whenever possible. For mythic history and the worlds it described, his primary source was, of course, Scripture. But Scripture was augmented by the history of heaven and hell as it evolved from the Church Fathers, through the Reformers and the Puritans. Heaven and hell no less than earth had their histories, as did their citizens: angels and demons. For Edwards, each of these worlds was equally real and dynamic, a constantly evolving protean force that emanated ultimately from the mind of God. Each, moreover, was equally knowable through creation and the revelation of nature, and through the Scriptures, the revealed self-disclosure of God in his diverse created worlds. Each, therefore, required a history. These histories would not be independent of one another, but interconnected and whole, like a finely spun tapestry. Instead of connecting doctrines, Edwards would weave a story— a story that introduced all the major tenets and philosophical underpinnings of Christian theology— but in the process, he would introduce a different, dramatic form, one that he identified as the history of redemption.

A History of the Work of Redemption

For a brief moment in 1739 speculative history enjoyed primacy over Edwards' immediate present. He found breathing room for big thoughts and substantial reading. The medium for these mythic ideas was a massive sermon series composed for his Northampton congregation. This

-- 11 --

discourse, or treatise delivered in installments, published as a separate volume in this Edition as A History of the Work of Redemption, is essential context to other sermons published in this volume, and so requires some summary.

In all, Edwards delivered thirty lecture-sermons between March and August 1739 on the Old Testament text from Isaiah 51:8, “For the moth shall eat them up like a garment, and the worm shall eat them like wool: but my righteousness shall be for ever, and my salvation from generation to generation.” The doctrine, sustained from first to last, was simply stated: “The Work of Redemption is a work that God carries on from the fall of man to the end of the world.” Yet lying behind this simplicity— surprisingly straightforward and almost commonplace— was a vast apparatus of reflection.

The content of these sermons was not philosophical or dogmatic but historical. Edwards could have approached his grand design through philosophy. He could have orchestrated the series, like his Yale College notebooks, around issues of atoms and resistance, mind and perception, God and Being, beauty and proportion, free will and determination, or some such. But he chose history instead.

Despite its sermonic form, the Redemption discourse was unique among Edwards' sermons. Suffice it to note here that even in the familiar sermonic form, the series differed decisively from anything Edwards accomplished before or, for that matter, after. The most obvious difference was the sheer length of the series. Earlier sermon series, save for Charity and Its Fruits, encompassed only three or four sermons. In his 1739 series, Edwards strove for something more comprehensive and complex than could be contained in a few sermons. Moreover, each of these sermons was structurally incomplete— a Doctrine without an Application, or an Application without a Use. Entire preaching units functioned as mere parts of a traditional sermon rather than as self-contained entities complete unto themselves. Thus, text and doctrine were explicated in Sermon 1, “reasons” in the form of historical epochs from the fall to incarnation in Sermons 2 through 20, an Application in Sermons 21 through 25, and an “Improvement” in Sermons 29 and 30.4

Edwards' doctrine of redemption, as the central thread of his great project, would not have been well suited to a systematic theology. To be

-- 12 --

grasped in all its completeness, it had to move out of the polemical confines of the schoolmen and theologians and present itself as a narrative: the greatest story ever told. Indeed, it is precisely the epic quality of A History of the Work of Redemption in its posthumously printed form in the nineteenth century that gave it its “enormous influence” on “popular culture.”5

The “scheme” of redemption perfectly fitted Edwards' ambitions to move through two times. At the outset he observed that “the work [of redemption] itself and all that pertained to it, was virtually done and finished [before creation], but not actually” in historiographical time. In virtual time— God's time— an indivisible divine “present” engulfs earthly past and future. God's time, and only God's time, is eternal. Even heaven, though without end, is finite. In A History of Heaven, Jeffrey Burton Russell makes this important distinction: “Endless life is one thing, but God's ability to comprehend and embrace all time in one moment is another. Endlessness is perpetual, but only the existence of the entire space and time of the cosmos in one moment is eternal.”6 In similar terms, Edwards pointed out that in God's time, every event merges with every other event, not in successive chronological sequence but as “several parts of one scheme… in which all the persons of the Trinity do conspire and all the various dispensations that belong to it are united, as the several wheels in one machine, to answer one end, and produce one effect.”7

Conscious of heaven and eternity, Edwards made clear that redemption required the fact of sin to generate the need for salvation; hence the fall marked the starting point for his narrative in actual time. The scheme or divine plan was in fact eternal and virtual. Before creation, “The persons of the Trinity were as it were confederated in a design and a covenant of redemption.” Indeed, “the world itself seems to have been created in order to it.”8 With the historical metanarrative in mind, Edwards prompted his congregation to expect a personally riveting narrative of what God had created them for. All thirty sermons tended “to one great end and effect.” It was, Edwards stated, a far more important history than creation, for creation was the means to a greater end. Since God had created the world as his stage to dramatize redemption, the end of creation could not merely be the happiness of his creatures, as rationalists and

-- 13 --

deists were claiming. It had to be God's own self-glorification.9 At the conclusion of his series, Edwards came full circle to this central thesis:

This Work of Redemption is so much the greatest of all the works of God, that all other works are to be looked upon either as part of it, or appendages to it, or are some way reducible to it. And so all the decrees of God do some way or other belong to that eternal covenant of redemption that was between the Father and the Son before the foundation of the world; every decree of God is some way or other reducible to that covenant. And seeing this Work of Redemption is so great a work, hence we need not wonder that the angels desire to look into it. And we need not wonder that so much is made of it in Scripture, and that 'tis so much insisted on in the histories, and prophecies, and songs of the Bible, for the Work of Redemption is the great subject of the whole Bible. In its doctrines, its promises, its types, its songs, its histories, and its prophecies.1

In other sermons from this period, the theme recurs. Thus in his sermon on The Importance and Advantage of a Thorough Knowledge of Divine Truth, delivered in November 1739, Edwards proclaimed, “Those who have applied themselves most closely, have studied the longest, and have made the greatest attainments in this knowledge, know but little of what is to be known. The subject is inexhaustible. That Divine Being, who is the main subject of this science, is infinite, and there is no end to the glory of his perfections. His works at the same time are wonderful, and cannot be found out to perfection; especially the work of redemption, which is that work of God about which the science of divinity is chiefly conversant, is full of unsearchable wonders” (p. 95).

Edwards' historical forays into heaven, hell, and earth were based on church teachings and Scripture, but his history of earth also drew from wide reading in general histories that preoccupied him in the days of this series and thereafter. If some periods of earth's providential history were

-- 14 --

missing from Scripture, Edwards noted, it was because profane histories filled the void. “Thus did the great God that disposes all things order it,” Edwards declared in the Redemption discourse. “He took care to give an historical account of things from the beginning of the world through all those former ages which profane history don't reach, and ceased not, not till he came to those later ages in which profane history related things with some certainty.”2 In The Importance and Advantage of a Thorough Knowledge, Edwards reflected on the centrality of print and the “republic of letters” it created: “We are in some respects under far greater advantages for learning of knowledge, now in these latter ages of the church, than Christians were formerly; especially by reason of the art of printing, of which God hath given us the benefit” (p. 101).

Edwards used the history of earth and earthly time as the spine of his narrative and interleaved the worlds of heaven and hell as they intersected, so that the whole resembled “an house or temple that is building,” in which “first the workmen are sent forth, then the materials are gathered, then the ground fitted, then the foundation is laid, then the superstructure erected one part after another, till at length the topstone is laid. And all is finished.”3 In these terms, earth was the foundation, heaven and hell the superstructure.

The themes Edwards developed in the Redemption discourse occupied him directly and indirectly in many of sermons preached in this period, and can be summarized under the headings “Heaven,” “Earth,” and “Hell.” When external events vaulted Edwards from speculative history to leading participation in the “Great Awakening,” he drew on these categories to understand the meaning of the revivals and to frame his sermons.

Heaven

Although earth grounded his narrative project, Edwards began with heaven: the creation of the angels and the eternal communications of the Deity. Few systematic theologies dwelt excessively on heaven and its citizens, which was one more reason why Edwards could not frame his life's ambition in a formal theology. Heaven could exist only in narrative form communicated to the church through a series of stories and histories. Heaven transcended formal theology and abstract categories and placed the ordinary person on a level with the most advanced academic.4 Heaven,

-- 15 --

no less than earth, was created and not eternal, and it was created for the same end: the work of redemption. Earlier, in his “Miscellanies,” Edwards had ruminated over invisible worlds. “Heaven is a part of the universe that, in the first creation and the disposition of things that was made in the beginning, was appropriated to God to be that part of the universe that should be his residence, while other parts were destined to other uses.”5

The entrée into this divine residence was love. In the sermon series on Charity and Its Fruits, preached just before the Redemption series, Edwards described heaven as “a world of love,” created “to be the place of God's glorious presence”: “There dwells God the Father, and so the Son, who are united in infinitely dear and incomprehensible mutual love.… There is the Holy Spirit, the spirit of divine love, in whom the very essence of God, as it were, all flows out or is breathed forth in love.… There in heaven this fountain of love, this eternal three in one, is set open without any obstacle to hinder access to it.”6

Although not eternal, heaven was older than earth. Like earth, heaven was a place with a physical, social, and spiritual topography. There were gardens, cities, temples, and fountains. If streets were not paved with gold (Edwards was ready to concede the metaphoric power of gold), there were nonetheless streets. Likewise, heaven had a spiritual architecture as the abode of the elect, the crown of the martyrs, and the fount of enlightenment. Heaven also had clouds on which Christ would one day descend; heaven radiated a physicality with material and moral landscapes of inexpressible beauty. At the center of Edwards' Dantean vision of heaven was supernatural light not unlike the “blazing point” of Paradiso, with angels spinning a ring of light.

Heaven embraced saints, angels, and, of course, the persons of the Trinity. Between them, the three constituents formed a divine “society.” Heaven, Edwards believed, was “the only way that ever has been contrived for the gathering together angels and men into one society, and one place of habitation.”7 The best human analogy to this heavenly society was the church community in worship. In They Sing a New Song, a public thanksgiving sermon preached in November 1740, Edwards explained that the closest earthly analogy to the saints in heaven was the church at worship, which, he concluded, “will make our public assemblies some image of

-- 16 --

heaven, and will make our sabbath days and thanksgiving days some resemblance of that eternal sabbath and thanksgiving that is solemnized by that innumerable company of angels and spirits of just men made perfect” (p. 241).

Modern readers might be surprised at how frequently angels appear as central characters in Edwards' tri-world narrative. In his sermons Edwards repeated themes he first recorded privately in his “Miscellanies,” reminding his hearers that “The creating heaven was in order to the Work of Redemption; it was to be an habitation for the redeemed and the Redeemer, Matthew 25:34. Angels [were created to be] ministering spirits [to the inhabitants of the] lower world [which is] to be the stage of the wonderful Work [of Redemption].”8 Some angels had names, like Michael the warrior, the archangel identified principally with ancient Israel and, later, at Armageddon, the commander of the celestial armies that battled Satan and his demons. In contrast, the archangel Gabriel appears as the messenger of mercy and promise in Daniel and Luke. In Luke, it is Gabriel who informs the Virgin Mary of her coming glory. A hierarchy of powers and offices existed among angels, from archangels and angels at the top whose activities embraced heaven and earth, to cherubim and seraphim, whose sole residence was heaven surrounding the heavenly throne (Isaiah 6:1-06). Satan himself was formerly an archangel and, in popular church teaching, second only to God. With his banishment for rebellion along with “hundreds” of other rebel angels, the ranks of remaining angels were purified so that, Edwards concluded, “they never will sin, and they are out of any danger of it.”9 Although only a handful of angelic names were known, and although most were invisible to human eyes, they numbered in the millions and exceeded the earthly creation. Even more, saints and angels interacted with the Trinity. In an earlier sermon, Edwards had pointed out that “God is a spirit and is not to be seen with bodily [eyes].… 'Tis not any sight with the bodily eyes. For [the] souls of the saints [in heaven], they see God, and the angels [also, who are] spirits and never were united to bodies.… 'Tis not any form or visible representation, not any shape or color or shining light that is seen, wherein this great happiness of the soul consists.”1

Throughout the sermons in this volume, Edwards situates angels at the center of his teachings. Scripture is filled with instances when God “hath… sent angels to bring divine instructions to men.” In heaven, angels “spend much of their time in searching into the great things of divinity.”

-- 17 --

When not singing and ministering, angels are studying. Repeatedly Edwards asks his hearers to become like angels in their study of Scripture. Both will find “the glorious work of redemption” at the center of that study: “[W]e ourselves may become like angels, and like God himself in our measure.… Such things as these have been the main subject of the study of the holy patriarchs, prophets, and apostles, and the most excellent men that ever were in the world, and are also the subject of the study of the angels in heaven” (p. 91). At the center of angelic contemplations of redemption is Christ's love. “He is so lovely and excellent, that the angels in heaven do greatly love him; their hearts overflow with love to him, and they are continually, day and night without ceasing, praising him and giving him glory” (p. 172).

Although citizens of heaven, angels spend considerable time on earth as ministering spirits. Indeed, they exist all around true believers in invisible armies. While rejecting the Roman Catholic belief in “guardian angels” for children commissioned at baptism, Edwards frequently reminded children that angels were particularly attentive to them. In his sermon Children Ought to Love the Lord Jesus Christ Above All, delivered in August 1740, Edwards consoled his young listeners with these words: “If you truly love Christ, all the glorious angels of heaven will love you. For they delight in those that love Christ; they love to see such a sight as children giving their hearts to Christ. There will be joy in heaven among the angels that day that you begin to love Christ. And they will be your angels; they will take care of you while you sleep, and God will give 'em charge to keep you in all your ways” (p. 178). But angels are neither childish nor limited to children. As ever-present realities they represented for Edwards the potential for human nature to become angelic; they lent an intensification of being and becoming that was unavailable to fallen human mortals.

Although peaceful after Satan's expulsion, heaven was never static. Indeed, it was a theater of dramas every bit as consuming as earth's. And unlike those on earth, heaven's inhabitants could participate in their own heavenly dramas and conflicts and, at the same time, view and even participate in the dramas on earth. Heaven no less than earth remained incomplete, in need of “newness,” so that “with respect to the saints and angels” all things “in heaven and earth and throughout the universe are in a state of preparation for the state of consummation; all the wheels are going, none of them stop, and all are moving in a direction to the last and most perfect state.”2

Earth

The history of earth, introduced in the History of Redemption series, was also integrated into other sermons represented in this period. In all these sermons, Edwards left behind his philosophical speculations on the creation of the cosmos as a grand emanation from the mind of God or “Being in general” and turned to the history of the fall and the need for salvation. To punctuate the primacy of redemption, he passed over creation entirely. He offered no talk of emanation or mind, no treatment of material reality as an idea in the mind of God. He portrayed humankind already on earth and (in Sermon 2 of the Redemption discourse) confronted with the historical fact of the fall. Even before the curse was leveled and Adam and Eve banished from the garden, the angels were informed. Edwards observed, “We must suppose that God's intention of redeeming fallen man was first signified in heaven before it was signified on earth because the business of the angels as the ministering spirits of the mediator required it.”3 So too in other sermons delivered in this period, ontological issues of creation and being were subordinated to the dramatic narrative of the fall, when heaven, earth, and hell intersected with one another and with the orchestrating Trinity. For example, in They Sing a New Song, delivered in November 1740, Edwards reiterated a now-familiar theme: “This work of God is more wonderful and more glorious by far than anything that Adam in innocency had to praise God. Yea, this is the most glorious subject that the angels in heaven themselves have for their songs. They once sang praises to God for the work of creation.… But now, since they have seen the work of redemption, these morning stars sing a new song, and the work of redemption is principally the subject of their praises” (p. 235).

Since redemption lay at the center of actual history, the hinge dates of history were tied not to the rise and fall of the great empires but to the outworking of redemption: in individuals such as Abraham, David, Christ, the Apostles, and Constantine; in movements such as the Reformation and the Puritan migration, and— quite soon, again— in places such as Northampton. In like manner, the triggering mechanism for these turning points in actual history would be not wars and conquests but revivals of true religion.

Throughout the history of fallen mankind, interaction between heaven, hell, and earth never ceased, according to Edwards, although it varied in intensity and immediacy. The form of that interaction he frequently

-- 19 --

likened to a journey or progress. In the artistic manner of Dante, Bunyan, Milton, or, later, C. S. Lewis, Edwards in his history portrayed a dramatic contest between good and evil, with confederations within the Trinity itself playing the triumphant role. At other times, Christ was simply prefigured through images and types of his earthly ministry, such as with Jonah in the whale's belly or David as prophet, priest, and king.4 This redemptive journey narrative would not cease with the close of Scripture. Indeed, all subsequent history was an appendage to these original journeys and carried the same overarching theme: “We ought not to rest in this world and its enjoyments, but should desire heaven.… He that is on a journey, he seeks the place that he is journeying to. Thus, he is not content with the accommodations that he meets with upon the road, to rest in them. We ought above all things to desire a heavenly happiness: to go to heaven, and there to be with God and dwell with Jesus Christ.”5 Like Calvin, Edwards rejected the Roman Catholic theology of purgatory and the medieval “mortalist” portrayals of the souls of deceased saints living a conscious life with Christ before the last judgment. But in the fullness of time, he believed, such conscious union would be gloriously realized.6

Edwards divided his earth history into three major epochs: from the fall of humankind to the incarnation of Christ, the God-man; from the incarnation to the bodily resurrection of Christ; and from the resurrection to the end of time. The briefest yet most important period of interaction between heaven, hell, and earth came during the life, death, and resurrection of Christ. At Christ's birth, the minions of Satan trembled even as the response in heaven was ecstatic: “This appears by their joyful songs on this occasion, heard by the shepherds in the night. This was the greatest event of providence that ever the angels beheld.”7

Following the resurrection, “Christ entered into heaven in order to the obtaining the success of his purchase.… And as he ascended into

-- 20 --

heaven, God the Father did in a visible manner set him on the throne as king of the universe. He then put the angels all under him, and he subjected heaven and earth under him, that he might govern them for the good of the people that he had died for.”8 Throughout his preaching Edwards drew on church and profane history to integrate the post-resurrection history of earth into one divine “conspiracy.” In Like Rain upon Mown Grass, for example, Edwards located the history of the church in its eschatological setting:

So when Christ came remarkably to deliver his church and overthrow their enemies in the time of Constantine the Great, he found it as it were cut down with a most cruel persecution that had been just before.… So when Christ remarkably appeared to revive his church in the time of the Reformation, he found it in a manner destroyed by the overspreading of the corruptions, darkness and persecutions of the church of Rome. So when Christ shall come to the destruction of Antichrist he will find his church in a very low state and as it were cut down by Satan, her grand enemy. So when he shall come at the end of the world… the church shall again be exceedingly reduced (p. 305).

Hell

Of hell, Edwards had no less detail for his history. In contrast to modern avoidance of hell, seventeenth- and eighteenth-century religious culture and art was more attuned to hell than to heaven.9 Although Edwards was more balanced in his preaching, hell was hardly neglected and in fact formed a central component in his redemption narrative. Soon after the world was created, “evil entered into the world in the fall of the angels and man.… Satan rose up against God, endeavoring to frustrate his design in the creation of this lower world.”1 Satan's rebellion would introduce the narrative of hell— a place as real and palpable as heaven and earth. Modern readers miss Edwards entirely if they cannot understand the immediacy of Satan in his thought. Removed or glossed over, Satan, Christ, the Trinity, and heaven and hell become mere abstractions, and the whole scheme of Edwards' history is lost.

-- 21 --

Edwards did not hesitate to introduce young hearers to the horrors of hell at their earliest moments. In Children Ought to Love the Lord Jesus Christ Above All, Edwards spared no detail in his description of the evils of hell and Satan, but then went on to assure his young listeners that they were safe in Christ. “If you love Christ, you will be safe from the devil, that roaring lion that goes about seeking whom he may devour. He will not be able to hurt; you shall be out of his reach. If the devil should appear to you, you need not be afraid of him, but might triumph over him. The devil knows that Christ will subdue him under the feet of such as love him. Christ will bring down that dreadful giant and cause all holy children that love him to come and set their feet upon his neck” (p. 179).

Satan's kingdom was hell, a place without goodness or God; a physicality of pain and suffering where flames licked at sinners with relentless ferocity. Where many medieval writers favored images of hell that played on infinite darkness, Edwards preferred images of furnaces and fire. In a “Miscellanies” entry he wrote, “Hell is represented by fire and brimstone.… Lightning is a stream of brimstone; and if that stream of brimstone which we are told kindles hell be as hot as streams of lightning, it will be vehement beyond conception.”2 What made this infinite stream of fire even more agonizing was the fact that God permitted the damned to view the alternative paradise of the saints in heaven, and vice versa. And, like Calvin's, Edwards' God allowed no second chance after death. Hell was eternal and irreversible. With relentless logic Edwards insisted that because God hates sin “it is suitable that he should execute an infinite punishment.”3

Just as there were types and images of heaven, so also did Edwards discern types of Satan, Antichrist, and hell. In “Miscellanies,” no. 702, Edwards identified the Antichrist with the papacy and went on to equate him with the devil, asserting, “As Christ is the image of God, so is Antichrist the image of the devil.”4 Sometimes outwardly pious church members fell captive to the devil and had to be excommunicated. In The Means and Ends of Excommunication, delivered in 1739 when a wayward communicant was ostracized, Edwards criticized lapsed saints who “are subjects of the visible kingdom of the devil” and thus reap the same punishment as the devil “in a manner agreeable to the condition they are in” (p. 77).

Like heaven's, hell's history was intimately connected to earth's, and

-- 22 --

necessary to engage the drama of redemption. In “Miscellanies,” no. 702, written a couple of years before the excommunication sermon, Edwards noted that “God hath so ordered it that all the great concerns and events of the universe should be some way concerning of this work [of redemption], that the occasion of the fall of some of the angels should be something about this.” Indeed, he continued, redemption was “why the fall of man was so soon permitted.” When the Israelites were enslaved in Egypt, Edwards observed, “Hell was as much and much more engaged [than Egypt]. The pride and cruelty of Satan, that old serpent, was more concerned in it than Pharaoh's.”5 With deliverance, Israel enjoyed a victory as much over hell as over Egypt.

After Egypt, other great but equally profane nations arose. From profane history, Edwards traced four great kingdoms— Babylon, Persia, Greece, and Rome— and found Satan lurking behind each. In the mightiest empire, Rome, “Satan's visible kingdom was raised to its greatest height,” both at the time of Christ and later, with the reign of Antichrist, the papacy.

With Christ's incarnation, the battle became intensely personal and earth-centered. Soon after Christ began his public ministry, he was led by the devil into the wilderness. There, “Satan made a violent onset upon him in his first entrance on his work, and now he had a remarkable trial of his obedience.” But in the end Christ “got the victory.” The resurrection marked the death knell of Satan: “Satan who lately was so ready to triumph and insult, as though he had got the victory in killing Christ, now finding himself fallen into the pit that he had digged, and finding his kingdom falling so fast, and seeing Christ's kingdom make such amazing progress such as never had been before, we may conclude he was filled with the greatest confusion and astonishment, and hell seemed to be effectually alarmed by it to make the most violent opposition against it.”6 When earth's history, at last, came to an end in the final climactic battles with Antichrist and Satan, all three worlds would come together, with heaven especially enlisted for the conflict: “At the day of judgment, the Sun of righteousness shall appear in its greatest glory; Christ shall then come in the glory of the Father, and all the holy angels with him.” In his sermon Christ the Spiritual Sun, Edwards cautioned that the destruction that accompanied this day would exceed the persecutions of the Jews at Christ's death. Soon after the resurrection, he declared, Christ “brought

-- 23 --

the amazing destruction of the unbelieving Jews, terribly destroying their city and country by the Romans. So when he will come in a spiritual sense at the beginning of the expected glorious times of the church, he will come [not only] for the deliverance and healing and rejoicing of his church, but for the amazing destruction of Antichrist and other enemies of his church” (p. 61).

With the toppling of Antichrist, Satan's earthly kingdoms would be utterly abolished. Satan would be banished from earth as he was from heaven. “And now the devil… shall receive his full punishment.… This world that formerly used to be the place of his kingdom, where he set up himself as God, shall now be the place of his complete punishment, and full and everlasting torment.” In Christ the Spiritual Sun Edwards further explained that “the world shall literally be a fire and shall be all like an oven, filled with the flames of the fiercest and most glowing heat” (p. 62).

There would be “new heavens” no less than a “new earth.” Indeed, from “Miscellanies” entries it is clear that Edwards believed that these places would be located strictly in heaven: “The new heavens and new earth, so far as a place of habitation is meant by 'em, are heaven and not this lower world.” Whereas the old heaven was corruptible, as evidenced by Satan's fall, the new heaven would be pure and incorruptible, a paradise without end. With Christ's second coming, the saints already in heaven and those joining them would see glories that were previously unavailable even in heaven. Human minds would see universes inside an atom: “'Tis only for want of sufficient accurateness, strength and comprehension of mind, that from the motion of any one particular atom we can't tell all that ever has been, [all] that now is in the whole extent of creation…, and everything that ever shall be.” In a corollary to this point, Edwards concluded, “What room for improvement of reason is there [in heaven], for angels and glorified minds!”7

Revivals as Millennial Harbingers

In the search for signs of the triumph of Christ the Redeemer, revivals assumed central importance. They were simultaneously tangible events, prophetic signs, and portents of coming triumphs. Recalling his immediate past, Edwards observed that the instrument God employed most directly for redemption was revival. “God's Spirit,” he ruminated, “shall be gloriously poured out for the wonderful revival and propagation of religion.

-- 24 --

This great work shall be accomplished, not by the authority of princes, nor by the wisdom of learned men, but by God's Holy Spirit.”8 Far from being centered in America, Edwards pointed out, light would pour on all the dark parts of the world as the course of revival progressed: “Great knowledge shall prevail everywhere. It may be hoped that then many of the Negroes and Indians will be divines, and that excellent books will be published in Africa, in Ethiopia, in Turkey— and not only very learned men, but others that are more ordinary men, shall then be very knowing in religion.… It shall be a time of great holiness; now vital religion shall everywhere prevail and reign.”9

Even as he read voraciously in the history of heaven, earth, and hell, and sketched their interconnected narratives, Edwards eagerly scanned the horizons of his own world for signs of the revival and regeneration that would presage the new heavens and the new earth. In a letter to Josiah Willard, a widely connected evangelical and secretary of the Massachusetts Province, Edwards inquired after the state of revivals worldwide. Familiar with Whitefield and the British Isles, he extended his inquiry to Prussia, in particular to the city of Halle, where a Dr. August Hermann Francke was rumored to have promoted revivals. He also expressed curiosity about places as far away as the East Indies and Moscovy, adding, “I cannot but hope that God is about to accomplish glorious things for his church, which makes me the more desirous of knowing as fully as may be the present state of religion in the world.”1

From his reading and correspondence, Edwards was able to apprise his congregation of recent events and set them in historical context. At a private meeting (probably held at a parishioner's house) in December 1739, Edwards reported on this work in God's Grace Carried On in Other Places. In particular, he singled out “a remarkable work of God's grace that has of late appeared in some parts of the British denominations.” Even more surprising was the fact that this movement grew out of the Church of England, which, though “sound in its principles in the beginning of the Reformation,” had fallen prey to heresy and corruption “especially during King Charles the Second's reign.” With mounting excitement, recalling Northampton's great revival of 1734, he informed his listeners that “God has raised up in England a number of young ministers” who were “called, by way of derision, the New Methodists” (p. 108). Soon this British revival would land on colonial shores with hurricane force.

Edwards and George Whitefield

Had history in the practical sense not intervened, it is likely that Edwards would have moved forward from sermon series to formal treatise on the history of redemption, as he did later with his famous Treatise on Religious Affections. Indeed, such a treatise would have been even more comprehensive than Religious Affections or, for that matter, Freedom of the Will or Original Sin. It would have contained all of these and more, the grandeur of which he later described in the letter to the Trustees. But in fact, nothing more was done. Something happened that wrenched Edwards from his speculations on the past, which dominated his thinking in 1739, and immersed him in the present of 1740— and of Northampton.

By late 1739, word of George Whitefield's stunning oratory and mass revivals had everywhere spread through print and word of mouth.2 News of unprecedented crowds numbering in the tens of thousands seemed almost unbelievable to colonists accustomed to local oratory. Part of their surprise was the sheer size of Whitefield's audiences while preaching in the fields, but part also was astonishment that revivals were taking place in Europe, where piety had supposedly died. For the first time, newspapers in England and the colonies discovered a popular audience for the subject of religion.

Everyone sought information on the charismatic preacher-celebrity. Whitefield's “general revival” had no precedent in the Anglo-American world: it encompassed all denominations, races, and even languages. German-speaking colonists in Pennsylvania marveled no less than slaves in Philadelphia and Puritans in New England. At each stop, colonial newspapers up and down the coast strained to provide news of the “Grand Itinerant's” journeys and triumphs. For his part, Whitefield was more than happy to accommodate his readers with advance notices and elaborate descriptions of his triumphs.

Two months after the last sermon in the History of Redemption series had been delivered, Whitefield arrived in New England. Whatever secret envy or criticism Edwards might have felt toward Whitefield was not expressed.3 Like it or not, Edwards was certain that he and Whitefield were providentially paired. Edwards desperately wanted Whitefield to visit Northampton, so that he could use the visit to prod his congregation into

-- 26 --

living up to their international reputation; and he wanted to give Whitefield a tale to carry with him to his extensive transatlantic contacts. As well, such a visit would point gloriously to the very culmination of redemption he had been thinking about.

The story of George Whitefield has long been a chestnut in popular histories of evangelicalism, faithfully recounted by revivalists in every generation. His prodigious revivals on both sides of the ocean were the first truly mass religious phenomenon in Anglo-American religious history and provided a symbol of piety for generations to come. But outside evangelical circles, Whitefield remained largely unknown, even by historians. Only recently has the story of Whitefield's revivals achieved the centrality it deserves in histories of American Protestantism and Anglo-American culture generally.4 For Edwards scholars, this recovery is of particular importance, for they have long been among Whitefield's chief detractors. Perry Miller, Edwards' greatest biographer, led the campaign of degradation designed to glorify Edwards at the expense of Whitefield. Likening Whitefield to a “pied piper” of religious quacks and charlatans, Miller asserted that “a more repulsive individual never influenced history.” His “reckless and irresponsible whining” stood in sharp contrast to the meditations of the Sage of Northampton. Indeed, Miller concluded, “[W]ithin the fold of revivalism, there are no personalities more uncongenial than Whitefield and Edwards.”5

However brilliant Miller's intellectual biography of Edwards, his depiction of Whitefield shows a remarkable lack of perspicacity that is unfortunate on two counts. First, it deprives scholars of signal insights into the origins of transatlantic evangelicalism and mass media; and second, it distorts our understanding of Edwards, especially in the period covered by this volume. Although it is true that Edwards and Whitefield never became close friends, and Edwards may have suffered some envy over the young Englishman's success, the fact remains that the two were intimately congenial copromoters of a “great awakening” that neither could have achieved alone. No one was more important to Edwards' hope of renewed revival in Northampton than Whitefield, and, reciprocally, no one was more important to Whitefield's legitimacy than Edwards. Having read Edwards' Faithful Narrative, Whitefield believed that the great crowds he attracted in England were suffused with the same divine inspiration that informed the revival in western Massachusetts. Through print as well as preaching, he linked his ministry with the Northampton “great awakening.” He made a clear connection by echoing the title of Edwards' revival account in that of his autobiography, published in 1739 as A Faithful Narrative of the Life and Character of the Reverend Mr. Whitefield. The subtitle provided another tie between the Work of God in England and that in America as it announced Whitefield's intention to travel to Georgia later in the year.6

Given the great chasm that Miller and others posited between Edwards' prodigious intellect and Whitefield's supposed simplicity, it is revealing that Edwards' initial interest in Whitefield was as much intellectual as rhetorical or emotional. In his “Catalogue” of reading, Edwards cites books such as William Law's A Practical Treatise upon Christian Perfection as “highly commended by Mr. Whitefield… in his 3rd Journal, p. 3.” To his entry on John Wesley's Hymns and Sacred Poems, Edwards appended, “see advertisement at the end of Mr. Whitefield's sermon.”7 In fact, Edwards was finely attuned to the transatlantic evangelical world, combing titles from “Mr. Tennant,” “Dr. Herman Frank,” “Ebenezer Erskine,” “Isaac Watts,” and “the Wesleys.”

Whitefield in Northampton

For Edwards, Whitefield's revivals could not have been better timed. Given Edwards' preoccupations with the transcendent history of redemption, news of Whitefield's revivals must have struck him with volcanic force. The great question in Edwards' mind was whether Northampton would take the lead or continue in its apathetic ways. Edwards stood near the front in the line of clergy inviting Whitefield to their pulpits. Well aware from Whitefield's Journal that the famed revivalist knew intimately of him, he warned Whitefield in his letter of invitation not to expect too much in the present day:

I am fearful whether you will not be disappointed in New England, and will have less success here than in other places: we who have dwelt in a land that has been distinguished with light, and have long enjoyed

-- 28 --

the gospel, and have been glutted with it, and have despised it, are I fear more hardened than most of those places where you have preached hitherto. But yet I hope in that power and mercy of God that has appeared so triumphant in the success of your labors in other places, that he will send a blessing with you even to us, though we are unworthy of it. I hope, if God preserves my life, to see something of that salvation of God in New England which he has now begun, in a benighted, wicked, and miserable world and age and in the most guilty of all nations.8

Whitefield accepted the invitation.

Throughout the months of December 1739 and January 1740, Edwards prepared his congregation to take their place in this great awakening. The first explicit reference to Whitefield in the surviving Edwards corpus appears in the sermon God's Grace Carried On in Other Places. Here Edwards turned to history. He began by tracing the fall from grace of the Church of England when “conversion work in a great measure ceased.” From there he described the emergence of the New Methodists, including the Wesley brothers, Charles and John, and above all Whitefield, “a young man who is a minister of the Church of England, who labors obediently and insists very much on the doctrines of faith in Christ and the New Birth.” The conversion events that followed, declared Edwards, were sent by God for the people of Northampton. In terms calculated to challenge, he observed, “It is not many years ago since God wonderfully appeared for us and the fame of the great work of God here was heard in different places, in the neighboring colonies and in England.… And therefore we shall especially be inexcusable if the tidings we hear don't stir up in us earnest desires after such a mercy.… How ill will it appear if we are not affected in our turn, and stirred up by what we hear of God's work in other places?” (p. 109).

Even while urging his congregation to pick up the challenge and play a leadership role in the international revivals, Edwards experienced grave inner doubts. On the eve of Whitefield's visit to Northampton, he penned a letter to the young evangelist Eleazar Wheelock of Lebanon Crank, Connecticut. The news was not good: “It is a sorrowfully dull and dead time with us. The temporal affairs of this town are, and have been for some years, most unhappily situated to be a snare to us, and I know not where to look for help but to God.” Then followed a plea, both personal and corporate,

-- 29 --

that God “would bless Mr. Whitefield's coming here for good to my soul, and the souls of my people.”9

Edwards' prayers would be answered. On October 17, Whitefield arrived in Northampton and preached in the meetinghouse and later that evening in the parsonage. The next two days he preached three more times. In published accounts, the two ministers agreed that something dramatic had happened. Whitefield reported great movings, including “Mr. Edwards,” who “wept during the whole time of the exercise.” In his turn, Edwards wrote that “the congregation was extraordinarily melted by every sermon; almost the whole assembly being in tears for a great part of sermon time.”1

Northampton After Whitefield

Following Whitefield's visit, Edwards sought to fan the flames by preaching a sermon series on the parable of the sower, enjoining his listeners to be planted in the Word. Although pleased that a revival spirit seemed to be thriving, Edwards was careful not to gloat. Recalling the earlier revival, he asked, “Was there too much of an appearance of a public pride, if I may so call it? Were we not lifted up with the honor that God had put upon us as a people, beyond most other people?” (p. 255). He was determined this time to learn from history in order to avoid repeating it.

In December, Edwards wrote to Whitefield describing the state of religion in Northampton as having “been gradually reviving and prevailing more and more, ever since you was here.” He noted of special importance “a considerable number of our young people, some of them children, having already been savingly brought home to Christ.” Among these young converts were “one, if not more, of my children.” In a later account, first written as a letter to Boston's Thomas Prince and later published in Prince's magazine Christian History, Edwards happily reported that “in about a month or six weeks there was a great alteration in the town, both as to the revival of professors, and awakening of others. By the middle of December a very considerable work of God appeared among those that were very young, and the revival of religion continued to increase; so that

-- 30 --

in the spring, an engagedness of spirit about things of religion was become very general amongst young people and children.”2

Edwards had good reason to focus on the young people. From a demographic analysis of Northampton church membership lists, Kenneth P. Minkema has shown that a significant number of the recalcitrant members who criticized Edwards and eventually dismissed him were older members who had entered the church under the ministry of his grandfather and predecessor, Solomon Stoddard. Many of these members resented Edwards' fame at the expense of their beloved Mr. Stoddard. But Edwards was not innocent: in retaliation he berated the aged as too old for conversion and held the young up as role models of faith.3

The young were Edwards' best hope and the members he especially cultivated for conversion. Signs of young people and even children converting appeared throughout his reports as a special providence. Following the drowning of a young man named Billy Sheldon in February 1741, Edwards pressed home the fragility of youth in Youth Is Like a Flower That Is Cut Down. The lesson he enjoined was familiar: “Don't set your heart on youthful pleasures and other vain enjoyments of this world.” Instead, prepare for eternity. Even as he spoke, Edwards warned, another youth was dying of consumption and “on the brink of eternity.… Therefore lose no more time” (p. 328).

In The Danger of Corrupt Communication Among Young People, delivered in July 1740, Edwards had directed his young listeners' attention to a different world, but this time to heaven rather than hell. He was particularly concerned about the consequences of conversion in respect to youthful conversation. In place of mundane or vulgar conversation he enjoined young listeners to move their thoughts and communications toward “the great things of another world.” Such subject matter would be infinitely rewarding and encouraging in contrast to the sorrows of life in this world. Young people everywhere, but especially in Northampton, “should be much in speaking of the saints' happiness and glory in heaven, where they will be perfectly holy and happy in the full enjoyment of God and Christ, and in perfect love and friendship one with another, forever and ever” (p. 163). Ironically, and unknown to Edwards until several years later, at this very time several young men were reading and distributing illustrated midwivery manuals, which they were using to harass young women.

At about the same time that Edwards wrote to Whitefield in December, he penned his “Personal Narrative.” In reading it, one sees how the revivals buoyed his sagging spirits and uplifted his soul. He recalled his earlier yearnings with startling immediacy. He now recalled how “my mind was much taken up with contemplations on heaven, and the enjoyments of those there; and living there in perfect holiness, humility, and love.”4 How much of this description pertained to Edwards' youthful experience and how much to his renewed hope in 1739 is unclear, but certainly these words expressed his present state.

Edwards the Awakener

Edwards was obsessed with Whitefield, and in his own way he began to imitate him. By December 1740 unmistakable evidence appears in Edwards' manuscript sermons that he had begun to experiment with and perfect his own revival rhetoric in Whitefieldian directions. If Edwards' greatest growth as a preacher occurred earlier, in the 1730s, his growth as a revivalist specializing in the New Birth came now. His Sermon Notebook “45, ” begun in 1739, is the largest of three extant and marks a shift in style that reflects both the effect of Whitefield's revivals and Edwards' own turbulent relationship with his congregation.5 For his part, Edwards increasingly outlined his sermons in order to achieve the appearance of Whitefield's extemporaneity.

Edwards knew he was no orator. His voice, gestures, and memory could not equal Whitefield's dramaturgical style. But he also knew that he had one advantage, which he intended to exploit for his own glory and the glory of God: rhetoric. Edwards was a genius with words, and he set himself to compose the “perfect idea” of an awakening sermon. To aid in that process, he would modify not only his delivery and gestures but the balance of the ideas and the very structure of the composition in ways that are easily uncovered.

Besides altering the form of his manuscript notes, Edwards shifted his content decisively from heaven to hell. Whereas the history of heaven communicated love, the essence of “the new sense of the heart,” Edwards believed, for a moment anyway, that one could get to life eternal only after first being scared to death.6 This rhetorical need demanded a change

-- 32 --

in emphasis from the history of heaven to the history of hell. In earlier years, sermonic invocations of heaven had easily surpassed those of hell. But in the 1740s, Edwards realized that hell, with all its fury and torture, would have to be enlisted if heaven were to be gained. Edwards knew that the indispensable emotional appeal in an awakening sermon was fear, even terror, and it knew no age limits. Children needed to absorb horror no less than adults. Just as children must be taught to fear fire, so also must they be taught to fear the fires of hell. In a defense of revival preaching, Edwards observed: “'Tis no argument that a work is not from the Spirit of God, that it seems to be promoted by ministers insisting very much on the terrors of God's holy law, and that with a great deal of pathos and earnestness.”7

Edwards' first deliberate attempt to shape a new awakening sermon appeared in Sinners in Zion, first preached in December 1740. Here hell and fear quickly gain the upper hand over beauty and consent. Early in the sermon Edwards signaled his intent to create a “perfect idea of fright.” To this end he selected a perfect text, Isaiah 33:14, “The sinners in Zion are afraid; fearfulness hath surprised the hypocrites. Who among us shall dwell with the devouring fire? Who among us shall dwell with everlasting burnings?” Edwards knew this text well. Ten years earlier, in the fall of 1730, he had preached a sermon in which, midway through the text, he quoted from Isaiah 33:14 to focus his listeners on fear and hell: “God makes [sinners] sensible that his displeasure is very dreadful. Before, they heard often about the anger of God and the fierceness of his wrath but they were not moved by it; but now they are made sensible that it is a dreadful thing to fall into the hands of the living God. They are made in some measure sensible of the dreadfulness of hell. They are led with fixedness and impression to think what a dismal thing it will be to have God an enraged enemy, setting himself to work the misery of a soul, and how dismal it will be to dwell in such torment forever without hope; Isaiah 33:14, ‘The sinners in Zion are afraid…’”8

Sinners in Zion returned to this theme with elaborate amplification. From start to finish, the goal of the sermon was fear. It is long, encompassing two preaching units, and was often repeated at later dates. The focus of the original text— and of Edwards' sermon— was less on pagans and infidels than on sinners among the people of God in Zion. These were the worst of sinners, for they ignored the light that was hidden from others;

-- 33 --

their fate would be horrific. They would inhabit hell, a place so terrifying that to describe it strained the capacity of language. But not the capacity of Edwards, who exploited language as few before him had done. To approximate the world of hell, he used all the images and metaphors he could invent. Chief of these was the fiery furnace. “Now if a person should be brought to the mouth of such a furnace, and there should see how the fire seemed to glow so as presently to make everything that was cast into it all over white and bright with fire, and should know that he was now directly going to be cast into this furnace, would not fearfulness surprise him?” Indeed it would. But Edwards waited until the very end of the sermon to identify exactly who was poised on the edge of that furnace. With mounting fury he came to the concluding Application, looked out on his congregation, and declared, “You are the sinners in Zion!” That pit, he declared, was prepared for the people sitting before him. Hence the invitation: “Will you not now thoroughly awake out of sleep and bestir yourself for your salvation?” (p. 284).

In examining the composition of Sinners in Zion, one sees that Edwards was already at work perfecting the style for a Whitefield-like revival sermon. The form would have to promote and encourage extemporaneous delivery, and the content must shift from heaven to hell, from the joys of redemption to the horrors of the damned. In a further step toward freeing up his speech, Edwards began to include large white space breaks in his notes as a signal to extemporize. In place of fully written-out sentences, he began supplying simple but potent rhetorical cues: “You are warned by it”; “You are invited by it.”

A Second Sinners

About half a year after preaching Sinners in Zion, Edwards preached another sermon on sinners: Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God. It is not clear what effect this Sinners had on his own congregation. Probably not much. They, after all, had heard its substance only two weeks before. Awakening sermons often require unfamiliar audiences and spontaneous delivery. Certainly no reports exist of exceptional responses. But the next time he preached it, at Enfield, Massachusetts, on July 8, the effects were extraordinary.

Enfield had gained a reputation as a stubbornly unawakened town, seemingly proud in its ability to resist the Spirit. So when Edwards was called in, a number of ministers came to see whether he could melt the congregations' hard hearts. Eleazar Wheelock was one witness to the

-- 34 --

showdown. He reported that before Edwards started his address, “the people hardly conducted themselves with common decency.” But that changed very quickly. “Before the sermon was ended,” Wheelock recalled, “the assembly appeared deeply impressed and bowed down, with an awful conviction of their sin and danger.”9 The Reverend Stephen Williams of nearby Longmeadow also attended and recorded the event in his diary with striking immediacy:

went over to Enfd - where we met Dear Mr E - of N. H. - who preachd a most awakening Sermon from those words Deut - 32.35 - and before ye Sermon was done there was a great moaning - & crying out throught ye whole House - what shall I do to be Savd - oh I am going to Hell -. oh what shall I do for a christ &c &c - So yt ye minister - was obligd to desist - shreiks & crys - were piercing & Amazing - after Some time of waiting - the congregation were still So yt a prayr was made by Mr W - & after that we descendd from the pulpitt and discoursd - with the people - Some in one place & Some in another - and Amazing & Astonishing - ye powr - God was Seen - &c Severall Souls were hopfully - wrought upon yt - night & oh ye cheerfullness & pleasntness of thier countenances - yt receivd comfort - oh yt God - wd strengthen & confirm &c - we Sung an hymn & prayd &c despersd - ye Assembly.

Such was the power— and the fear— generated by this sermon that Edwards apparently never finished preaching it— possibly the only time such an interruption had happened to him except for the day several years earlier when the gallery of the old Northampton meetinghouse fell. Reflecting on the sermon, Williams wrote, “I Seemd affectd & movd-ready to dissolve in Tears &c-but cant well tell why.”1

Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God is arguably America's greatest sermon. It has been analyzed extensively both in this Edition and in other works.2 But what is interesting for our purposes here is not the final text

-- 35 --

as it appears in print, but the handwritten notes, reproduced in this volume for the first time. At the end of these handwritten notes is a brief, two-page outline of the sermon that Edwards prepared, probably for multiple deliveries with minimal notes or prompts.3 When reading the fragment, as opposed to the fully written-out text, one can discern the almost “perfect idea” of an awakening sermon:

1. [Sinners] always exposed to fall.

2. Suddenly fall.

3. By their own weight.

4. Nothing but God that holds 'em up.

(1) No want of power in G.

(2) They deserve it.

(3) They are condemned to it. 'Tis the place they belong to.

(4) God is angry enough with them.

(5) The devil if not restrained would immediately fly upon them and seize them as his own.

(6) They have those hellish principles in them, that if God should take off his restraints.

(7) 'Tis no security that there are no visible means of death at hand.

(8) Their own care and prudence to preserve their own lives.

(9) The schemes they lay out for escaping damnation.

(10) There is no promise.

For all the attention paid to Sinners, virtually no one has noticed this highly condensed version. It confirms the novelty of the sermon, not only on the level of content and rhetoric in print, but of its extemporaneous abbreviation. Assuming that Edwards delivered this sermon on more than two occasions, we can see this two-page fragment text as the real sermon: the highly portable and powerful cue card that allowed multiple deliveries and unprecedented terror. Contained in these two pages was rhetorical dynamite.

Students familiar with Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God can read the outline and fill in the blanks as easily as Edwards. All balance is gone; speculative knowledge is peripheral; history is to be made, not understood. Awakening sermons aim to awaken, pure and simple. Only two emotions pertain, fear and rage: fear from the human perspective at the precariousness

-- 37 --

of the human condition and the horrors of the devil and hell; rage from the divine vantage point. Edwards is perhaps most terrifying, indeed almost out of control, when speaking for God. God, in Edwards' angry hands, can hardly contain his loathing, anger, and hatred of sinners. “Yea, God is a great deal more angry with great numbers that are now on earth, yea, doubtless with many that are now in this congregation, that it may be are at ease and quiet, than he is with many of those who are now in the flames of hell.… The wrath of God burns against them, their damnation don't slumber, the pit is prepared, the fire is made ready, the furnace is now hot, ready to receive them, the flames do now rage and glow. The glittering sword is whet, and held over them, and the pit hath opened her mouth under them” (p. 406). In this selection and many others, it is grammatically unclear who prepares the pit of unimaginable horror. Is it the devil, as Edwards would have claimed in his more reasoned moments, or God himself? The very characteristics of God and Satan blend into one supernatural fury directed at unrepentant sinners seated— or groveling on the floor— before the preacher.

Edwards and the Millennium

To keep his congregation focused on the need to preserve the revival spirit, Edwards returned to the themes of history and the worlds of heaven and hell. In Seasons of Ingathering, Edwards effectively employed the image of Christ and the angels coming from heaven armed with sickles to separate the saved from the damned. The damned he represented as grapes about to be harvested. The angels would gather the damned and throw them into “the great winepress of God's wrath.” The use of the winepress, Edwards explained, “is to glorify [God's] wrath.… [Hell is] a place every way fitted for the purpose. Everything in it [is] contrived and fitted for that, as all the parts of a winepress are prepared for the making of wine from the grape. 'Tis the grand receptacle of all the vessels of wrath. God has prepared but one place of torment for all the whole universe that {remain impenitent}. There the devils [will torment them].” Lest his hearers not get the point, Edwards applied his text with a warning that hearkened back to earlier days: “God now seems to be withdrawing from many parts of the land. If God should withdraw from us, great calamity will surely come upon the souls of those that are left behind. I warned you before, in the last time of {awakening}, and my words are verified” (p. 488).

Reports of raw emotional excesses and lay exhorting in the revivals circulated rapidly after 1741, and, at first, Edwards sought to minimize them. In a sermon on December 21, The Curse of Meroz, he used the battle

-- 38 --

of Deborah and Barak with the Canaanites as a type of the time “when that great battle between Christ and his enemies” would occur. With Armageddon in the balance, he pleaded with his congregation not to judge the revivals prematurely by their side effects. In words that anticipated his treatises on Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival and Religious Affections, he urged that the revivals not be judged by heightened bodily agitations. “'Tis no wonder at all that some are carried something beyond themselves.… There are undoubtedly imprudences and errors that attend the work of God, and always will be.… But must we therefore always harp upon them?” (p. 507).

If Edwards was captivated by history, it was not the history of America. Earlier accounts that seek to identify Edwards with an American (i.e. New England) exceptionalism miss the degree to which New England and America as discrete entities hardly figured in his earth narrative. For a brief moment in the height of the revivals, he turned his attention homeward to America as the possible seat of God's new heavens and new earth at the end of history. But this proved to be a passing speculation. Not only was America too small for his history, but earth itself had to take its place in a larger cosmos.

Related to exceptionalism is typology. Much has been made of Edwards' fascination with this exegetical discipline. Natural tropes as types elicited his most lyrical prose, for they fused the worlds of heaven and hell, creation and redemption. But the use of typology for nationalistic reasons, common with many Puritans before him, is not present. In none of his speculations did Edwards identify America or New England as the New Israel, the antitype for which ancient Israel was the type. And, except for the brief speculation in 1741, in no sense was New England the “vanguard of universal history.”4 For Edwards, God's New Israel was the universal and invisible body of Christ— believers everywhere united in a “concert of prayer” for God's triumphant reign.

In the heat of Whitefield's tour, Edwards dared to hope (“posit” would be too strong a term) that “what is now seen in America, and especially in New England, may prove the dawn of that glorious day: and the very uncommon and wonderful circumstances and events of this work, seem to me strongly to argue that God intends it as the beginning or forerunner

-- 39 --

of something vastly great.”5 Except for this brief American-centered enthusiasm, Edwards always understood the millennium and the church in universal terms. However optimistic about “God's mighty power” at work, the vast majority of Edwards' pronouncements on the subject of the millennium preclude claims of an American exceptionalism. For instance, the April 1741 sermon Importunate Prayer for Millennial Glory was universal in its orientation. From revivals at home and abroad, Edwards proclaimed that “now God seems in his providence, in an especial manner, to be calling us to be ready for those [end] times.” But where were they to commence? The answer was, everywhere. From letters and magazines Edwards could document “so remarkable a pouring out of the Spirit of God in many places and the commotion the nations of the world seem to be in” (p. 372). Northampton believers should not be deluded into thinking they were the center of God's plans. They remained too ignorant of the “state of God's church in general.”

Occasional Sermons

Of course, revivals were not the only activities in this period. Part of every colonial preacher's life was the need to devise occasional sermons, and these continued month in and month out. Occasional sermons have only recently been understood for their unique place in the community, encompassing a variety of events from ordinations to funerals to fasts and thanksgivings. Fasts and thanksgivings represented especially important weekday occasions when ministers could address themselves to temporal and secular affairs. Of these, none were more pressing than war. In time, revival enthusiasm would wane and energies turn to war.

Edwards was a patriot as much as a revivalist, and in his military preaching one finds many themes common to all Puritan ministers, with one important distinction. To an uncommon extent he introduced angels into his occasional sermons no less than his regular preaching. In God's Care for His Servants in Time of Public Commotions, a fast-day sermon “for success in war, Feb. 26, 1741,” Edwards selected an apocalyptic text from Revelation 7:1-03 that featured four angels standing on the four corners of the earth. These angels, Edwards stated, serve under “command sent from heaven,” and God “makes use of the ministration of angels in regulating those events.” Again Edwards rehearsed profane history when, in the days after

-- 40 --

Constantine, the angels “had commission to hurt the earth and the sea.” To supply hope, Edwards employs the prophecy in Revelation to assure his listeners that God will not allow great destruction until his “seal” is on the foreheads of all his saints. Mixing revival with war, Edwards was pleased to report: “We see that the angel that has the seal of the living God is very busy: his sealing work goes on in these American plantations; many are sealed daily in one place and another.… So we hear it has been in Pennsylvania, and in Carolina and Georgia, and in the Jerseys and New York, and in Boston.… God is taking care that the elect may not be hurt” (p. 359). Two months later Edwards delivered another fast-day sermon entitled Importunate Prayer for Millennial Glory. True to form, Edwards' focus here was not New England or America, but “the world.”

Second Thoughts

In June 1741, Edwards approved limited lay exhorting in a letter to Deborah Hatheway, an eighteen-year-old convert from Suffield who had no pastor to counsel her. In his advice, Edwards allowed lay exhortation on a private level and, in some circumstances, to larger circles: “I would advise you especially, to be much in exhorting children and young women your equals; and when you exhort others that are men, I would advise that you take opportunities for it, chiefly when you are alone with them, or when only young persons are present.” In a later letter to Deacon Moses Lyman, formerly of Northampton, Edwards conceded that while the revivals carried “some imprudences and irregularities,” yet “as to the work in general, they have all the clear and incontestable evidences of a true divine work.” As for the great “outcry against ministers riding about to preach the gospel,” such practices were neither novel nor wrong. In fact, most traveling clergy that he was aware of “scarcely ever are absent from their people on the sabbath.”6

Later that year Edwards appeared less sanguine about itinerants, whether lay or clerical. With spring thaw, a handful of ordained and lay itinerants had begun crisscrossing the region. The most famous was Gilbert Tennent. Settled ministers, even friends of the revivals like Edwards, were nervous about the direction revivals seemed to be taking. Increasingly, physical outworkings, accompanied by visions and trances, predominated. Many lay people began to identify these bodily effects as signs of regeneration.

-- 41 --

The most sensational reports were of the young James Davenport, scion of a noble ministerial family and the youngest graduate in the history of Yale College. By 1741 his campaign for “saving” Connecticut moved into high gear. Claiming special powers of discernment, he routinely accosted settled clergy and labeled them “unconverted”— a term that was first widely applied to New England clergy by Whitefield. Davenport's campaign began in New London and moved from there up and down the Connecticut shoreline. In August his travels brought him to New Haven and to Yale, where he proceeded to harangue the minister Joseph Noyes as “an unconverted hypocrite and the devil incarnate.”7

The week following Davenport's denunciation of Noyes— and Yale— Edwards was scheduled to preach the commencement sermon to the faculty and students of Yale College. In circumstances as grave and portentous as his famous Boston lecture of 1731, God Glorified in the Work of Redemption, Edwards turned his attention entirely to the history-making present. He titled his sermon The Distinguishing Marks of a Work of the Spirit of God. The sermon echoed his earlier Northampton sermons The Curse of Meroz and Keeping the Presence of God, balancing warnings with an overall assessment of the revivals as a work of God. But this time, he spent considerably more time on the errors of the revival and the false signs that a seeming work of God was legitimate. Knowing the mood of Yale's faculty, he devoted long sections to “negative signs” of true revival, many of which had occurred in the immediate experience of his listeners. For the first time, he introduced the devil at some length, noting how Satan could mimic and induce many of the same signs of grace that true Christians sought as evidence of their justification. Bodily agitations, lively imaginations, and excessive fright— all were negative signs of a work of God.

Yet, when Edwards had laid the sum of the distinguishing marks in the balance, he backed away from his assault on the negative signs and pronounced the revivals in New England a work of God. How did he know? Because of the “positive signs” everywhere that could not be imitated by the devil. These included a heightened esteem for the risen Christ, a discernible change in way of life, a love of holy Scripture, a thirsting after doctrine and speculative knowledge of God's dealings with creation, and, most important, a new sense of love for God and humankind. Knowing his particular audience, as well as his own mind, Edwards laid particular emphasis on the need for “human learning” in the salvation process: “'Tis

-- 42 --

too manifest to be denied, that God made great use of human learning in the apostle Paul, as he also did in Moses and Solomon.” With positive signs factored in, Edwards could only conclude “that that extraordinary influence that has lately appeared on the minds of the people abroad in this land… is undoubtedly, in the general, from the Spirit of God.”8

Yale's faculty were not persuaded. Having just been burned, first by Whitefield and then by their own Davenport, they could not share their former colleague's bright assessment of the state of religion in the land. The fervor remained too censorious and confrontational for it to be a work of God. Even their own students were a case in point of the religious climate's negative effects, and none more than the Yale College Class of 1741. Included in this group were Samuel Hopkins, destined to be Edwards' most prolific disciple; Samuel Buell, a soon-to-be evangelist par excellence in Northampton, of all places; and David Brainerd, who was expelled during his sophomore year after proclaiming that Chauncey Whittelsey, tutor in the college, had “no more grace than a chair.”

But beyond Yale, Edwards' commencement address proved to be a rhetorical triumph and was immediately forwarded to the press by the friends of the revivals in Boston and London. Partly because of their response to Whitefield, but even more in response to his native imitators, the clergy of New England had divided widely over whether the revivals were a work of God.9 Many New Light friends of the revivals needed a moderate champion like Edwards, who was a strenuous advocate of revival but also firmly anchored in the Standing Order and ministerial elite. In the past, Edwards had sided with the elites against popular radicals. Throughout the 1730s, Mark Valeri observes, “Edwards focused his critique against popular discontent and sanctioned the social values of stability and deference, as a defense of, inter alia, his own social location. He himself was a member of the town's elite by blood, education, profession, and temperament.”1 Edwards would do so again in the 1740s.

By January 1742, Edwards was worrying anew that Northampton was lagging. In a letter to his colleague Joseph Bellamy, he confessed that “religion in this and the neighboring towns has now of late been on the decaying hand.” Still, there was a bright spot: the youth continued to flourish. “There has been the year past the most wonderful work amongst

-- 43 --

children here, by far, that ever was: God has seemed almost wholly to take a new generation, that are come on since the late great work seven years ago.”2

At the same time that apathy and contention were on the rise in Northampton, religious enthusiasm was reaching unprecedented heights in the aftermath of the activities of James Davenport and his associates. A concerned Edwards began to back off his encouragement of itinerancy— especially lay itinerancy. In part the choice was taken out of his hands when Connecticut passed legislation outlawing the practice. But beyond that, his own misgivings grew. In a second letter to Moses Lyman, in May 1742, he shared his reservations, especially over lay exhorting, no matter how sincerely grounded: “I hope you will consider the matter, and for the future avoid doing thus. You ought to do what you can by private, brotherly, humble admonitions and counsels; but 'tis too much for you to exhort public congregations, or solemnly to set yourself by set speech to counsel a room full of people, unless it be children.… Such things have done a vast mischief in the country.”3

In February 1742, a downhearted Edwards left for a two-week preaching tour around Leicester, Massachusetts. In his absence he appointed the recently graduated Samuel Buell to fill the Northampton pulpit. To Edwards' simultaneous joy and dismay, Buell proceeded to set the congregation on fire in a way Edwards had done in Enfield but not in his own Northampton. People cried out in meeting, and, as Edwards later described it, “the whole town seemed to be in a great and continual commotion, day and night.” He cautiously confirmed that the town's enthusiasm was “such in some respects as I never saw it in before.” Still, there were limitations: the greatest enthusiasm “was principally among professors; the appearances of a work of conversion were in no measure equal to what had been the summer before.” Buell stayed on for several weeks after Edwards' return, and their combined efforts were rewarded with “a very great revival of religion.”4

Meanwhile, extremes multiplied. Individuals were having visions and going into trances, lying motionless for twenty-four hours at a time. Among these was Edwards' wife, Sarah Pierpont Edwards, whose narrative of her ecstatic religious experience during this period so impressed her husband that he later used it— though in a sanitized form so as to divert criticism— in Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival. But other enthusiasts caused concern.

-- 44 --

“When the people were raised to this height,” Edwards worried, “Satan took the advantage, and his interposition in many instances soon became very apparent: and a great deal of caution and pains were found necessary to keep the people, many of them, from running wild.”5

In mid-March Edwards sought a different avenue to build on the momentum, drafting a covenant renewal for the church. Covenant renewal had been used in New England churches since the late seventeenth century as a means of renewing loyalties to the religious and social ideals of the Puritan experiment. In essence, members of the church recovenanted, or rededicated, themselves through a document that affirmed their determination to follow the ways of God as a congregation. After the draft had been vetted by the church's “principal men,” the deacons, and the town's religious societies, the minister would lead “the people into a solemn public renewal of their covenant with God.”

To pursue covenant renewal at this time was a logical step for Edwards. Fifteen years of weekly preaching had taken its toll. His powerful rhetoric had spent its course, strains with the town elders were becoming increasingly personal. Even his wife was no longer exempt from criticism of her dress and style. If Edwards could not preach revival effectively, he could at least ritualize renewal. Covenant renewal rituals provided another means of reasserting the power of the church (and minister) over members' lives. But more pressing, these acts could renew and channel revival fervor. With its emphasis on lifelong Christian behavior as opposed to passing emotions, the covenant crystallized Edwards' prominent concerns of the previous two years.

On March 16, Edwards preached a preparatory sermon, Renewing Our Covenant with God. While conceding that many individuals had received a New Birth during the revivals, he declared the present as the time for a collective rededication. He hoped in this to avoid the downturn in enthusiasm of seven years earlier. If individuals would hold themselves and others accountable to the group, this would tie them together in the bonds of Christian love and watchfulness. Although ostensibly celebratory, the sermon is a surprisingly harsh jeremiad. Clearly Edwards was angry and frustrated with his congregation, especially the older members, and this strained relationship signaled the beginning of the end of his ministry.

The text of the covenant renewal, printed in a letter to Thomas Prince, was surprisingly long by colonial standards, and the tone of censure remained. Members were asked to lament “our past backslidings and ungrateful

-- 45 --

departings from God.” Social commentary abounded as members were asked to pledge the civil good: “[W]e will not make our worldly gain, or honor, or interest in the affections of others, or getting the better of any of a contrary party, that are in any respect our competitors, or the bringing or keeping them down, our governing aim, to the prejudice of the interest of religion and the honor of Christ.”6 Clearly, this was a covenant that would be impossible to fulfill.

Not surprisingly, then, in April 1742 a disappointed and denunciatory Edwards once again questioned the godliness of some of the enthusiasm. The Buell and Davenport revivals revived in him only misgivings. In Keeping the Presence of God, a fast-day sermon, he warned, in ways that anticipated Religious Affections, “'Tis a great mistake to think the devil can't come in. I have known it in abundance of instances: the devil has come in, even in the midst of the most excellent frames” (p. 530).

In these same months he expanded on his reservations, the need of the moment being “to distinguish hypocrites” and thereby purify the revivals. Edwards developed the ideas of Distinguishing Marks into a full-blown treatise entitled Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival. The massive treatise (378 pages) was Edwards' most ambitious to that date and confirms 1742 as a critical period of transition from attention to sermons and sermon series to a newly discovered interest in more formal treatises. In many ways, it marks a retreat to the speculative days of 1739 and the History of Redemption project. Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival was composed in late 1742 and published in Boston in March 1743.7 Even then, Edwards hoped to see divisions among the clergy healed and sought to protect sincere converts from “diabolical opposers.” He well knew that where the world of heaven dawned brightest the forces of hell would be raised most mightily, and it was in this context that he hoped to enlist all New Englanders.

In other ways, Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival echoes and expands on Distinguishing Marks. In one important regard, however, Edwards introduced a new emphasis: the “affections”— itself a retreat from the ministry of fear he had earlier promoted in imitation of George Whitefield. Old Light opponents could never get beyond the old faculty psychology that separated the will from the affections and gave primacy of place to the will. In these terms, religious enthusiasm must be bad, for it gave the emotions primacy over the will. In a bold psychological innovation, Edwards

-- 46 --

obliterated the sequential distinction between the affections and the will, arguing that “the affections of the soul are not properly distinguished from the will, as though they were two faculties in the soul. All acts of the affections of the soul are in some sense acts of the will, and all acts of the will are acts of the affections.”8

At that very moment in early 1742, he was apparently beginning sermons that would culminate in his Treatise on Religious Affections, published in 1746. With this momentous reconstruction, it became possible to provide a psychological grounding for the role of emotions in religion, if properly embedded in knowledge and a Spirit-given faith. Indeed, the role of the Holy Spirit was itself chiefly engaged with the affections, transforming the sense of the heart from love of self to love of God and, in the process, from the world of hell to the world of heaven. This, Edwards concluded, was the true grace of the revivals and the reason they had to be accounted a work of God: “[H]oliness has its seat chiefly in the heart, rather than the head: it therefore follows… that it consists chiefly in holy affections.… The informing of the understanding is all vain, any farther than it affects the heart; or, which is the same thing, has influence on the affections.”9 Patricia Tracy recognizes that along with a reintegration of the psyche, “The real importance of Some Thoughts, when it is seen as a companion piece to the ‘Personal Narrative,’ is its self-confidence in defining true conversion.”1 If he could no longer promote true conversion to a headstrong congregation, Edwards could at least define it for all posterity to read.

The period of the Great Awakening, so reflexively associated with Jonathan Edwards, was, in fact, not so great for him. In proportional terms, church membership in Northampton registered only modest increases. His parishioners, and even his own wife, responded more fervently to the preaching of other itinerants. Even his most famous sermon, Sinners in the Hands of an Angry God, was enthusiastically received only outside of Northampton. His older congregants blocked him at every stage, and even a covenant renewal failed to rekindle bonds of love and mutual affection.2 Outside of Northampton, Edwards' attempts to reason his way to a rapprochement between Old Lights and New Lights fell on deaf ears.

-- 47 --

Neither side was interested in accommodating the other. Far from being great, the years 1741–42 marked the beginning of the end for his Northampton ministry.

But all was not lost. Edwards still had the life of the mind, and to that he would return. The great treatises were on the near horizon and would clinch his international reputation. More important, the grand history of redemption remained. “Redemption,” he would later confess to the Trustees of the College of New Jersey, has “swallowed up my mind,” and has continually been “the chief entertainment and delight of my life.” From this project he knew that his destination was not in this world, but in heaven. In the end— perhaps not too far off— God's cause would triumph. Of that triumph, Edwards could be certain. He still had history to prove it.
