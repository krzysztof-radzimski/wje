Bringing the Ark to Zion a Second Time
And said unto them, Ye are the chief of the fathers of the Levites: sanctify yourselves, both ye and your brethren, that you may bring up the ark of Lord God of Israel unto the place that I have prepared for it. For because ye did it not at the first, the Lord our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order. I Chronicles 15:12–13.

Here I would observe,

1. What that affair is about which David here gives charge, viz. the affair of bringing up the ark into Zion, to the place that David had prepared for it. The ark was a type of Christ, and it was the symbol of the divine presence with that people. The people Israel had no image in their temple to represent God, as the heathens had images in their temples to represent their gods; but they had an ark, or a chest overlaid with gold, wherein were the law, tables of the covenant. And this was the symbol of the divine presence, first in the tabernacle, and then in the temple.

Over the ark, or upon the ark, there was the mercy seat, which was the throne of God where he sat, and where he dwelt between the cherubims in the tabernacle and temple. God's giving the children of Israel the ark to be among them, was a token that God was among them and dwelt with that people as his people. And therefore the presence of the ark in that land was esteemed the greatest privilege that that nation enjoyed above other nations. They accounted it their glory— and so indeed it was, when we consider that that presence of God went with the ark. And therefore when the Philistines took the ark and carried it away out of the land, and the wife of Phinehas heard that the ark of God was taken, cried out, “The glory is departed from Israel: because the ark of God is taken; the glory is departed from Israel: for the ark of God is taken” [1 Samuel 4:21-22].

After the Philistines had returned the ark, it was left a great while at Kirjath-jearim, a place in the outskirts of the land. Therefore it is said that

-- 249 --

while the ark abode in Kirjath-jearim, that “all the house of Israel lamented after the Lord,” because the time was long, even twenty years (1 Samuel 7:2). They looked upon it a sorrowful time, when Israel had not God dwelling in the midst of them but upon their utmost borders, as though he was not willing to come amongst such a sinful people anymore, and was ready to depart again, and leave 'em and go into another land, as he had lately done.

But after David was made king, it was a joyful time among them, for then David had leave and direction from God to bring up the ark of God from Kirjath-jearim into Zion. And therefore they went about this work very joyfully, as we have an account in the thirteenth chapter of this book. They were glad to see the ark coming again after such long absence, but their joy lasted but a little while: for God made a breach among them and turned aside to the house of Obed-edom the Gittite, a stranger, and sojourned there. But after a while, David seems to have had a leave and direction from God a second time to fetch up the ark of God into Zion; and it is upon this occasion that David gives this charge that we have in the text. So that his charge here given is with relation to that which [was] a special token of the presence of God in Israel, and was about their treatment of that which was Israel's special privilege and glory; it was concerning the joyful occasion of God's coming to dwell amongst them again after long absence.

2. We may observe what the charge is that he gives, which in substance is to sanctify themselves and treat the ark in a due manner.

3. We may observe the special reason why he thus5 insists on such a charge, viz. what they had suffered by an undue treatment of the ark before: “For because you did it not at the first, the Lord our God made a breach upon us, for that we sought him not after the due order.” They did not behave themselves as they ought to have done in bringing up the ark before, when the Levites ought to have carried [it]. They put it upon a cart to be drawn by oxen. God had in the Law given direction how the ark should be carried {by the Levites (Numbers 3)}, but it seems they had forgotten the Law or had not been duly observant of it. And then, when the oxen shook it, Uzzah, one that drave the cart, put forth his hand to secure it, and God break forth upon him and smote him that he died.

He had no leave to touch the ark, but only in such a manner and at such times as God directed. There was no direction in the Law about their taking hold of it on any such occasion, nor would there have been any such

-- 250 --

occasion, if the Law had been attended and the Levites had carried the ark with its staves instead of the oxen's drawing it. By this means their joy was at an end, and God refused for the present to go up and take up his settled abode in Zion amongst his people there.

But now David has leave to go again and bring up the ark, and this is the reason he insists in this charge that now they should treat the ark in due order and in a manner agreeable to God's Word, that they no more suffer in like manner for their error.

Doctrine.
When a people have once suffered by any errors they have committed in their treatment of special privileges and tokens of God's presence, they should beware that they ben't guilty of those errors the second time.

The following reasons may be given of the doctrine:

I. There should at all times be the utmost care to treat the tokens of God's presence in a due manner, and that both upon account of the greatness of the being that is present, and the preciousness of the privilege that a people have by the tokens of his presence. The greatness and honorableness of the Being whose presence is manifested should excite to the most diligent heed how to behave ourselves. If some great prince should come among us, would not [it] awaken care in us to behave ourselves properly towards him? Should not we be watchful lest we should behave ourselves some way indecently and offensively on such an occasion? How much more when the King of kings manifests himself present among a people. How careful was Moses of his behavior on Mt. Sinai, when he understood that God was there and see the tokens of his presence in the burning bush.

And how was Jacob's attention awakened at Bethel, when he waked out of sleep and understood that God was there (Genesis 28:16-17). Says he, “Surely God is in this place; and I knew it not. He was afraid and said, How dreadful is this place! for this is none other than the house of God, this is the gate of heaven.” And so when God came down on Mt. Sinai in the presence of the people, what care was required of them in their behavior, how more certain bounds and limits set, which every one must take heed to himself that he did not transgress, lest the Lord should break forth upon him. So it is said, Ecclesiastes 5:1-02, “Keep thy foot when thou goest to the house of God, and be more ready to hear, than to give the sacrifice of fools: for they consider not that they do evil. Be not rash with thy mouth, and let not thine heart be hasty to utter any thing before God: for God is in

-- 251 --

heaven, and thou upon earth: therefore let thy words be few.” Which implies as much as that we ought to have the utmost care of every step we take and all our behavior in a place where God is present, to be very attentive and heedful, and not foolish and rash with our foot or our mouth, when he is present that is so much above us.

And then, if we consider how precious the privilege is that a people have in the special tokens of God's presence, it should engage them to treat such a privilege in a due manner. Men are wont to be very careful of that which they esteem very precious; it shall be safely laid up in the choicest place and shall be kept and handled with the utmost care.

II. What a people have suffered for their error in their treatment of such a privilege in times past, ought to be taken as a warning from God to take heed to themselves for the time to come. We should learn righteousness by God's judgments. There is a voice in the providence of God as well as in the Word of God. God speaks once, yea, twice, and man perceives it not; but he opens men's ears to discipline by his rod [Job 36:10].

The breach that God [visited] upon Uzzah was on purpose to teach the children of Israel that error, and it was so taken and improved.

III. If they continue in their error, they can expect no other than still to suffer for it. As God has showed his displeasure once, so he will doubtless still be displeased in the commission of the same error. Yea,

IV. If their error be repeated, they may well expect to suffer more than they did before, and that because their sin will now be added to. To the same error they were guilty of before, will be added obstinacy and regardlessness of the warning God has given 'em; and so God will be more displeased, and therefore greater tokens of his displeasure may be expected. And as God's design in his judgments for sin in his people is to teach them and convince them of their sin, if they don't hear when he calls and warns, they may expect louder warnings of the same kinds, that they may be made to hear and understand.

Application.

I proceed now to make application of this to our case, in this church and congregation, with respect to our treatment of our special privileges and tokens of God's presence, that God has heretofore granted and now grants amongst us.

In these days of the gospel, the professing people of God have no such external token and symbol of the presence of God as the ark, that may go from 'em and come to 'em again, as the ark went from Israel into the land

-- 252 --

of the Philistines, and sojourned in Kirjath-jearim, and then was brought up into Jerusalem. But the tokens of God's presence in his church now are spiritual. The way that God manifests himself to be amongst a professing people now is by the influences and operations of his Holy Spirit upon them. At a time of the pouring out of God's Holy Spirit upon a people, God appears amongst them in a more glorious manner than of old he appeared amongst the children of Israel, above the mercy seat over the ark.

We in this place have had such tokens of God's presence amongst us in times past, especially in that remarkable season that began about seven years ago. And God is now again in great mercy granting us a measure of that same token of his presence, whereby we possess a much greater privilege than the children of Israel did in having the ark among them; and doubly6 have as much cause to take heed to ourselves, with respect to our treatment of such a privilege, as they had to take care how they handled the ark.

There is great reason to consider and inquire whether God did not see some things displeasing to him in our treatment of such a privilege when we last enjoyed it. You know the awful breach that God made upon us in the time of it, towards the close of it, over one of the principal men in the town.7 And the awful breach God not long after made in the house of public worship, in the time of our solemn assembly, putting us into a terrible surprise, breaking us off from the public exercise— an accident by which many lives were greatly exposed, and many were sorely wounded.8

And, which is more than all, God departed from us and left us. The joy of bringing up the ark lasted but a little while; the ark turned aside from us and did not come to take up its settled abode in our Zion. We were soon left, and sunk into a very dull, cold, dead state. Which was very unexpected to ourselves and others after so great and wonderful an appearance of God amongst us.

It was a joyful time in Israel when they were bringing up the ark from Kirjath-jearim, after it had been there so long and they had lamented after the Lord. But how soon was their joy at an end, how unexpectedly did God turn aside from them. So who would have thought, that saw what a blessed joyful season it was, in that remarkable wonderful season, that times would be so soon and so greatly altered.

-- 253 --

Certainly these things may be sufficient to put us upon strict inquiring whether we sought the Lord after the due order: whether there was not some considerable and notable errors that we were guilty of in our treatment of so great a privilege, while we enjoyed it. It greatly concerns us to find out our former errors, and to beware of 'em, now God is graciously returning to us again and we are going about the joyful work of bringing up the ark the second time.

That we may so do,

I shall, first, show wherein the error consisted that was committed in Israel in their bringing up the ark the first time; and second, shall lead you to inquire whether we have not been guilty of the same errors in the last time of the remarkable tokens of the presence of God among us, and shall warn against them for the future.

I. I would show wherein the errors consisted that was committed in Israel in bringing up the ark the first time. It seems to have consisted in these three things, viz. pride, irreverence and disobedience.

First. In pride. When the children went about this great work, they were very joyful. It is said, 1 Chronicles 13:8, that “David and all Israel played before God with all their might, and with singing, and with harps, and with psalteries, and with timbrels, and with cymbals, and with trumpets.” But they seem to have gone about the work in too self-sufficient a manner, not with a due sense of their exceeding sinfulness and utter unworthiness of such a privilege as that God should dwell among them and that the ark of his strength should be brought to them. They did not sanctify themselves beforehand. When they went about bringing up the ark the first time, we read nothing of their offering up any sacrifices for their sins, as they did the second time. The second time, after God had smitten 'em and humbled 'em, then they were sensible of their need of sacrifices for their sins, and therefore went about it with sacrifices offered to God. As we read, 1 Chronicles 15:26, “They offered seven bullocks and seven rams.” The first time, they had forgot themselves and offered no sacrifice, as not considering how unworthy they were of such a privilege as they hoped now to enjoy. One would think they had reason enough to consider their need of sacrifices the first time, considering how God had testified his displeasure against them in departing from them, forsaking the tabernacle of Shiloh, and delivering the ark into captivity to the Philistines as he had done, and after that remaining so long in Kirjath-jearim; but they did not reflect upon it, nor consider their own unworthiness. And so it seems to have been from their pride and self-sufficiency that, when they were about

-- 254 --

such a great work, they never consulted God's law to see in what manner the ark ought to be carried. They were not sensible of their own blindness and ignorance; they thought their own word sufficient to direct 'em in this matter, and so substituted their own inventions in the room of God's institutions. They put the ark into a new cart to be drawn by oxen— and that a very unsuitable way of carrying it— in a proud conceit of the sufficiency of their own wisdom to direct them.

So it seems to have been from pride in Uzzah that he presumed to lay hold on the ark, when he had no warrant from God's law so to do. He looked upon it, that he had a great privilege; God had put great honor upon his father Abinadab's family, in that the ark had remained there for twenty years. And who should be appointed to drive the cart in which the ark was carried but he and Ahio, and he was lifted up with those things. And it was this that made him so forward and presumptuous, so boldly to handle the ark of God.

Second. Another part of their error was irreverence, which was very much a fruit of the other. They treated the ark, upon which the name of the Lord of hosts was called, in an irreverent manner. They were full of joy, and their hearts were lifted up on the joyful occasion; and in the midst of their rejoicing, they forgot themselves and handled the ark in a bold irreverent manner. If the people's hearts had been so full, as it ought to have been, of reverence towards the ark, they would not have set oxen to draw it; and if Uzzah's heart had been possessed with due reverence, he would not have presumed to touch it, excepting only as directed and commanded by God's law.

Third. The third part of their error was that they placed religion rather in profession and show than in obedience. There was a great deal of profession and show of respect to God among them. They gathered all Israel together, from all quarters of the land, a mighty company of them, to attend the ark. This had a great show of respect.

And then, as the ark went along, they praised God. It is said, they played “with all their might, with singing, with harps, and with psalteries, and with timbrels, and with cymbals, and with trumpets” [1 Chronicles 13:8]. In this, again, they made a great profession and show of respect.

And then, when the oxen stumbled and shook the ark, and it seemed to be in danger of falling, and Uzzah put forth his hand to hold it, that again had a show of respect. But at the same [time], they were very negligent of obedience to God's commands. When they so abounded in testimonies of respect, in profession and show, they were greatly wanting in obedience, not considering that to obey is better than sacrifice, and to

-- 255 --

hearken than the fat of lambs: for at that very time, they were in direct disobedience to some of God's commands, as they were in carrying the ark in a cart drawn by oxen, when God had commanded that it should be carried on the shoulders of the Levites. And so Uzzah brake God's law in laying hold on the ark, for the ark was never to be touched but in such and such a manner, and on such and such occasions, as the law of God particularly prescribed.

These were the errors that Israel were guilty of in bringing up the ark the first time, for which they suffered, and for which God for a while turned aside from them. Now let us, in the

II. [Second] place, inquire whether we han't been guilty of the same errors in our behavior under that wonderful mercy we received, when God gave such remarkable tokens of his presence amongst us, several years since.

First. Let us strictly inquire whether or no we were not too much lifted up with our privilege. Was there too much of an appearance of a public pride, if I may so call it? Were we not lifted up with the honor that God had put upon us as a people, beyond most other people? Were we not foolishly taken with our comparative honor, and pleased to see ourselves lifted above others, too much forgetting what a stiff-necked unworthy people we were? We might well be joyful, but did not we, in the midst of our rejoicing, and playing with harps and psalteries and cymbals, forget our sins and great unworthiness? Was there not too much of an appearance of it in our talk of the great work that was among us, before others of other towns? Was there not too much of an assuming air? Did it not make us something self-sufficient in our way of talking to others, as though we thought we were the people, and we were confident that we ourselves were guides to the blind, and a light of them that are in darkness, and instructors of the foolish, and teachers of babes?

Let us be strict in examining ourselves as to this matter, and let us take heed to ourselves, to avoid all appearance of anything of that nature for the future. It surely becomes us, considering how unworthy we are, not to take the least glory to ourselves before God or men, but greatly to humble ourselves. Let us speak humbly of ourselves before others, and walk humbly before them as a people that are fully sensible that no glory belongs to them, and that we in ourselves are no better than the worst of our neighbors. O! let us take great heed that Christ have all the glory, that he alone may be exalted, and that we all may be abased.

And let particular persons strictly examine themselves whether they han't been lifted up with their particular experiences. I think, according

-- 256 --

to what observation I have made— as I have had [more] opportunity of very extensive observation than any other person in the town— that is has been a pretty prevailing error in the town, that persons are not sufficiently sensible of the danger of self-righteousness after conversion. They seem to be sensible that persons are in danger of it before they are converted, but they think that when a man is converted, he is brought off wholly from his own righteousness, just as if there was no danger of any workings of self-righteousness afterwards.

But this is from a great mistake of what is intended by a man's being brought wholly off from his own righteousness when he is converted. 'Tis not meant that a self-righteousness principle is wholly done away, that there is no remains of such a disposition in the heart. There is as much of the remains of that as there is of any other corruption in the heart.

So a man is brought, when converted, wholly to renounce all his sins as well as to renounce all his own righteousness. But that don't argue that he is wholly freed from all remains of sin. So no more is he wholly freed from remains of self-righteousness. There is a fountain of it left. There is an exceeding disposition in men, as long as they live, to make a righteousness of what is in themselves, and an exceeding disposition in men to make a righteousness of spiritual experiences, as well as other things; and it was well if some more were sensible of it. [A convert is] apt to be exalted with high thoughts of his own eminency in grace.

Let every one examine himself. There is no pride in the world more secret and more difficult for persons to discern in themselves than this pride.

How was it with you in that wonderful time, wherein you received so much? Was not you very apt to look on yourself as an eminent saint, one more eminent than most others? Was it sometimes natural to you to think that God would show you some peculiar favors above others?

Was there not prevailing in you the reverse of that disposition, spoken of, Philippians 2:3, “Each esteem others better than themselves”; and the disposition that the apostle Paul was of, who calls himself “less than the least of all saints” (Ephesians 3:8)?

Let every one examine himself what has commonly been his motive in speaking of his own experience, whether it has not often been your own honor, and that you might be well accounted of by men. And han't this been a temptation to some of you, to set out your own experience as much as you could?

You that think that God has done great things for you: how has it been

-- 257 --

with you? Han't you been really lifted up? Don't go away with a notion that you are above danger of it. There is not one of you, no, not the best saint of you all, that is above danger of it. Don't think that you are above danger of it because discoveries have had a tendency to make you lie low and to work by humility. You are in danger of being lifted up with that very humility. None of [you] are better than the apostle Paul, and he was in danger of {being “exalted above measure through the abundance of the revelations” given him} (2 Corinthians 12:7).

Therefore, don't think there is no need for you to keep a strict eye upon your own heart with respect to those things. There is need of the strictest scrutiny imaginable, and a continual watch. Therefore let every one deal faithfully with himself in examining what is past, and let us all keep the strictest watch over ourselves for the future. Let us watch over our own hearts and over our behavior. Let us be careful to avoid all appearance of being lifted up with what God has done for us. And if you think that God has given you better experiences than others, yet it will be very imprudent in you to say so, or to say what implies it: for though you seem to ascribe it to God and not yourself, yet it will be as much as to say9 that your opinion of yourself is that you are a more eminent saint than most of your neighbors. Therefore 'tis best to avoid such expressions concerning what you have met with: “It seems to me I have met with great things”; “It seems to me God has done great things for me”; “It seems to me I have no cause to be ashamed to speak before kings,” and the like. Whatever you think, you had better avoid such expositions and all expressions that carry in it that you think you are an eminent saint. If you talk of your own experiences, talk of 'em in a very humble manner, and don't be forward to talk of your experience unless it be for good ends. Don't do it for the sake of the good thoughts of others.

And beware you don't set forth your experiences in too strong expressions. You may possibly be in greater danger of this than you are aware of. It is natural in all cases, when men's affections are moved and are flowing— let it be in temporal things or in spiritual things— to speak of things about which they are affected, and set 'em out in very strong expressions.

Natural men that hear those strong expressions are ready to expect that those that speak should live like angels. And when they don't find, then it does 'em a great deal of hurt; it hardens 'em and makes 'em think that all is a mere delusion and fancy, and makes 'em question whether there

-- 258 --

be anything in religion. Let us take heed to ourselves to avoid all manner of appearances of pride in our rejoicings. Let us not forget our unworthiness while we rejoice at the bringing up of the ark.

Second. Let us examine ourselves, whether we han't been also guilty of the second part of the error that the children of Israel were guilty of in {bringing up the ark the first time}, viz. irreverence, or not treating those great privileges and extraordinary tokens of God's presence among us with due reverence.

And here I would particularly mention one thing [in] which I think some have been guilty of this error to a great degree, and that is in commonly talking of things of religion in a laughing and light kind of manner. 'Tis to be feared that many in the last time of [the] pouring {out of God's Spirit} were in this respect like the children of Israel, that in the midst of their great rejoicings, and playing on harps {and psalteries and cymbals}, they forgot the distinction that was between God and them. They had forgot how great a God that was, whose ark that was that they were then removing, and began to treat it in a light kind of manner.

So it has certainly been in a great measure [with us], and therefore no wonder God has made a breach upon us, and turned aside from us, and left us. This is not the way that the Scripture teaches us to rejoice in God, to rejoice with a light merry sort of an air. But there we are said to “rejoice with trembling” (second psalm, eleventh verse). The meaning {is that we should treat extraordinary tokens of God's presence among us with due reverence}.

Persons talking of things of religion with much laughing and lightness was one thing that greatly obscured the work of God that was here before, and doubtless much offended and displeased the infinite majesty. This did not savor of that reverence that was in Moses on the Mount, when God discovered himself to him [Exodus 3].

Did we savor of that reverence that Jacob showed after God had manifestly discovered himself to him {at Bethel}? He had as much reason to rejoice, {and to play on harps and psalteries and cymbals}, but he says after it, “How dreadful is this place! this is none other but the house of God” [Genesis 28:17]. He sets himself, with the greatest reverence and solemnity of spirit, to give God the glory of what he had done for him there. It don't seem to hold forth a due sense of our littleness, how that God is in heaven and we upon earth.

Such a way of talking of things of religion and of spiritual experiences savors of a great deal [of] freedom that men use with the ark of God. And indeed, 'tis to be feared we have provoked God by being very free and bold in handling the ark.

-- 259 --

There is another sort of joy that is a great deal better and sweeter than {a light merry sort of an air}, as many here can testify: a solid, solemn, reverential joy, more apt to bring forth humble tears than a light kind of laughter.

When there is any grace in that kind of joy, that works so there is a great deal of nature mixed with grace. Such experiences are mixed experiences. Those that are purely spiritual are not wont to dispose persons to talk lightly {of religion}.

Such joy is more superficial, is not so deep, nor does it put near so much honor upon God as {a reverential joy}, nor does it so much honor religion in the eyes of others.

When persons that have an hope of conversion talk of things of religion with becoming seriousness, it will have a great deal more tendency to affect and solemnize the hearts of natural men that hear it, than to hear it talked of with an air of lightness. This rather tends to prejudice them against it.

It don't savor of that which we are directed to in Ezekiel 16:63, “That thou mayest remember, and be confounded,” but it savors too much of pride and self-sufficiency and presumption.

Third. Let us inquire whether we han't also been guilty of the errors of the children of Israel that was last mentioned, viz. taking more care to testify our respect to God by a show and profession than by obedience to God's commands. It must be confessed that there has been a great deal done at testifying respect to God in outward profession and show. There was in the time of the last outpouring {of God's Spirit} a great deal of religious talk and a great deal of zeal at frequenting religious meetings, as the children of Israel flocked from all parts {to bring the ark to Jerusalem}.

But then, at the same time, did not God see that there were many of his commands with respect to duty to our neighbors neglected? Did not God see that, all this while, men kept their old leaven of malice in their hearts one towards another, kept it alive through the very midst of that wonderful season, ready to break out again when occasion should serve? No wonder, therefore, that God who saw that turned away from us. And han't many of the professors of conversion too much more neglected many of the second-table duties1 than they have those things that belong to a fair profession? Han't we been too much like a cake not turned, religious as to the external duties of one table, but not the other? They that place religion chiefly in outward show, such as frequenting religious meetings,

-- 260 --

rather than in keeping {God's commands}, will neglect thereto if it proves troublesome.

Let us, therefore, also avoid this error for the time to come. Let us abound in those things that appertain to a good profession, but let us not leave the other undone. Let the principal thing with us be to obey the voice of the Lord. This God did much more require than the other. Jeremiah 7:22-23, “I spake not unto your fathers concerning burnt offerings or sacrifices: but this thing commanded I them, saying, Obey my voice, and I will be your God, and ye shall be my people: and walk ye in all the ways that I have commanded you, that it may be well unto you.” This is the way to have God abiding with us. John 14:23, “If a man love me, he will keep my words: and my Father will love him, and we will come unto him, and make our abode with him.” Let us cast away all the old leaven of malice out of our hearts, and let us henceforward abound in duties of justice and forgiveness, and peaceableness and meekness, and forbearance and long— suffering. This will be the way to have the outpourings of the Spirit of God continued with us.

And of all the external things that we can do to have the Spirit of God continued amongst [us], I believe the most likely thing to be successful to that end, of any one thing whatsoever, is abounding in deeds of love and charity. The abounding in deeds of love is the likeliest way to have the God of love and peace always dwelling with us (Romans 15:33). If we would now manifest our thankfulness for what God does for us by obeying that precept of Christ, Matthew 10:8, “Freely ye have received, freely give,” and should be much in deeds of love and charity,2 in helping one another under our difficulties and straits, and promoting any design that tends to encourage that duty, I believe no one external thing whatsoever is so likely to be successful to this end. We see how God has smiled upon the charitable disposition that was manifested when the Reverend Mr. Whitefield was here.

The success of the children of Israel should encourage us to avoid our former errors. They had the joy of the presence of the ark but a little while the first time, because they sought not the Lord in the due order. But when they were sensible of their error, and the second time corrected it, then they were successful; then they actually brought up the ark into the city Zion and placed it {in the tent} with joy and gladness, and had no interruption or allay to their joy.

-- 261 --

It was one of the most joyful times in Israel that ever was, and God thenceforth continued amongst them. And if we in like manner correct our former errors, there is no reason to doubt but we shall have like success, and that God, who now is returning to us and calling us a second time to bring up the ark, will henceforward no more depart from us.3
