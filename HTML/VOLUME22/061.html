Renewing Our Covenant with God

-- 509 --

In February 1742 a disappointed Edwards left for a two-week preaching tour to the area of Leicester, Massachusetts. As his Northampton stand-in he allowed a young protégé of his, Samuel Buell, to fill the Northampton pulpit. To Edwards' joy and dismay, Buell set the congregation on fire. People cried out in meeting and, as Edwards described it, “the whole town seemed to be in a great and continual commotion, day and night.” Indeed, the town was “such in some respects as I never saw it in before.” Buell stayed on for several weeks after Edwards' return, and their combined efforts were rewarded with “a very great revival of religion.” During this time, individuals saw visions and went into trances, lying motionless for twenty-four hours at a time. Among these was Sarah Pierpont Edwards, whose narrative of her ecstatic religious experience during this period so impressed her husband that he later used it in Some Thoughts Concerning the Revival.1

In mid-March Edwards sought to build on the momentum by drawing up a draft of a covenant renewal for the church. Covenant renewal had been used in New England churches since the late seventeenth century as a means of renewing loyalties to the religious and social ideals of the Puritan experiment. Basically, members of the church would recovenant, or rededicate, themselves to following the ways of God as a body of believers. After the draft had been vetted by the church's “principal men,” the deacons, and the town's religious societies, Edwards “led the people into a solemn public renewal of their covenant with God.”

To have a covenant renewal at this time was a logical step for Edwards. First, it provided another means of reasserting the power of the church over members' lives, as seen earlier in The Means and End of Excommunication. But more pressingly, this act could renew and channel revival fervor. With its emphasis on Christian behavior and discourse, too, the covenant

-- 510 --

crystallized prominent concerns of Edwards' over the previous two years. He laid the groundwork on March 9 by preaching a sermon on Isaiah 44:3-05, in which he sought to bind recent converts to the church as well as encourage the preservation of the revival spirit. He told his people that by this act of covenanting they were “subscribing to the head” and joining the commonwealth of God.2 At the close of the sermon, Edwards announced that the following week the congregation would adopt the covenant.

On March 16 Edwards preached Renewing Our Covenant with God. In this sermon, preparatory to the ceremony, Edwards called on Northampton to renew its covenant with the Lord as one body. Throughout the revival time, many individuals had turned to Christ and personally renewed their covenant with Christ. Now Edwards called for a collective rededication. There were many reasons for such a call. One of his primary goals throughout this revival was to avoid the downturn in enthusiasm of seven years earlier. If individuals would hold themselves and others accountable to the group, this would tie them together in the bonds of Christian love and watchfulness.

Though ostensibly solemn and celebratory, this sermon is a jeremiad. Rather than a warning of punishment which assumes a collective accountability, it is a call for collective accountability so that the church of Northampton can stand closer to God. Yet Edwards is aware that recovenanting is not specifically mandated in Scripture— a problem that had plagued the practice since New England clergymen began it in earnest earlier in the century as a means of eliciting reform. Thus he pulls together a great deal of evidence to legitimate the act. He first uses examples from Scripture and then appeals to the “light of nature.” Lastly, he considers the nature of church membership, which assumes participation in a collective body of believers. Church members may have joined as individuals, but in doing so they became part of a community. To Edwards' way of thinking, he is not creating a community anew but merely holding its members to the spiritual bylaws to which they have already agreed.

The defensive tone of Renewing Our Covenant suggests that not everyone in the congregation was convinced that a covenant renewal was necessary or even legitimate. The sermon is structured around a series of propositions that contain a number of objections and answers, perhaps actual objections presented to Edwards. His defense of covenant renewal

-- 511 --

as not “superfluous” also suggests that a segment of the church still resisted. While Edwards viewed the covenant as a sign of the people's consent to obey God, many in the congregation could have viewed it as a sign that they were to obey the minister and rein in their material ambitions.

To read the covenant is to see why there might have been objections to it.3 It is unusually long for a covenant renewal and its demands were clearly unrealistic, even for the most harmonious of communities— which Northampton was not.4 The covenant resounds with Edwards' insistence on the social evidences of Christianity. Roughly the first half of the piece deals with individual moral behavior— particularly economic behavior— that affected other individuals or the community as a whole. The people of Northampton were to deal fairly and openly with one another, render everyone his due in business, make restitution for any wrongs, and avoid unjust reflections upon the characters of others. Those in positions of power were not to use their influence either for personal gain or to further the interests of parties. The youth of the parish were to avoid irreligious pastimes as well as “freedoms and familiarities in company” that led to sexual promiscuity. Ironically, at this very time some young men of the parish were making salacious use of a book on midwifery to mock young women. In two years this scandal would come to light, and Edwards and his people would be embroiled in the “Bad Book” case as he brought the young men up for public rebuke.

The sermon Renewing Our Covenant and the covenant renewal of 1742 therefore reflect the changing relationship of Edwards and his church. In the coming years, Edwards would use the covenant as a measuring stick to point out— in increasingly vigorous terms— his people's spiritual and moral failures.5 The 1740s would see increased instances of conflict between minister and people, as Edwards struggled against what he perceived as increasing worldliness and disrespect in the congregation, and the people against what they perceived as their pastor's efforts to accrue power and to dictate over their lives. Consequently, Edwards increasingly isolated himself from his congregation, devoting less effort to his sermons and instead writing on the nature of conversion and working in the interest of revival abroad. If he could not hope for awakenings at home, he

-- 512 --

would look for them elsewhere. In the process, pastor and congregation left each other behind.

* * *

This sermon is eight duodecimo leaves in length. Edwards inscribed it “March 1742.” It has no Exposition or Application; instead, it is characterized by a series of propositions, some containing lists of objections and answers. To judge by the lack of repreaching marks and later emendations, the sermon was not delivered again.

The outlined nature of the written text allowed Edwards to extemporize as much as he may have needed. Each page is divided into two columns, and each column in turn is divided into “boxes” of various sizes by horizontal lines. Within each “box” Edwards wrote topical statements, fragmentary key phrases, or even single words that would help him maintain the sermon's general flow. This manuscript's columnar form is one of Edwards' first in this format. By the middle of the 1740s, nearly all of his sermons are written in this fashion.
