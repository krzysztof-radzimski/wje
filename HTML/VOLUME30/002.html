"Prophecies of the Messiah" Miscellanies no. 1067.3 Misc. 1067.3 (ed.) Jonathan Edwards

80. PSALM 86. In the 86th Psalm there is a prophecy of the times of the Messiah, as that alone sufficiently demonstrates, that here is a prophecy of the calling of the Gentiles and conversion of all nations. V. 9, "All nations whom thou hast made shall come and worship before thee, O Lord; and shall glorify thy name." And this prediction is, in the 40th and following chapters of Isaiah, attended with a declaration of the infinitely superior greatness and glory of God above the gods of the Gentiles, and his being the only true God, and there1 being no works like his, vv. 8, 10.

And this prophecy of the calling of the Gentiles is here spoken of as a consequence of God's hearing and answering the prayer of the Psalmist, and delivering him out of trouble, v. 7, as it is in Ps. 22 and Ps. 138.

V. 10, "For thou art great, and dost wondrous things," is agreeable to very many prophecies of the Messiah that speak of great wonders God would work in his day.

V. 16, "give thy strength unto thy servant, and save the son of thy handmaid." This is agreeable to the Messiah's being called the seed of the woman [Gen. 3:15], and is also agreeable to Is. 7[:14], "a virgin shall conceive, and bring forth a son," etc. See note on Ps. 116:16.2

V. 13, "Thou hast delivered my soul from the lowest hell," is agreeable to Ps. 16[:10], "thou wilt not leave my soul in hell."

81. PSALM 58. The 58th Psalm seems not to be spoken with regard to Saul and other rulers in Israel in his days that persecuted him, but to be wholly prophetical concerning the future destruction of the enemies of the church. There are several things that evince that the Psalm has not respect to Saul and his companions and assistants in persecution:

1. Those that David speaks of are a vast multitude of people, v. 1, "O congregation": in which there is an allusion either to the whole multitude of Israel, that were wont to meet three times a year, or the seventy elders. And again, it is added, "do ye judge uprightly, O ye sons of men," as though it was a great part of mankind that were those that he had respect to. But David's enemies in Saul's time were comparatively but few; the very ground of Saul's persecuting him, was that he had the affections and esteem of the greater part of the nation of Israel, which made Saul jealous of him. I Sam. 18[:5], "And David went out whithersoever Saul sent him, and behaved himself wisely: and Saul set him over the men of war, and he was accepted in the sight of all the people, and also in the sight of Sauls' servants"; and v. 16, "And all Israel and Judah loved David, because he went out and came in before them."

2. The enemies are spoken of as suddenly destroyed and disappointed and vanishing away, even at that very time when they are making great preparations to destroy God's people, and have invaded them, and are at the point of executing their design, vv. 6-10. But this don't agree at all with Saul and his fellow persecuters of David: for when Saul was destroyed, he had been making preparation to go to war with the Philistines, and was gone out to war with them who were great enemies of God's visible church, and had for some time given over pursuing after David, I Sam. 26:25 and 27:1, 4. But these things well agree with what is often prophesied of the destruction of the enemies of the church in the Messiahs days, in Is. 24-35, Ps. 46 and 48, Ezek. 38 and 39, and many others. That, vv. 8[-9], "As a snail which melteth, let every one of them pass away: like the untimely birth of a woman, that he may not see the sun. Before your pots can feel the thorns, he shall take them away as with a whirlwind, both living, and in his wrath," is very agreeable to Is. 29:5-8,

Moreover the multitude of thy strangers shall be like small dust, and the multitude of the terrible ones shall be as chaff that passeth away: yea, it shall be at an instant suddenly. Thou shalt be visited of the Lord of hosts with thunder, and with earthquake, and great noise, with storm and tempest, and the flame of devouring fire. And the multitude of all nations that fight against Ariel, even all that fight against her and her munition, and that distress her, shall be as a dream of a night vision. It shall be as when a hungry man dreameth, and, behold, he eateth; but he awaketh, and his soul is empty: or as when a thirsty man dreameth, and, behold, he drinketh; and he awaketh, and, behold, he is faint, and his soul hath appetite: so shall the multitude of all the nations be, that fight against mount Zion.

See also Is. 41:11-12. Compare also what is said in this Psalm, v. 6-11, with Is. 30:27 to the end.

3. The imprecations and curses that are here, vv. 6-9 together with v. 10, "The righteous shall rejoice when he seeth the vengeance: he shall wash his feet in the blood of the wicked," is not applicable to David with regard to Saul, who was so far from trampling on his blood that he greatly lamented his death, and condemned those that insulted him in his misery. But this 10th verse is exactly parallel with many prophecies of the destruction of the enemies of the church in the days of the Messiah. That "the righteous shall rejoice when he seeth the vengeance," is agreeable to Is. 30:29, 32 and innumerable other prophecies. And that in the same verse, "he shall wash his feet in the blood of the wicked," is agreeable to Ps. 68:23, "That thy foot may be dipped in the blood of thine enemies"; and Is. 26:5-6, "he bringeth down them that dwell on high; the lofty city, he layeth it low, . . . The foot shall tread it down, even the feet of the poor, and the steps of the needy"; Mic. 7:10, "now shall she be trodden down as the mire of the streets"; and Mal. 4:3, "And ye shall tread down the wicked; for they shall be ashes under the soles of your feet."

What is said, vv. 3-5, of the desperate natural corruption and depravity of man, as appearing in the persecutors of the church, is agreeable to Ps. 14 and 53, before taken notice of.3

V. 11, "So that a man shall say, Verily there is a reward for the righteous: verily he is a God that judgeth in the earth," is agreeable to Mal. 3:18, "Then shalt ye return, and discern between the righteous and the wicked, between him that serveth God and him that serveth him not"; and Ps. 102:13, 15, "Thou shalt arise, and have mercy upon Zion: for the time to favor her, yea, the set time, is come. . . . So the heathen shall fear the name of the Lord, and all the kings of the earth thy glory."

82. PSALM 65. That there is a prophecy of the times of the Messiah in the 65th Psalm, is evident from that, that here the calling of the Gentiles and the conversion of all nations to God is here foretold, as v. 2, "unto thee shall all flesh come"; and v. 5, "thou art the confidence of all the ends of the earth, and of them that are afar off upon the sea." To the like purpose is that, v. 8, "thou makest the outgoings of the morning and of the evening to rejoice"--that is, the utmost ends of the earth in the east and west, whence go forth the morning and evening--which is agreeable to v. 5; and to Is. 24:16, "From the utmost parts of the earth have we heard songs, even glory to the righteous"; and Ps. 113:3, "From the rising of the sun to the going down of the same the Lord's name is to be praised"; Is. 59:19, "So shall they fear the name of the Lord from the west, and his glory from the rising of the sun"; and Mal. 1:11, "For from the rising of the sun even unto the going down of the same my name shall be great among the Gentiles"; Is. 43:5, "I will bring thy seed from the east, and gather thee from the west."

Here is a prediction of great salvation that God would accomplish for Zion, in answer to prayer and after waiting for God, vv. 1-2, 5, agreeable to Is. 62:6-8, etc., and Ezek. 36:37 and Gen. 49:18, Is. 25:9 and 26:8 and 33:2 and 64:4, Is. 8:17, Mic. 7:7.

V. 3, "as for our transgressions, thou shalt purge them away," is agreeable to Is. 33:24 and Joel 3:21, Jer. 31:34 and 33:8, Mic. 7:18, Ps. 85:2, Zech. 3:4, Is. 43:25 and 44:22 and 40:2, Jer. 3:12-13, Hos. 14:1-2.

V. 5, "By terrible things in righteousness wilt thou answer us," is agreeable to Ps. 45[:4], "ride forth because of truth, meekness and righteousness; and thy right hand shall teach thee terrible things," and very many places in forementioned prophecies that speak of very terrible things that [God] would do for the deliverance of his people from their enemies.

'Tis signified in the 8th verse, that hereby God would strike a dread upon the hearts of the nations, all over the world; which is agreeable to Ps. 58:9-11, Ps. 48:5-6, Ps. 46:6, Ps:14:5, Is. 2:18-21.

V. 7, "Which stillest the noise of the seas, the noise of their waves, and the tumults of the people," is agreeable to Ps. 46:8-10. See also other places referred to, §47.

What is said, v. 9 to the end, concerning God's watering and blessing the earth, and causing it to yield its fruits in abundance, is agreeable to Ps. 67:6 and 85:12, Is. 30:23-24, Jer. 31:12, Ezek. 34:27, 29 and 36:29-30, Amos 9:13-14.

83. PSALM 66. In the 66th Psalm, there is clear evidence of a respect to the times of the Messiah: for here is mention of all nations, or the whole habitable world, being brought to the acknowledgement and worship of the true God; and it is described in like manner as in other prophecies of the times of the Messiah, viz. by all lands rejoicing in God, and praising, worshipping him, and joyfully singing to his name, vv. 1-2, 4, 8.

What is said, vv. 3, 5, of God's terrible doings, is agreeable to what is said in the foregoing Psalm, v. 5, and innumerable other prophecies before mentioned.

What is said, v. 3, of God's enemies being affrighted through his terrible works, and so being afraid any longer to openly oppose, but submitting through fear, is agreeable to the foregoing Psalm, v. 8, and Ps. 46:6 to the end, and Ps. 58:9-11 and 48:5-6 and 14:5, and Is. 2:16, 19.

This Psalm speaks of these great and terrible works being wrought for the deliverance of his people, after they had been in great affliction, in subjection to the power and insults of their cruel enemies, as v. 10, etc.; which is agreeable to other prophecies of the redemption of the Messiah.

84. PSALM 80, ESPECIALLY VV. 15-17. Ps. 80:17, "Let thine hand be upon the man of thy right hand, upon the son of man whom thou madest strong for thyself." The person here spoken seems evidently to be the Messiah, for the context makes it evident that it was some great person that God would raise up and strengthen to that end, that he might turn his people to God and accomplish deliverance for his people, and put new life into them, and save his vine that he brought out of Egypt after her hedges had been broken down, and it had been exposed to all its enemies without any protection, and after "the boar out of the wood" had wasted it and "the wild beast of the field" had devoured it; which by the prophecies of the Messiah everywhere, can be no other than the Messiah.

His being called the man of God's right hand, is agreeable to Ps. 110[:1], "Sit thou on my right hand"; and Ps. 2:6-7, "I have set my king . . . Thou art my son"; and Ps. 89:27, "I will make him my firstborn, higher than the kings of the earth."

The whole verse, "Let thine hand be upon the man of thy right hand, upon the son of man whom thou madest strong for thyself," is agreeable to Ps. 89:19, 21, "I have laid help upon one that is mighty; . . . With whom my hand shall be established: mine arm also shall strengthen him"; and Is. 42:6, "I the Lord have called thee in righteousness, and will hold thine hand, and will keep thee, and give thee for a covenant of the people"; and Is. 49:8, "I have helped thee: and will preserve thee, and give thee for a covenant of the people, to establish the earth, and to cause to inherit the desolate heritages."

He seems here to be mentioned in this verse as a man and "the son of man," because it is a remarkable and wonderful thing, that a man that is a worm, and the son of man that is but a worm, and is so often spoken of as the grass and flower of the field, and a blast of wind, and one in whom is no help and altogether vanity, and by no means to be trusted in, should be made strong.

The son of man spoken of, v. 17, that God had made strong for himself, that was the man of God's right hand, seems to be the same with the BRANCH that he had made strong for himself, spoken of, v. 15 foregoing; which is the same man whose name is the BRANCH, spoken of, Zech. 6[:13], that was to sit on God's throne in the temple, and the same with that tender twig that God would set on the mountain of the height of Israel, and cause it to "bring forth boughs, and bear fruit, and to be a goodly cedar," etc., Ezek. 17:22-24.4

'Tis no argument against this, that this Branch is spoken of, v. 16, as being "burnt with fire," and so needing deliverance as well as the rest of the vine: for that is agreeable to Is. 53, and it is also agreeable to Ps. 22 and 69 and other psalms that have been already considered, that the Messiah as well as his people should be oppressed and as it were consumed by his and their enemies, and that in his deliverance they have deliverance.

One benefit here mentioned of God's hands being upon the man of his right hand, and his being strengthened to deliver and save his people, is that they should no more go back from God, v. 18. This is agreeable to Jer. 32:40, "And I will make an everlasting covenant with them, that I will not turn away from them, to do them good; but I will put my fear in their hearts, that they shall not depart from me"; so Jer. 31:32-33, and Ezek. 36:26:27 and 39:29.

V. 1, "shine forth," and v. 3, "cause thy face to shine," [and] so vv. 7 and 19, is agreeable to Ps. 67:1, "and cause his face to shine upon us"; to Is. 60, at the beginning, "Arise, shine; for thy light is come, for the glory of the Lord is risen upon thee"; and Mal. 4[:2], "The Sun of righteousness shall arise," etc., and many other parallel passages that have been before mentioned.

V. 1, "thou that dwellest between the cherubims, shine forth." This is an earnest request of the church and a prophecy that God, that hid himself and veiled his glory, and as it were dwelt in a secret place in those ages of the Mosaic dispensation while the Mosaic temple with its veil was standing, would openly manifest his glory to all his people without a veil, and give free access and communication, and not only to the high priest once a year. God's place where his glory, or Shechinah, dwelt, was in the thick darkness, in the Holy of Holies, within the veil between the cherubims; which was called the secret of God's presence and of his pavilion [Ps. 18:11], the secret of God's tabernacle, and "the secret place of the most High." There his glory was hid first by the veil of the temple, and then with the wings of the cherubims; see Ps. 27:5 and 31:20 and 91:1. This is a prediction of his glorious shining forth, that all might behold it and have access at all times to it, which the high priest had but once a year. This is agreeable to Is. 45:14-15, "they shall fall down unto thee, and make supplication unto thee, saying, Surely God is in thee; and there is none else, there is no God. Verily thou art a God that hidest thyself, O God of Israel, the Savior"; and v. 17, "But Israel shall be saved in the Lord with an everlasting salvation: ye shall not be ashamed nor confounded world without end"; v. 19, "I have not spoken in secret, in a dark place of the earth: I said not to the seed of Jacob, Seek ye me in vain"; v. 22, "Look unto me, and be ye saved, all ye ends of the earth"; and Ps. 89:15, "they shall walk, O Lord, in the light of thy countenance"; and Is. 2:[:5], "O house of Israel, come, let us walk in the light of the Lord"; Is. 60[:1-2, 19], "the glory of the Lord is risen upon thee. [. . .] the Lord shall arise upon thee, and his glory shall be seen upon thee. [. . .] The sun shall be no more thy light by day; . . . the Lord shall be thine everlasting light"; Is. 24[:23], "Then shall the moon be confounded, and the sun ashamed, when the Lord of hosts shall reign on mount Zion, [. . .] before his ancients gloriously"; and Jer. 3:16, etc., "they shall [say] no more, The ark of the covenant," etc., "but Jerusalem shall be the throne of the Lord"; Is.5 52:8, "they shall see eye to eye, when the Lord shall bring again Zion"; Ps. 102:16,6 "When the Lord shall build up Zion, he shall appear in his glory"; Is. 66:18, "I will gather all nations and tongues; and they shall come, and see my glory"; Is. 35:2, "they shall see the glory of the Lord, and the excellency of our God"; and 33:17, "Thine eyes shall see the king in his beauty"; Is. 40:5, "And the glory of the Lord shall be revealed, and all flesh shall see it together."

[Vv.] 1-2, "shine forth. Before Ephraim and Benjamin and Manasseh stir up thy strength, and come and save us," i. e. "Appear for our deliverance from our enemies, under whom we have been in captivity and bondage, as thou didst for the deliverance of [Israel], as thou didst when thou deliveredst Israel from their Egyptian bondage, and leddest them from Egypt to Canaan, when thou wentest before Ephraim, Benjamin and Manasseh, whose camps were westward behind the ark and the tabernacle of the congregation "; see Num. 2:18-23. This shows that the bringing of God's church out of captivity and bondage to foreign enemies is spoken of in this Psalm, agreeable to most other prophecies of the redemption of the Messiah; and 'tis also agreeable to other prophecies of the Messiah, that his redemption should be compared to the going forth of the children of Israel out of Egypt. Is. 11:15-16, "And the Lord shall utterly destroy the tongue of the Egyptian sea; and with his mighty wind he shall shake his hand over the river, and shall smite it in the seven streams, and make men go over dryshod. And there shall be an highway for the remnant of his people, which shall be left, from Assyria; like as it was to Israel in the day when he came up out of the land of Egypt." So Num. 23:33, Mic. 7:15, Is. 43:16-19 and 63:11-13 and 64:3-4, and Jer. 16:14-15 and 23:7-8, and Ezek. 20:35-37, Hos. 2:15.

In that latter expression, "stir up thy strength, and come and save us," v. 2, there seems to be a reference to God's arising, the cloud of glory being taken up, and the ark being taken up and setting forward, when God led the armies of Israel onward in their march towards Canaan to fight with their enemies, when Moses said, Num. 10:35, "Rise up, Lord, let thine enemies be scattered; and let them that hate thee flee before thee." And David and Solomon, on the like occasion of the removing of the ark, express themselves to the like purpose, thus: "Arise, O Lord, thou, and the ark of thy strength," Ps. 132:8, II Chron. 6:41-42.

Vv. 3, 7, 19, "Turn us again, O God of hosts, cause thy face to shine; and we shall be saved," is agreeable to Jer. 31:18, "turn thou me, and I shall be turned."

85. PSALM 24. The 24th Psalm seems, from the beginning to the end, to have respect to the times of the Messiah. This Psalm speaks of the privileges of God's people--such as having an interest in his temple and ordinances, going up to the house of the Lord and standing in his holy place--as not confined to any particular nation, but such as should be bestowed on all that have clean hands and a pure heart, of [all] nations and all the inhabitants of the earth indifferently, for all nations are alike God's: for "the earth is the Lord's, and the fullness thereof; the world, and all that dwell therein," they are alike the work of his hand, as vv. 1-2. And therefore the privileges of God's people shall not be confined to any particular family or generation of men, but whosoever have pure hearts and clean hands, of all that dwell upon the earth, are God's generation or nation, his Israel, and shall be accounted as those that belong to Jacob; as v. 6, "This is the generation of them that seek him, that seek thy face, O Jacob." The like reason is here given why the Gentiles should be called and accepted, as in Ps. 86:9, "All nations whom thou hast made shall come and worship before thee, and shall glorify thy name"; and Ps. 89:11-12, "The heavens are thine, the earth also is thine: as for the world and the fullness thereof, thou hast founded them. The north and the south thou hast created them: Tabor and Hermon shall rejoice in thy name"--i. e. Hermon, a desert mountain, a noted haunt of wild beasts of prey, at a great distance, and the border of the vast deserts of Arabia (see Ps. 42:6 and Cant. 4:8), as well as Tabor, a mountain in the midst of the land of Israel; and Is. 42:4-6, "He shall not fail nor be discouraged, till he have set judgment in the earth: and the isles shall wait for his law. Thus saith God the Lord, he that created the heavens, and stretched them out; he that spread forth the earth, and that which cometh out of it; he that giveth breath unto the people upon it, and spirit to them that walk therein: I the Lord have called thee in righteousness, and will hold thine hand, and will keep thee, and give thee for a covenant of the people, for a light of the Gentiles."

In this Psalm, it is not only foretold that all of every nation that have clean hands and pure hearts, and han't left their soul to vanity nor sworn deceitfully, shall ascend into the hill of the Lord and stand in his holy place. But 'tis implied that such only shall be admitted to this privilege, not any other, though they belong to the nation of Israel; which is agreeable to many forementioned prophecies of the Messiah, as Is. 33:14-17, "The sinners in Zion are afraid; fearfulness hath surprized the hypocrites. . . . He that walketh righteously, and speaketh uprightly; he that despiseth the gain of oppressions, that shaketh his hands from holding of bribes, that stoppeth his ears from hearing of blood, and shutteth his eyes from seeing evil; he shall dwell on high: his place of defense shall be the munitions of rocks: [. . .] Thine eyes shall see the king in his beauty"; Is. 26:2, "Open ye the gates, that the righteous nation which keepeth the truth may enter in" (here, the righteous are represented as being a nation or generation, God's Israel, agreeable to Ps. 24:6); Ezek. 44:9, "Thus saith the Lord, No stranger, uncircumcised in heart, nor uncircumcised in flesh, shall enter into my sanctuary, of any stranger that is among the children of Israel"; Is. 57:13, "he that putteth his trust in me shall [. . .] inherit mine holy mountain." See also Is. 56:6-7 and Ezek. 20:38-40. Ps. 118:19-20, "Open to me the gates of righteousness: I will go into them, and I will praise the Lord: this gate of the Lord, into which the righteous shall enter." See also Is. 53:1 and 60:21, and Ezek. 49:7, 9; Joel 3:17, Ps. 22:25-26, 30 and 69:31-32, 36; Is. 35:8 and 4:3, Ezek. 20:38, Ps. 85:8-9.

And whereas those that have pure hearts, etc., in this Psalm, [are described] as being a distinct race, the nation or generation that are God's people, v. 6, "This is the generation of them of them that seek him, that seek thy face, O Jacob": this is agreeable to Is. 26:2, "Open ye the gates, that the righteous NATION that keepeth the truth may enter in"; and Ps. 22:25-26, "My praise shall be of thee in the great congregation: I will pay my vows before them that fear him. The meek shall eat and be satisfied: they shall praise the Lord that seek him," with v. 30, "A seed shall serve him; it shall be accounted to the Lord for a generation"; and Ps. 69:35-36, "For God will save Zion, and will build the cities of Judah: that they may dwell there, and have it in possession. The seed also of his servants shall inherit it: and they that love his name shall dwell therein."

V. 7, to the end, is a prophecy of the Messiah's coming to Zion, ascending God's holy hill and entering God's holy temple, to sit on his throne there, in order to the setting up of his kingdom in the world. By the connection of the Psalm, we may doubtless conclude that these gates and everlasting doors here spoken of, are the gates and doors of God's holy place that was in his holy hill, spoken, v. 3 foregoing, and the same with the gates mentioned, Is. 26:2, "Open ye the gates, that the righteous nation that keepeth the truth may enter in"; and Ps. 118:19-20, "Open to me the gates of righteousness: I will go into them, and praise the Lord: this gate of the Lord, into which the righteous shall enter." The person here speaking is the Messiah, the same that says, the next verse but one preceding [Ps. 118:17], "I shall not die, but live, and declare the works of the Lord"; and the same of whom it is said, in the next verse but one [v. 22], "The stone which the builders refused is become the head of the corner," and v. 26, "Blessed is he that cometh in the name of the Lord." So that this prophecy is very parallel with that, and also with that, Mal. 3:1, "and the Lord, whom ye seek, shall suddenly come into his temple, even the messenger of the covenant, whom ye delight in"; and Ezek. 43:2-4, "And, behold, the glory of the God of Israel came from the way of the east: and his voice was as the noise of many waters: and the earth shined with his glory. . . . And the glory of the Lord came into the house by the way of the gates whose prospect is towards the east"; and ch. 44:2-3, "This gate shall be shut, it shall not be opened, and no man shall enter in by it, . . . It is for the prince; . . . he shall enter in by the way of the porch of that gate"; and so ch. 46:1-2. There, in Ezekiel, the chief gate of the temple is represented as reserved only for God and the Messiah; so here, the heads of the gates, or the chief gates, are called upon to open themselves to the king of glory.

'Tis an argument that this latter part of the Psalm is prophetical, because it speaks of a temple; but there was no temple in being in David's times. It speaks of "everlasting doors," which could not be meant of the door of the tabernacle, for the very nature of a tabernacle is not to be a durable dwelling, II Sam. 7:6, 10. And this is an argument that this latter part of the Psalm speaks of the times of the Messiah, and that the temple that it refers to is not Solomon's temple, but the temple that was to be in the Messiah's time, because the doors are once and again called everlasting doors: for neither the door of the tabernacle, nor Solomon's temple, were everlasting. The nature of a tabernacle is to be a transient building, made to be taken down; and the doors of the tabernacle, wherein the ark was in David's time, were so far from being everlasting, that they were very near to an end, and ceased when Solomon built the temple. And the doors of Solomon's temple were not everlasting; the destruction of that temple is spoken of in this book of Psalms by Asaph, Ps. 74. But 'tis foretold that the temple that should be in the Messiah's time, should be everlasting, in Ezek. 43:7 and ch. 37:26; which is also implied in very many other places.

'Tis probable this Psalm was penned on occasion of the removing of the ark, and therefore we may the more easily and reasonably suppose that it speaks of the Messiah, because it has before been shown that the 68th and 132nd Psalms, that were penned on the same occasion, do respect the Messiah; and that by God's entering the everlasting doors on his holy hill, spoken of in this Psalm, is meant the same thing as his ascending into his holy hill, Ps. 68:15-18, "The hill of God is as the hill of Bashan; . . . Thou hast ascended on high." And God is mentioned here in this Psalm as entering into the sanctuary on his holy hill, in the same character and in the same manner as in Ps. 68, viz. as the Lord of armies, "the Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle" [Ps. 24:8]; and as returning to Zion in glorious triumph, after a great display of his mighty power in conquering his enemies. So is he represented ascending into the sanctuary in Mt. Zion, in Ps. 68: "Let God arise, let his enemies be scattered: . . . As smoke is driven away, . . . as wax melteth before the fire, so let the wicked perish at the presence of God," vv. 1-2; "Kings of armies did flee apace," v. 12, "Thou hast ascended on high, thou hast led captivity captive," [v. 18].

The repeating that interrogation, "Who is this King of glory?," on this occasion of his return in triumph from a glorious victory over his enemies, together with the answer to it, is agreeable to the like interrogation on the same occasion, and the answer, in Is. 63:1, "Who is this that cometh from Edom, with dyed garments from Bozrah? this that is glorious in his apparel, travelling in the greatness of his strength? I that speak in righteousness, mighty to save."

When the Messiah comes in such glorious triumph and high exaltation and magnificence , the inquiry might well be made with great admiration, "Who is this?," seeing he had before been seen, as is declared in other prophecies, to be "a tender plant, a root out of a dry ground, having "no form nor comeliness" [Is. 53:2]; and as when David, who was but a stripling, returned in glorious triumph to Jerusalem, after the battle with the Philistines with the head of Goliath in his hand, Saul inquired once and again with great admiration, who he was. I Sam. 17:55, "Whose son is this youth?," and v. 58, "Whose son art thou then, thou young man?" And the answer made, "The Lord strong and mighty, the Lord mighty in battle," is much like the song of the daughters of Israel when they came forth out of the cities of Israel to meet David, when he came from the slaughter of the Philistine, saying, "David hath slain his ten thousands" [I Sam. 18:7].

That such an inquiry should be made repeatedly in this Psalm, betokens him a very wonderful person, agreeable to Is. 9[:6], "his name shall be called Wonderful."

If we consider the connection of this latter part of the Psalm with the former part, it seems to be implied that the King of glory here spoken, was in a peculiar manner free from sin, of clean hands and a pure heart, and therefore all others had a right to ascend God's holy hill and to stand in his holy place. This is agreeable to what is said in that parallel place, Is. 63:1, "I that speak in righteousness"; and Is. 53:9, "because [he] had done no violence, neither was any deceit in his mouth"; and v. 11, "my righteous servant"; and Ps. 45:7, "Thou lovest righteousness, and hatest wickedness: therefore God hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness above thy fellows." 87. THE BOOK OF PSALMS, IN GENERAL, IN GREAT PART RELATES TO THE MESSIAH. Add this to §60. This is a great argument the book of Psalms is, a great part of it, prophetical, and also that very much of it relates to the times of the Messiah, even in those parts of it where the speech is in the present or preter tense, viz. that God's people are so often spoken of as being in captivity, and the temple, city and land in desolation, or as being restored from a state of captivity and desolation, in psalms that were penned at a time when no such thing was, nor had lately been.

The 9th Psalm, which [is] a Psalm of David, speaks of [the] destructions of God's people and their cities, even a long-continued desolation, so that their memorial had perished with them, and that, by their enemies that were heathen, vv. 5-6; and of God's people as having been oppressed by them, v. 9; and their blood shed, v. 12; and God's people becoming poor, afflicted and needy by means of those oppressions, v. 18; but their heathen enemies becoming exceeding proud and insolent, v. 20; but, as consequent on this, God's arising to judgment to plead the cause of his people, and utterly destroying their heathen enemies and oppressors, and delivering his people and judging the world in righteousness, and hereby giving great occasion for the rejoicing and praises of those that dwell in Zion, vv. 11, 14; and that God would thus destroy all the wicked of the earth, even all the nations that forget God, v. 17; and that God would thus arise and gloriously appear, and judge and deliver and avenge the cause of his people to their exceeding joy, after their long waiting for him, v. 18; and God's hereupon setting up an everlasting kingdom, vv. 7-8, agreeable to many of the forementioned prophecies of the Messiah. [In] that Psalm that is recorded, I Chron. 16:8-36, which David delivered to Asaph on occasion of the carrying the ark into Zion, David speaks of gathering the people together and delivering them from the heathen, v. 35.

The 14th Psalm, which is also a Psalm of David, speaks of God's looking down from [heaven] to take notice of the affairs of mankind, after [he] had seemed as it were for a long time to neglect them; and particularly to take notice of the oppressions of God's people by their enemies, whereby God's people become their prey, and were wasted and destroyed by 'em without restraint; and God's awaking as it were for the help of his people and as visibly present with them, to rebuke their enemies and put them to flight. So also Ps. 53, which [is] almost the same with Ps. 14. See §51.

And we can't conclude that the 126th and 134th Psalms, that speak plainly of the captivity of Israel as a thing present or past: I say, we can't conclude from the subject matter of these psalms, that they were written in the time of the captivity, or after it; any more the 74th and 79th Psalms, that speak no less expressly of--yea, more particularly of--the captivity and desolation of the land, that, we are told, were penned by Asaph. Those expressions in the beginning of Ps. 126, "When the Lord turned again the captivity of Zion, we were like them that dream. Then was our mouth filled with laughter, and our tongue with singing," is very parallel with those words of David, Ps. 14:7 and 53:6, "When the Lord bringeth back the captivity of his people, Jacob shall rejoice, and Israel shall be glad."

Again, 'tis an argument that the penmen of the psalms, when they speak in the first person singular, have not a chief respect to themselves personally, but speak in the name of the Messiah, or of the church of God; because the penmen of those psalms, when they wrote them, they wrote them for public use, or for the use of the church of Israel in their public praises in the sanctuary. And this is evident by their inscriptions: they are inscribed and directed in their titles, very many of them, to the musicians that were in the sanctuary. And that these inscriptions were prefixed to the psalms by the penmen themselves, may be argued from this, that some of them are inscribed to principal musicians in the sanctuary, by name, that, it appears by the Scripture history, were living at that time and were contemporary with the writers of the psalms; as particularly, Ps. 39 and 62, that were psalms of David, are12 inscribed to Jeduthun, who was one of the leading musicians that David himself had appointed for the public praises of Israel in the sanctuary, as appears by I Chron. 25:1-6. So is Ps. 77, that is a Psalm of Asaph, inscribed to Jeduthun, that was Asaph's contemporary. And as there is such plain reason to conclude that the inscription of these psalms were by the penmen them[selves], so there is no otherwise of such kind of inscriptions as are prefixed to other psalms.

Now it is not to be supposed that David, and Asaph and others, would write their own personal prayer, containing their private complaints and things concerning them only as particular persons, and send 'em to the temple to be sung there as the voice of the whole congregation of Israel to God, in their most public and solemn worship.

Again, as to some of the psalms, wherein the Psalmist speaks in the first person, the very matter of the psalm plainly shows that the Psalmist don't speak in his own name, but in the name of the church of God, or of the head of the church; because in the same psalm wherein the Psalmist speaks of his affliction, distress and his cries for deliverance, he speaks of Zion's deliverance and prosperity as that deliverance that he speaks of, and as an answer to those cries. 88. HOSEA 5:15 AND 6:1-3. That prophecy that we have in Hos. 5:15 and 6:1-3, is a prophecy of what shall be accomplished in the days of the Messiah: for it is a promise of the restoration of Israel from that desolation threatened and foretold in the prediction immediately preceding, in the 4th and 5th chapters; and therefore there is all manner of reason to suppose that it is the same restoration of Israel that is spoken of in the 1st, 2nd and 3rd chapters, which I have before shown to be that which [is] accomplished by the Messiah. In those chapters, there is first a threatening and description of desolation that God will bring on the land, and then follows a promise of a restoration, as here; and 'tis evident that the desolation that is there described, and which there we have a promise of restoration from, is the very same with the desolation described here,15 of which these verses contain a promise of restoration from. The desolation is spoken of in like manner. The mother of the children of Israel is spoken of as being destroyed, ch. 4:5; so ch. 2:2-5, etc. This desolation is spoken of as an utter desolation, ch. 4, v. 3; so ch. 2:6-14. The sin for which the desolation is threatened is the same, and spoken of in the same manner, viz. whoredom of the church of Israel against God her husband. Compare ch. 4:10 to the end and ch. 5:3-4, 7. 'Tis here in like manner represented that God shall begin to afflict and punish the children of Israel, as seeking in vain for help from her lovers. Compare ch. 5:13 with ch. 2:6-7. Judah and Ephraim, or Israel, are both spoken of distinctly in the threatenings of desolation here, as in the foregoing chapters; and it is foretold here that the desolation of Israel or Ephraim shall be first, and that after Ephraim is destroyed, Judah shall receive great favor from God, ch. 4:15-17, as in ch. 1:6-7.

Nothing can be more evident than that it is the same destruction that is threatened here as in the preceding chapters, and therefore undoubtedly the restoration here spoken of from the same desolation, is the same restoration. In the preceding chapters we see the manner of the Prophet, first to foretell the people's desolation, and then to foretell their restoration by the Messiah; and then to return again and speak of the same desolation, and then to speak again of the redemption of the Messiah; and so again and again. And so 'tis evident he goes on to do in these chapters, which are a continuation of and further amplification of the same prophecies.

And further, the restoration is represented in the same manner: first, by their way being hedged up, and the people being brought to see that none could help them, and despairing in the help of such as they sought help from (compare ch. 5:13-14 with ch. 2:6-7); and their being brought earnestly to seek God by sore affliction, and so having comforts restored (compare ch. 5:14-15 and [ch.] 6, at the beginning, with ch. 2:14-15); their hope restored in a way of their putting away sin the Achan, or troubler, and returning from sin to God (compare ch. 5:15, ch. 6:1 with ch. 2:15.

The restoration of God's people here foretold is represented as a resurrection from the dead, ch. 6:2, as is very frequent in prophecies of the restoration of the Messiah. God's going forth at this time is represented as the rising of the morning, agreeable to II Sam. 23[:4], "he shall be as the light of the morning"; and Is. 60 at the beginning; and Mal. 4[:2], "the Sun of righteousness shall arise," etc.; Ps. 46:5, "God shall help her, when the morning appears," as in the original. Ch. 6:3, it is said, "he shall come unto us as the rain, as the latter and former rain unto the earth," which is agreeable to David's last words, "as the dew," etc.; and Ps. 72[:6], "He shall come down as the rain on the mown grass."

91. DANIEL 9:24 TO THE END. I am come now to consider that great prophecy of the Messiah in the 9th chapter of Daniel, [v.] 24 to the end, which is one of the most remarkable and plain of all in the Old Testament. I have hitherto called the person spoken of in forementioned prophecies "the Messiah," for distinction's sake, meaning thereby no more than the great king that was spoken of in prophecies mentioned before; which person, in some of the forementioned prophecies, is called God's Messiah or Anointed. But it now remains to be shown that the person here called by the name of the Messiah or the Anointed, as his proper and distinguishing name, is the same great king so often spoken of in prophecies that have been already taken notice of.

And here, first, the name of the MESSIAH or the Anointed. If we consider what must be understood by such a name, according to the common acceptation and use of such like appellations in the Old Testament, viz. some person chosen and singled out by God to some great office, dignity and honor, and for some eminent service and benefit to God's Israel, and called and appointed of God to that dignity and work, and peculiarly fitted of him for it: but who can that great person be that is foretold in this prophecy, as one that should arise in ages to come in Israel, that is in these respects eminently and above all others called the Messiah or the Anointed of God, but the person spoken of in forementioned prophecies? What other great person that was to arise in Israel in after ages, could there be more worthy of such a name, than that great king so often spoken of in other prophecies as the greatest king of Israel, their greatest deliverer and redeemer, captain and protector, the grand instrument of their good and minister of divine blessings to them, spoken of all along and revealed by God as the grand promise to that people from the first calling of Abraham to that time; that is called God's elect, in whom his soul delights, Is. 42:1; God's servant, whom he had chosen, Is. 43:10; to whom God had said, "Thou art my servant, in whom I will be glorified," and one that God had formed "from the womb to be his servant, to bring Jacob again to him, [. . .] to raise up the tribes of Jacob, and to restore the preserved of Israel, and to be "for a light to the Gentiles" and God's "salvation to the ends of the earth," and one that God would choose and would give for "a covenant of the people, to establish the earth," and one that God called from the womb and made mention of his name from the bowels of his mother, Is. 49:1-9; one that God had called in righteousness, and would give "for a covenant of the people, and light of the Gentiles," Is. 42:6; and one that was in an eminent manner his servant and the messenger whom he sent, and one that is God's king that he had set on his holy hill of Zion, and that by an immutable decree he had appointed to the dignity of his son, Ps. 2; and that was the stone that God had laid in Zion, Is. 28; and the son of man whom he had made strong for himself, Ps. 80:15, 17; one that God had found, and chosen, and exalted out of the people to be his servant, and that he would make his first born, higher than the kings of the earth? If there was any other greater person that God intended to raise up for greater benefit to his people, and more worthy on these accounts to be called, by way of eminency, THE MESSIAH or THE ANOINTED, why are all other prophecies silent about him?

Again, if we consider what those offices were to which persons were introduced by anointing among the Jews, it will confirm that he that is spoken of in forementioned prophecies, is this person here spoken, to whom belongs, in a peculiar manner and above all others, the name of the ANOINTED. And they were kings and priests and prophets, but the Messiah is ever spoken of as a King, and THE KING, THE GREAT KING that God had appointed to have the dominion over his people and over all nations, and to have it alone, and to have it forever. And he is spoken of also as the great priest of God, the high priest, a priest not only coming before the throne of God in the Holy of Holies once a year, as the high priests of old, but a priest on God's throne, Zech. 6; and consecrated and established in the priesthood immediately by God himself, and with remarkable solemnity, and that not only to be above all other priests but to be priest forever, to have no successor, so that there should be no high priest but he--Ps. 110[:4], "The Lord hath sworn, and will not repent, Thou art a priest for ever after the order of Melchizedek." And also the grand PROPHET that God would raise up in Israel like to Moses [Acts 3:22], and far above Moses. See §44.

Again, if we consider what the Old Testament teaches us was signified by the anointing oil, it further confirms that the person spoken of in forementioned prophecies is this great person here foretold that is, by way of eminency, called the Anointed: and that is the Spirit of God. 'Tis evident that the Spirit of God was signified by oil of old. It is apparent by Zech. 4. There, Zechariah has a vision of two olive trees conveying their oil through golden pipes into the bowl of the candlestick; and the angel, in the 5th and 6th verses, explains this vision to Zechariah as signifying the communication of the Spirit of God to the church of Israel, to help them and cause them to prosper in the affairs of the temple, and his worship and service. "Then the angel that talked with me answered and said unto me, Knowest thou not what these be? And I said, No, my lord. Then he answered and spoke unto me, saying, This is the word of the Lord unto Zerubbabel, saying, Not by might, nor by power, but by my spirit, saith the Lord of hosts." And it is evident, by Is. 61:1, that by anointing a person to an office, was signified the qualifying of him for his office, by putting God's spirit on him: "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me; because the Lord hath anointed me." But he that is spoken of in forementioned prophecies, is spoken of as one that should, in an eminent and peculiar manner, have [the] Spirit of God put upon [him], as in the last mentioned text, Is. 61:1, "The Spirit of the Lord God is upon me; because the Lord hath anointed me"; and so Is. 11:2, "the spirit of the Lord shall rest upon him, the spirit of wisdom and understanding, the spirit of counsel and of might, the spirit of knowledge and of the fear of the Lord"; and Is. 42:1, "Behold my servant, whom I uphold; mine elect, in whom my soul delighteth; I have put my spirit upon him"; Is. 59:19-21, "the spirit of the Lord God shall lift up a standard against him. And the Redeemer shall come to Zion," etc. "As for me, this is my covenant with them, [. . .] My spirit that is upon thee, and my words which I have put in thy mouth," etc.

Again, there is no person that we read of, in all the history of the Old Testament, that seems to be spoken as being so eminently the Lord's "Anointed," as "David," and therefore who is so likely to be the person intended by the great, future prince of God's people here spoken of, that is called, by way of eminency, THE ANOINTED, THE PRINCE, as that greatest of all kings, so often foretold in other prophecies and spoken of there under the name of david; being very often called by that name, as being the great antitype of David, and is called the "rod out of the stem of Jesse," and the Branch "out of his roots" [Is. 11:1]; and the righteous Branch growing up unto David [Jer. 33:15], and a Branch out of his roots; and the great king that shall sit on the throne of his father David marginal

Lastly, the person spoken of in forementioned prophecies is expressly said to be anointed, and to be anointed above other princes, and one whom God hath anointed, and is spoken of as he that is by way of eminency God's ANOINTED or MESSIAH, as Is. 61:1, "the Lord hath anointed me"; Ps. 45:7, "thy God hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness above thy fellows"; Ps. 2:[1-]2, "Why [do] the heathen rage [. . .] against the Lord, and against his Messiah," or "anointed"; v. 6, "Yet have I anointed my king on my holy hill of Zion" (as it is in the original); Ps. 20:6, "Now I know that the Lord saveth his anointed"; Ps. 89:20, "I have found David my servant; with my holy oil have I anointed him."

And as the person here prophesied of is called the Messiah, so he is called THE PRINCE. The word is dygin;, which properly signifies "leader." The word is used in Scripture to signify the same as "king," as I Kgs. 14:7, "Go, tell Jeroboam, Thus saith the Lord God of Israel, Forasmuch as I exalted thee from among the people, and made thee prince over my people Israel," where the word in the original is the same as here. So I Kgs. 16:1-2, "Then the word of the Lord came to Jehu the son of Hanani against Baasha, saying, Forasmuch as I exalted thee out of the dust, and made thee prince over my people Israel"; and Prov. 28:16, "The prince that wanteth understanding is also a great oppressor." But who can this great, anointed prince be but that same great king everywhere spoken of by foregoing prophets, that God should raise to deliver and reign over his people in the latter days?

In the angel's manner of speaking here, there is implied a plain reference to foregoing prophecies of this Messiah, the prince, that he speaks of. The names are made use as names well known already, and the person is spoken of as a person that Daniel and God's people expected from foregoing prophecies; and he only declares to him how long it will before he will appear, and what will follow upon it. Daniel had the books of the foregoing prophets in his hands, as appears by v. 2, where he read much of a certain great king or prince that God would anoint and send for the salvation of his people. He had been earnestly seeking to God for the redemption and salvation of his people. God, in answer and great favor to him as a man greatly beloved, makes known to him for his encouragement something more particularly concerning the coming of this glorious redeemer and head of prosperity and happiness of his people than had hitherto been known, and particularly concerning the time of his coming. But what other great, future, anointed prince and savior of Israel is foretold or promised by foregoing prophets, but he that is spoken of in those prophecies that I have already observed? The Messiah is called by the same name, dygin;, in Isaiah. Is. 55:4, "Behold, I have given him for a witness of the people, a leader and commander of the people." The word translated "leader" is the same as here.

There is probably also some reference to what God had before revealed to Daniel. Daniel before had had a vision, ch. 7, of a glorious prince, "one like the Son of man" coming with clouds of heaven to the seat of "the Ancient of days," and of their being given to him by that Ancient of days "dominion and glory and a kingdom, that all people, nations and languages should serve him"; whose dominion "is an everlasting dominion, which shall not pass away, and his kingdom that which shall not be destroyed"; under whom "the people of the saints of the most High God" should possess "the kingdom and dominion, and greatness of the kingdom under the whole heaven," and should reign forever, even forever and ever. Doubtless, this glorious vision deeply impressed and very much took up Daniel's mind, who was so concerned for the welfare of God's people. And therefore when the angel tells him how long it will be till the great anointed prince appears, he must naturally understand it of this great prince that he had this vision [of]. And indeed, the vision leaves no room for any other prince over God's people in their future prosperous day: for he is represented as reigning alone absolutely over all the inhabitants, and reigning forever without his kingdom's ever passing away, or ever having any succession.

And besides, 'tis signified to Daniel at this time that this that is here foretold, even the coming of this Messiah, the prince, and what he should accomplish at his coming, was the grand event so often exhibited in foregoing visions and prophecies, and that had been the main subject of prophecies. "Seventy weeks are determined . . . to bring in everlasting righteousness, and to seal up the vision and prophecy, and to anoint the most Holy" [v. 24]; "to seal up the vision and prophecy," i. e. to finish it, or bring the prophecy to its grand event and accomplishment, in that which it chiefly and ultimately aimed at, and to accomplish that which is as it were the sum of all prophecy. Ezek. 28:12, "Thou sealest up the sum, full of wisdom, and perfect in beauty."

The Messiah's being here called "the most Holy," is agreeable to Ps. 16, "nor suffer thine Holy One to see corruption"; Ps. 45:7, "Thou lovest righteousness, and hatest wickedness: therefore God, thy God, {hath anointed thee with the oil of gladness above thy fellow}"; his being called God's righteous servant [Is. 53:11], and being often said to be "just, ruling in the fear of God" [II Sam. 23:3], reigning in righteousness, being the righteous Branch [Jer. 33:15], God's being "well pleased for his righteousness' sake" [Is. 42:21]; his having "done no violence," nor guile being found in his mouth [Is. 53:9], and his having a pure heart and clean hands [Ps. 24:4], etc.

That, v. 24, "to finish the transgression, and to make an end of sins, and to make reconciliation for iniquity," is agreeable to Jer. 31:34, "for I will forgive their iniquity, and remember their sin no more"; and 33:8, "And I will cleanse them from all their iniquity, whereby they have sinned against me; and I will pardon all their iniquities, whereby they have sinned, and whereby they have transgressed against me"; Mic. 7:19, "he will subdue our iniquities; and thou wilt cast all their all their sins into the depth of the sea"; Is. 43:25, "I, even I, am he that blotteth out thy transgressions [. . .], and will not remember thy sins"; and Is. 44:22, "I have blotted out, as a thick cloud, thy transgressions, and, as a cloud, thy sins"; and many other places.

"And to bring in everlasting righteousness." Here seems to be a reference to what had been often foretold by the former prophets, concerning a future bringing in of everlasting righteousness, probably in such passages as those in Is. 56:1, "my salvation is near to come, and my righteousness to be revealed"; and 46:13, "I will bring near my righteousness; it shall not be far off, and my salvation shall not tarry: and I will place salvation in Zion for Israel my glory"; and 51:5-6, "My righteousness is near; my salvation is gone forth, . . . the heavens shall vanish away like smoke, and the earth shall wax old like a garment, and they that dwell therein shall die in like manner: but my salvation shall be for ever, and my righteousness shall not be abolished"; and v. 8, "For the moth shall eat them up like a garment, and the worm shall eat them like wool: but my righteousness shall be for ever, and my salvation from generation to generation"; and 32:17, "the work of righteousness shall be peace; and the effect of righteousness quietness and assurance for ever"; and 61:11, "For as the earth bringeth forth her bud, and as the garden causeth the things that are sown in it to spring forth; so the Lord will cause righteousness and praise to spring forth before all nations"; and in the next verse, viz. ch. 62:1, "For Zion's sake will I not hold my peace, and for Jerusalem's sake I will not rest, until the righteousness thereof go forth as brightness, and the salvation thereof as a lamp that burneth"; Ps. 85:10-11, "righteousness and peace have kissed each other. Truth shall spring out of the earth; and righteousness shall look down from heaven"; v. 13, "Righteousness shall go before him; and shall set us in the way of his steps." And if we take the righteousness spoken of here, that the Messiah brings in, more especially for that which is reckoned to his people in their justification, which he brings in, as well as makes an end of sins and makes reconciliation for iniquity, this is agreeable to Is. 53:11, "by his knowledge shall my righteous servant justify many; for he shall bear their iniquities"; and Jer. 23:5-6, "and this is the name by which he shall be called, The Lord Our Righteousness"; and Is. 45:24-25, "Surely, one shall say, in the Lord have I righteousness and strength: . . . In the Lord shall all the seed of Israel be justified, and shall glory."

If we take finishing the transgression, and making an end of sins, and bringing in everlasting righteousness, for the taking away sin itself from God's people and giving them everlasting, inherent righteousness by sanctification, this is agreeable to Jer. 32:39-40, "And I will give them one heart, and one way, that they may fear me FOR EVER, for the good of them, and their children after them: . . . I will put my fear in their hearts, and they shall not depart from me"; and ch. 31:33-34, "But this shall be the covenant that I will make with the house of Israel; After those days, saith the Lord, I will put my law in their inward parts, and write it in their hearts; and will be their God, and they shall be my people. And they shall teach no man every man his neighbor," etc. To the like purpose, Ezek. 11:19-20 and Ezek. 36:25-27, "Then will I sprinkle clean water upon you, and ye shall be clean: from all your filthiness, and from all your idols, will I cleanse you. A new heart also will I give you, and a new spirit will I put within you: and I will take away the stony heart out of your flesh, and I will give you an heart of flesh. And I will put my spirit within you, and cause you to walk in my statutes, and ye shall keep my judgments, and do them"; v. 29, "I will also save you from all your uncleanness." See also ch. 37:23, and Is. 32:16-17 and 45:8 and 61:3, and innumerable other places.

In this prophecy 'tis foretold that, about the end of the seventy weeks, reconciliation should be made for iniquity and the Messiah should "be cut off, but not for himself," and that he should so make an end of sin, and finish the transgression, and complete reconciliation for iniquity, that he should make "the sacrifice and oblation to cease," vv. 24, 26-27. 'Tis agreeable to other prophecies that he should be cut off, or die. [This] is agreeable to Ps. 22:15, "thou hast brought me into the dust of death," and is implied in other prophecies where it is said, "my flesh shall rest in hope" [Ps. 16:9], etc., "Thy dead men shall live, with my dead body shall they come" [Is. 26:19]. By this expression, the Messiah shall be "cut off," is implied that he should die, and also that he should die a violent death, which is agreeable to Is. 53, where the great, future Savior foretold by Isaiah, his dying a violent and terrible death, is very plainly and fully foretold. V. 5, "he was wounded" and "bruised"; v. 7, "He was oppressed, and he was afflicted, . . . he is brought as a lamb to the slaughter"; v. 8, "He is taken from prison and from judgment: . . . he was cut off out of the land of the living," where the like expression is used, as here in this place in Daniel--he was "cut off"; v. 9, "he made his grave with the wicked, and with the rich in his death"; v. 10, "it pleased the Lord to bruise him; and put him to grief: . . . thou shalt make his soul an offering for sin"; v. 12, "he hath poured out his soul unto death."

It is said that he should be "cut off, not for himself," i. e. for others, as a sacrifice and oblation, making an end of sin and making reconciliation for iniquity. This is agreeable to Is. 53, in a great variety of expressions there.

And whereas here 'tis intimated that this sacrifice for sin should put an end to all other sacrifices and oblations, and cause the sacrifices of Moses' Law to cease: this necessarily follows from other prophecies, for 'tis there foretold that he should be priest forever, and should have no successor; and in the 53rd of Isaiah 'tis foretold that his own blood, or life, or soul is the sacrifice that this priest should offer for sin, and that this sacrifice should be acceptable with God as a satisfaction for sin, so that we should have peace by it, and should be healed, v. 5; and that "the iniquity of us all" should be laid upon him, v. 6; and that he should justify many, because he should bear their iniquities, v. 11. And 'tis there implied that he should offer this sacrifice but once; for 'tis promised, vv. 10-11, that if he should make his soul once "an offering for sin," he should thenceforward "prolong his days" and should "see of the travail of his soul, and be satisfied." And 'tis in other prophecies implied that the sacrifices of the Law of Moses should cease in the Messiah's days, as Is. 66 at the beginning, "Thus saith the Lord, The heaven is my throne, . . . where is the house that ye build unto me? . . . He that killeth an oxen," etc.; Ps. 50:7, etc., "Hear, O my people, and I will speak; O Israel, and I will testify against thee: I am God, even thy God. I will not reprove thee for thy sacrifices or thy burnt offerings, to have been continually before me. I will take no bullock out of thy house, nor he goats out of thy folds. . . . Will I eat the flesh of bulls, or drink the blood of goats?"; Mic. 6:6-8, "Wherewith shall I come before the Lord, and bow myself before the high God? shall I come before him with burnt offerings, with calves of a year old? Will the Lord be pleased with thousands of rams," etc. "He hath showed thee, O man, what is good; and what doth the Lord require of thee, but to do justly, and to love mercy, and to walk humbly with thy God?"

92. DANIEL 12:1-3. The three first verses of the 12th chapter of Daniel, seem very plainly to be a prophecy of the appearing of the Messiah. "At that time shall Michael stand, the great prince which standeth for the children of thy people," etc. This seems to be the same great, anointed prince spoken of in the 9th chapter. Though the word in the original is not the same, yet both are well rendered "prince," as being much of the same signification. And the word is the same that is used for the Messiah, Is. 9:6-7, "to us a child is born, to us a son is given: and the PRINCIPALITY shall be upon his shoulders" (as it is in the Hebrew, the word being of the same root with "prince" here): "and his name shall be called Wonderful, Counselor, The mighty God, The everlasting Father, The PRINCE of Peace" (the same word as in this place).

The signification of the word "Michael" agrees well to the Messiah, viz. the LOWLINESS of God, or the poverty of God, or the smiting of God. This agrees with what is said of the humiliation, lowliness, affliction and sufferings of the Messiah in other prophecies.

The compellation here given him of the great prince well agrees to the Messiah, who is said to be one that is mighty, and to whom God says, "Gird thy sword on thy thigh, O most mighty" [Ps. 45:3], and is everywhere represented as so great a person. And 'tis not likely that such a compellation would be given to any mere man or created angel.

And his being called the great prince that "standeth for the children of thy people," is a further evidence that 'tis the Messiah that is meant. Who can this great prince be that stands for the children of God's people, but the person that is elsewhere spoken of21 as he that shall rule over men [II Sam. 23:3]; that most mighty one that shall gird his sword on his thigh, and ride forth because of truth, meekness and righteousness [Ps. 45:3-4]; and that shall "judge the poor of the people, and save the children of the needy, and break in pieces the oppressor," and "deliver the needy when the[y] crieth, the poor also, and him that hath no helper," and "redeem their soul from deceit and violence" [Ps. 72:4, 12]; who is their Immanuel, and the Lord their righteousness, that shall "stand for an ensign of the people" [Is. 11:10]; and one that is lifted up as a standard against the enemy when he comes in like a flood [Is. 59:19]; and is the king of God's people that shall come to them, having salvation [Zech. 9:9]; is the Redeemer that shall come to Zion [Is. 59:20]; is the defense of God's people like an "hiding place from the wind, a covert from the tempest, as rivers of water in a dry place, and as the shadow of a great rock in a weary land [Is. 32:2]; that greatly loves God's people and unites himself to them, and interests himself much in their concerns, so he betroths them unto him and rejoices over them, as the bridegroom over the bride [Is. 62:5]; and is the angel of the covenant that God's people delight in [Mal. 3:1]; and the angel of his presence that in his love and in his pity redeems them [Is. 63:9]; who of old appeared to Joshua with a sword drawn in his hand, as the captain of the Lord's host [Josh. 5:13]; who stands as the shepherd of God's people, to feed them and to defend them, and stands as their intercessor, to make "intercession for the transgressors" [Is. 53:12]. 'Tis impossible that a compellation should agree better to the character of any person than this compellation, "The great prince that standeth for the children of thy people," does the character of the Messiah, as exhibited in the prophecies of the Old Testament. So ch. 10, at the last verse, he is called their prince. Now there is nothing in any part of the Scripture that looks as if any one particular angel of the created angels did, above all others, interest himself in the affairs of God's Israel, and was appointed over 'em, and had the care of them committed to him as their prince. And besides, this--viz. that Michael was their prince, that stood as it were alone for their defense against their enemies--is there mentioned as a thing much noted in the Scripture or in other writings of the prophets, as it can be understood of nothing else. "But I will show thee that which is noted in the scripture of truth: and there is none that holdeth with me in these things, but Michael your prince." There is nothing in the following words that this can be applied to, and writings of proceeding prophets do indeed speak abundantly of this; but they say nothing of any created angels that is their prince, that is set peculiarly over them and stands peculiarly for them.

And whereas Michael, ch. 10:13, is called the first of the chief princes, this is to represent him as the head of all principality, and is agreeable to Ps. 89:4, "I will make him my firstborn, higher than the kings of the earth." This name, according to the Hebrew idiom, signifies much the same thing as "prince of princes," but only expressed more strongly: for in that language, the prince of princes signifies as much as a Prince in the superlative degree, or the chief of all princes, as the Holy of Holies signifies the most holy of all. But here the expression is stronger; 'tis not only the chief of princes, but the chief of the chief of princes. Therefore, there is much reason to think that the person here spoken, is the same with that spoken of, ch. 8:25, "he shall also stand up against the Prince of princes"; by which undoubtedly a divine person, and not any mere creature, is intended.

Here Michael, this great prince that stands for the people of God, is represented as delivering them in that [that] is, in a peculiar and eminent manner, the time of the trouble of Israel above all others, which is agreeable to what is represented of the Messiah, Jer. 30:3, etc., "For, lo, the days come, saith the Lord, that I will bring again the captivity of my people Israel and Judah"; vv. 5-9, "For thus saith the Lord; We have heard a voice of trembling, of fear, and not of peace. Ask ye now, and see whether a man doth travail with child? wherefore do I see every man with his hands on his loins, as a woman in travail, and all faces are turned into paleness? Alas! for that day is great, so that none is like it; it is even the time of Jacob's trouble; but he shall be saved out of it. . . . in that day, saith the Lord of hosts, I will break his yoke from off thy neck, . . . But they shall serve the Lord their God, and David their king, whom I will raise up unto them."

Here the resurrection of the dead is spoken of as attending Michael's appearing, which has often been observed to be frequently spoken of as what will attend the coming of the Messiah. And particularly, as here [v. 1], Michael is said to stand up, and the dead to rise; so Job says [19:25], "I know that my Redeemer liveth, and that he shall stand at the latter day over the earth" (as the words would have been more properly rendered): "and though after my skin," etc.

V. 3, "they that be wise shall shine as the brightness of the firmament." This is agreeable to Is. 60, at the beginning, "Arise, shine; for thy light is come, . . . kings shall come to thy light, and princes to the brightness of thy rising";22 and v. 15, "I will make thee an eternal excellency, a joy of many generations."
