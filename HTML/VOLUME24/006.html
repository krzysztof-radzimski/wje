Appendix C. Financial Accounts of Edwards' Children

The "Children's Accounts," located on the closing pages of the MS numbered as pp. 902–4, raise as many questions as they answer. One must speculate how Edwards is "borrowing" from his children in some cases at a point when they were still toddlers or infants. Perhaps he was taking funds from an amount that he and Sarah set aside for an inheritance or for an "allowance," though the modern notion of an allowance seems inappropriate especially because of the age of the children at the point when some of the records were entered. If a separate ledger of such accounts exists, it has not been located or identified. Perhaps the funds were ultimately intended as a dowry for the daughters or as an asset to be used for the education of the sons. These accounts reflect the care Edwards took to be accurate and to repay in full the debts to his children. They also cast light on the complexity of the currency situation in eighteenth-century New England.On currencies in colonial America, see John J. McCusker, Money and Exchange in Europe and America, 1600–1775: A Handbook (2nd ed. Chapel Hill, Univ. of North Carolina Press, 1992).

-- 1266 --

The teaspoons we got for our three eldest children came to 1£—1s—9d. for each of the three couples, making and all. The silver came to 12s—9d., and the making to 9s. Sarah is debtor 12–9, the price of the silver, which is to be subtracted out of what we have used of her money, the price of the making, 9s, being given for the use of the money and of the spoons. The other two children paid 3s—11d. of their own silver that was not set down so that they are made debtors but 8s—10d., which is to be subtracted from the account of money borrowed of them, the 9 shillings for the making being also given them for the use of the money and spoons.
