C45. Samuel Hopkins of Springfield to Hannah Edwards, March 9, 1736 C045.S.Hopkins-H.Edwards 3-9-36 Samuel Hopkins of Springfield

C045. Samuel Hopkins to Hannah Edwards,3/9/36. BRBL, f. 1525.

springfield March 9. 1735/6

Dear sister

were I possesed of half the tenderness to, and Inclination to serve, my fellow Creatures, y t appears in you, it would oblige me to Exert myself for your help in ye present situation of your affairs. and such a degree of it I seal to you, as draws from me this letter; which please to Accept in good part; and as an Evidence of my Concern for you.

I love you dearly as a sister (without flattering as deceit I speak it) And therefore was possesed of no small degree of Joy and satisfaction when I saw you, a little while ago, set at liberty as a Roe from ye hunter, and as a bird from ye hand of ye fowler: and in ye same proportion my sorrow and uneasiness now Arise, to see you again about to be worried and Entangled in ye same snare y t you was in before.

Possibly there there are but few if any who have a fuller understand ing of your Case than I have: And so far as I Can Judge I think you are in duty obliged still to Assert your own liberty: And totally to dismiss ye affair that now perplexes you, let who will say what they will. for you Can’t freely and of Choice proceed to marriage, and it is inconsistant with ye law of Marriage, and of Nature too, y t you should proceed, if it is not freely and of Choice —— y t you Can’t freely and of Choice proceed, is what I know not only from your Conduct, but also from your own mouth: And your own reason will oblige you to Conclude y t it must be of Choice, or not atall. And for y t Reason you ought to put an End to ye affair.

Again to keep ye thing in hand perplexes and kils you, but to dismis it restores you to ye Enjoyment of yourself and friends. for ye truth of all this I refer you to your own Experience: when you have had ye afair in hand, for yours past, has it not been a Constant uneasieness to you, has it not wracked nature, and Exhasted your spirit and life, so y t you have appeared like a fadeing flower? on ye other hand have you not returned to ye vigor and spriteliness of youth, when it has been dismised: therefore ye Law of self preservation obliges you to put an End to it

Again you are obliged In Justice to mr R-1

to do it: for so long as his hopes are kept alive, and not fully Answered, he will be in Constant uneasieness and perplexity; but were the affair Entirely Ended, it would (I am Confident) restore him to himself. Hope defered makes ye heart sick. but hope Cut off, often works ye Cure. And so far as you have made ye Experiment have you not reason to think y t this is ye Case? has he not had ye possession of himself better when he has looked upon ye Case as lost than any other time for years past. In short ye Case seems to be thus, when you are nearest to one another you are most uneasie; when you are furthest apart ye better you Enjoy yourselves. And I think ye Conclusion must be this, part and live, or marry and die.

yea further I think y t God in his providence has made ye way of your duty plain before you, by Continuing in you such a disinclination to the thing, after so many Endeavours to Reconcile yourself to it. is not ye hand of God in this? and ought you not to look upon it as a voice from heaven, forbiding ye thing? Can you go Contrary to it, without running Counter to ye Call of divine providence?

I suppose you will grant y t in ordinary Cases that must govern, marginal And a person’s inclination or disinclination must determine ye matter but you will say y t yours is an Exempt Case, And make such an Enquiry as this.

Q. What shall a young woman do if she, if she really fears y t a denyal will undo the man? upon which my are I will give you my tho’ts as Clearly as I Can. —— And I will in ye first place suppose the worst, viz. y t ye man is undone if he be denyed. And yet I think it is your duty to deny him: for you are undone if you have him, and there is no Law, divine or humane y t obliges a man to love his Neighbour better than himself. And if one of you must be undone, and you have ye Casting voice in ye Case, I think you ought to free yourself before any other person.

Again you are both undone if you marry, and better one than two. But in ye next place, there is not half ye probability of his being undone by a denyal, as there is of your being undone by a Compliance. for it has often been observed, and I suppose is a Just observation, that those men, who will needs die if they are bagg’d, and will go to ye Ends of ye Earth and never be heard of more &c. make the worst Husbands if they gain their point, and make but little of baggs if they are Cast upon them. — look round you, dear sister, yea Enquire of ye best man you Can find, and give me an instance if you Can of a woman marrying a man to save him from being undone, who has not undone herself by marrying of him. And these reasons may be given why such matches are allways unhappy. (1) the person y t is so unmanly, as to Compel a woman to have him, by such a plea, has not anough of ye man in him to make a good husband. (2) a man who so over values ye Creature will Certainly fail of haveing his Expectations Answered, in ye Enjoyment of ye object he is so much in love with; And therefore his passion will End in ye uneasieness of a disappointment. and Consequently be in an uneasie posture, y t will render them both unhappy. yea (3) he will always be Jelous y t ye woman dont love him after marriage, who he knows did not before. and (4) when the heat of his irregular passion (which cannot last long) is Cooled, and abated, by Enjoyment, it will, very likely, turn into resentment. And then where is ye poor woman? she had better be a slave.

I seriously tell you, my sister, y t I had rather see you marry a man who professes y t he don’t love you above other women, than to see you marry ye same man in such a violent passion. for one who is so Indifferent will find his Expectations vastly more than answered, in ye Enjoyment of such so fine a woman. but he whose Expectations are raised so Exceeding high, will be ye subject of an unhappy disappointment, even tho he marry ye best of women. And to say ye best of it, such violent passion, is a fearful overvaluing ye Creature. And gives Just reason to fear y t a blessing will not attend it.

upon ye whole, is there not ten times the danger of your being both undone by marrying, than there is of his being undone by being denyed, or rather by his being kept off now he is denyed.

but further such persons if they are denyed, bear it as well as other men, and make as little or less of it, as any persons whoever. when did you know a man prove as good as his word, who was so very fond of dying for love?

I grant indeed y t when a man is deeply in love, it is a heavy affliction, to have a bagg forced upon him, this is what Experience as well as observation has taught me. And when such an affliction is fresh and strong upon a person, he may think it will be too hard for him. but as in other afflictions so in this, ye weight of it decreases by length of time, till it is worn off. And such violent passion goes off very Easiely. and ye subjects of it have in pretence, if not really, rejoyced y t they have been denyed. to ye truth of this your Eldest sister Can testifie. for when she marginal was as much afraid of ruining mr T 2

as you be of undoing mr R. it proved otherwise, and he pretended he was glad of it, when they were parted. upon ye whole, I don’t suppose you have any reason to fear y t a bagg would prove mortal in ye Case.

I will allow y t you know more of mr R. than I do. but you must allow y t I am better acquainted with our sex than you be. and I will give you this one direction, to help you in your Conduct towards for ye future. when you see a man about to die for love. you must attribute one half of his mallady to Conceit, and if he be a man y t is inclined to ye spleen, it must be ye largest half. one quarter (if he be a pretty honest man) you must attribute to dissimulation; and the quarter that is left wil never kil him. tho I am Joculer in this passage, yet there is a great deal of truth in it. And I am sensible, by my own Experience, y t men Can’t die by ye bagg half so Easiely as they think for.

possibly you will Enquire further whether your keeping Company so long, is not an obligation upon your’s to which I answer That it is impossible y t this should oblige you to go Contrary to your own Inclinations, as I observed before, but then,

Whose fault is it y t you have kept Company so long? has it not proceeded from his pressing importunely? and Can that be an obligation upon you suppose a man Came to me to buy a farm, and I tell him I will Consider of it a week. at ye weeks End he Comes, and I tell him that I will not sell it don’t intend to sel it. he urges me y t I will Consider of it a month longer, and upon his importunity I do it, At ye End of y t month he urges me to Consider of it a month more, and so on. And I finally tell him, I think it not best to sell my farm. would not you think that man out of his wits if he should urge y t I was under obligations to sel it to him, because ye thing had been so long in hand? when it was by his Importunity only y t I had it under Consideration so long.

if you Enquire, whether you ought not to take still more time to see if you Can reconcile yourself to it. I think you have been much too long upon y t Already, and y t you Can’t be Justified in making any further tryal. it looks to me too much like a degree of self murder, to keep yourself in that uneasie posture any longer.

if you should ask me how you shall put an End to it. my advice would be this. y t you should Cut him off Entirely from haveing any Conversation with you, for this, seems to me to be ye only way y t is left you. for if you give him opportunity of Conversation with you, he will look upon ye thing as yet in hand, say what you will.

if you object, or any other should object and say, you must treat him with Civility, it will not do to be so harsh and severe with him, &c.

I answer. y t if you should break ye broom staff over his head and bid him never say a word to you more of y t matter, you would not deal with him more Injuriously than he does with you. why will you think y t so much tenderness is to be used on your part, while nothing of that nature is used on his? must you be Gentle and kind to him, while he is doging you to death? Contrary to all ye rules of reason and humanity? I dont say this, with any design y t you should treat him ill. but I think you ought to resent his unreasonable treatment of you, and give him no more opportunity to do it.

I hope, dear sister, you may have ye divine Conduct and marginal direction in this and all your affairs. and y t God who governs all things in infinite wisdom, will bring these things to an happy issue. And what better Course Can you take, than being much at ye throne of grace, with your Earnest requests for divine Conduct? and what Can be more Encouraging hereto, than y t sweet text phil.

4. 6. be Careful for nothing but in every thing — let your requests be made known to God? Commit your ways to him, and he will direct your steps.

we dont know of any body going to Boston at ye time you speak of in your letter. my wife sends you some flax, by mr Bliss. I shall be glad to see you at my house, and will freely Come and fetch you if that will be any kindness to you. if you will send me word when.

we Received your letter lasst night after nine of ye Clock, and I was loth to miss of this opportunity of writing to you, I have therefore been up ye whole night to write this. and it is now almost sun rise. if it will be of any service to you that will be a sufficient reward for watching.

we present our duty to father Edwards and Mother, and love in large measure to yourself and ye rest of our sisters. I am.

your loving Brother And Humble ser t

S. Hopkins.

1. Matthew Rockwell of East Windsor, who claimed that Hannah Edwards was legally bound to marry him. ↩
