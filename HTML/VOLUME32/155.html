"To his Excellency, Sir William Pepperrell.

"Stockbridge, Nov. 28, 1751.

Honoured Sir,

"When I had the opportunity the last spring of waiting on your honour at your seat at Kittery, & was there gratified & honoured by the kind & hospitable entertainment of your house, I was favoured with some conversation with you, concerning the affairs of the Indians at Stock bridge, & the business of the mission here, to which I had then been invited. And you were then pleased generously to offer me your good offices, to assist me in this employ ment, in any respect which you could render me, through your acquaintance and correspondance in london.

"I have lately been favoured with a letter from the Hon. Andrew Oliver of Boston, wherein he was pleased to send me an Extract of a letter he had seen, which was sent to you from Mr Joshua Paine,3 of London, concerning a proper plan for a school for Indian girls in thise parts, & to propose to me to write to you on the subject of the said Extract. This encourages me to hope that a letter from me, on this subject, to your Excellency will be kindly received. marginal

"With this hope, I would take leave to say, that I think that, as the boarding-schools here are now in their commencement, & are yet to receive their form & character, & that among a people hitherto unaccustomed to any method of instruction whatsoever, it is a great pity but that the method actu ally adopted should be free from the gross defects of the ordinary method of teaching among the English.

"One of these grand defects, as I humbly conceive, is this, that children are habituated to learning without un derstanding. In the common method of teaching, so far as my observation extends, children, when they are taught to read, are so much accustomed to reading, without any kind of knowledge of the meaning of what they read, that they continue reading a long time without understanding, even a very long time after they are capable of understand ing, were it not for an habit of making such & such sounds on the sight of such & such letters with a perfect inattentiveness to any meaning. In like manner they are taught their catechism, saying over the words by rote, which they began to say, before they were capable of easily & readily comprehending them, being long habituated to make sounds without connecting any ideas with them. They so contin marginal ue, even until they come to be capable of well understanding the words, & would perhaps have the ideas properly signified by the words naturally excited in their minds on hearing the words, were it not for an habitual hearing & speaking them without any ideas; so that if the question were put in phraseology somewhat new to which they have not been accustomed, they would not know what to answer. Thus it happens to children, even with regard to the plainest printed catechisms, even those which have been contrived with great care & art, so that they might be adapted to the lowest capacities.

"I should therefore think that, in these boarding-schools, the children should never read a lesson, without the master or mistress taking care, that the child be made to attend to, & understand, the meaning of the words & sentences which it reads, at least after the child begins to read without spelling, & perhaps in some degree before. And the child should be taught to understand things, as well as words. After it begins to read in a Psalter, Testament or Bible, not only the words & phrases should be explained, but the things which the lesson treats of should be, in a familiar manner, opened to the child's under standing; & the master or mistress should enter into conversation with the child about them. Familiar questions should be put to the child about the subjects of the lesson; & the child should be encouraged & drawn into on to speak freely & in his turn also to ask questions for the resolution of his own doubts.

"Many advantages would arise from this method. By this means the child's learning will be rendered pleasant, entertaining & profitable, as his mind will gradually open & expand with knowledge & his capacity for reasoning improved. His lesson will cease to be a dull, wearisome task, without any suitable pleasure or benefit. This will be a rational way of teaching. Assist ing the child's reason enables him to see the use & end & bene fit of reading at the same time that he takes pains from day to day to read. It is the way also for the child to [ ] from its infancy to think & reflect, & to beget in it an early taste for knowledge & a regularly increasing appe tite for it.

"So also with regard to the method of catechizing children; be sides obliging them to give the answers in the printed catechism, or in any stated form of words, questions should be asked them from time, in the same familiar manner, as they are asked questions commonly about their ordinary affairs, with familiar instructions, explanations, & rehearsals of things intermixed; &, if it be possible, by wise & skilful management, into the habit of conversation on divine evidence & should gradually be divest ed of that shyness & backwardness, usually discovered in children, marginal to converse on such topics with their superiors. And when the print ed catechism are used, as I am far from thinking they ought to be entirely neglected, care should be taken, that the child should attend to the meaning of the words, & be able to understand them; to this end, not only explaining the words & sentences, but also from time to time varying the phraseology, put ting the question in different words of the same sense, & also intermixing with the questions and answers, whether printed or not, some improvement or application, in counsels & warnings given to them; founded on the answers that have been given.

Beside the things already mentioned, there are other things, which, as it appears to me, ought to be done, with regard to the education of children in general, wherein the common methods of instruction in New-England, are grossly defective. The teacher, in familiar discourses, might, in a little time give the children a short general scheme of the Scriptural history, beginning with the Creation of the world, & descending through the various periods of that history, informing them of the larger divisions & more important events of the story, & giving them some idea of their connexion one with another first of the history of the Old Testament, & then of the New. And when the children had in their heads this general scheme, then the teacher marginal might at certain times entertain them, in like familiar discourse, with the particular stories of the Scriptures, sometimes with one & sometimes with another, before they can obtain the knowledge of them themselves by reading; for example, at one time the story of the Creation, at another time the story of the flood, at another of the dispersion of the nations, at another of the calling of Abraham, at another the story of Joseph, the bringing of the children of Israel out of Egypt: And in the New Testament, the Birth of Christ, some of the chief Acts of his life, his Death, his Resurrection, his Ascension, the Effusion of the Holy Spirit at the Day of Pentecost, & some of the chief of the Acts of the Apostles; withal, pointing out to them the place which each event has in the general scheme, & the connexion it has with other main parts of it. The teacher in a familiar manner should apply the events of the story discoursed upon for with the design of informing the child's understanding, influencing his heart, & directing his practice. Children who is4 able to read their bibles, might be set to read a particular scriptural history, sometimes one & sometimes another, diligently observing it, & examin ing for themselves all that is said concerning it. And when he has done, he might be called to the master or mistress, & enquired of concerning the particulars of the history, to see that he has paid attention, & is able to give a good account of it.

And I can see no good reason, why children in general, beside marginal the scriptural history, should not, in a like familiar manner of conversation, be taught something of the great successive changes & events, in the Jewish nation & the world at large, which con nect the history of the Old & New Testament. Thus they might be informed in short, of the manner in which the Four Great Monarchies succeeded each other, the Persecutions which the Jews suffered from Antiochus Epiphanes, & the principal Changes which happened to their Church & State before the coming of Christ. And they might be shewn how such & such Events were a fulfilment of such & such Prophecies. And when they learn the History of the New Testament, they might with much profit & entertainment have pointed out to them, many plain Prophecies of the Old Testament, which have, their Fulfilment in him. And I can see no good reason why children can not, or may not, be taught something in general of Ecclesias tical History, & be informed how things, with regard to the State of Religion & the Church of God, have gone on, as to some of the Main Events, from the time when the scriptural history ended to the present time; & how given Prophecies of the Scriptures have been fulfilled in some of these Events; or why they may not be told what may yet be expected to come to pass, according to the scriptural prophecies, from this time, to the end of the world.

It appears to me obvious also, that, in connection with all this, marginal they should be taught somewhat relating to the Chronology of events, which would make the story so much the more distinct & entertaining. Thus, they may be taught how long it was from the Creation of the world to the Coming of Christ; how long from the Creation to the Flood; how long from the Flood to the calling of Abraham, &c; How long David lived before Christ; how long before the Captivity in Babylon; & how long the Captivity, before Christ &c. How long since the Birth of Christ, how old he was when he began to preach, & when he was crucified; how long after his Resurrection before he ascended; How long also his death ascended after the destruction of Jerusalem by Nebuchadnezzar, until Babylon was destroyed by Cyrus; how long after the beginning of the Persian Empire, before that empire was overthrown by Alexander; when was the great oppression of the Jews by Antiochus Epiphanes; when Judea was conquered by the Romans; how long before Christ How long after Christ's Resurrection, before the Destruction of Jerusalem; & how long before the empire became christian; how long after Christ, before the Popes claimed such & such powers; When the worship of images was introduced; how long before the Reformation, &c. &c. All children are capable of being informed & having an idea of these things, & can much more easily learn them, if endeavours were used to that marginal end, than many things which they do learn.

And with like ease, & with equal benefit & the greater they might be taught some of the main things in Geography: which way the land of Canaan lies from this; how far it is; which way Egypt lay from Canaan; which way Babylon lay from Jerusalem, & how far; which way Padan Aram was from Canaan; where Rome lay from Jerusalem; where Antioch; &c. &c.

And I cannot but think it might be a pretty easy thing, if proper measures were taken, to teach children to spell well, & girls as well as boys. I should think it may be worth the while, on various accounts, to teach them to write, & also to teach them a little of Arithmetic, some of the first & plainest rules. Or, if it be judged that it is needless to teach all the children all things, some difference might be might made in children of dif ferent genius, & children of the best genius might be taught more things than others. And all would serve the more spee dily & effectually to change the taste of Indians, & to bring them off from their barbarism & brutality, to a relish for those things, which belong to Civility civilization & refinement.

Another thing, which properly belongs to a christian education, & which would be unusually popular with them, & which would in several respects have a powerful influence, in promoting the great end in view, of leading them to renounce the marginal coarseness, & filth & degradation, of savage life, for the cleanliness refinement & good morals, is teaching them to sing. Music soft- Music, especially sacred music, has a powerful efficacy to soften the heart into tenderness, to harmonize the affections, & to give the mind a relish for objects of a superior character.

In order to promote the salvation of the children, which is the main design of the whole Indian establishment at this place, I think that, beside their attending public worship on the sabbath, & the daily worship of the family, & in the school, & frequent counsels & warnings given them, when all together, by their teachers; each child should, from time to time, be dealt with singly, particularly & closely, about the state & concerns of his soul; & particular care should be taken to teach & direct each child, concerning the duty of Secret prayer, & the duty pressed & enforced on every one; & care should be taken, that all may have proper opportunity & convenience for it.

I would say nothing concerning buildings, lodgings, household stuff, cattle, servants, husbandry instruments, & utensils for the children's work; as it is agreed on all hands that these are necessary; & the providing of them will doubtless be left to the care & direction of Trustees tha tshall be appointed.

But I would beg leave to say further, with regard to methods marginal to forward the proficiency of the children in their learning, that I cannot but think measures might be devised greatly to encourage & animate them in it, & excite a laudable ambition to excel. One thing I have thought of which, as appears to me, might have a happy tendency this way in each of the board ing schools: At certain periods there should be a sort of public examination in the school, on a day appointed for the purpose, which shall be attended by all the Trustees, & all in the town who are in any respect employed connected with Indian affairs, & some of the neighbouring ministers, & gentlemen, & ladies; & also that the chiefs of the Indians be invited to attend; at which there should be a public trial of the proficiency, which has each one has made, in the various branches which have been taught, as in reading, writing, spelling, arithmetic; knowledge in the principles of religion, knowledge of church history &c; & that a provision shall be given to such as are found to excel, which may be done in something that will very much please Indian children, with but little expence: And likewise that the works of the children be then produced, to be judged of, that it may be determined who has made the greatest proficiency in learning to sew, to spin, to knit &c; & that a reward be given to such as have excelled. And perhaps also that a reward be then given to such as, by the testimony of their teachers & governors, have excelled in virtue or diligence, in care to speak the truth, in strictly observing the sabbath, in good manners, marginal in respect to their superiours &c. And that, on the day of the public trial, there be something of an entertainment made for the members of the school, & those who are invited to attend. This might tend, not only greatly to stimulate the children in their learning, but would be very pleasing & animating to the tribes of Indians, & would have great influence in rendering them very favourably disposed to the affairs of the schools.

But your Excellency will easily see that, in order to the practicableness of these things, in any tolerable degree & manner, it is necessary that the children should be taught the English tongue. And indeed this is the of the most absolute necessity on almost every account. Indian languages are extremely barbarous & barren, & very ill fitted for communi cating things moral & divine, or even things speculative & abstract. In short they are wholly unfit for a people possessed of civilization, knowledge & refinement.

Besides, without their learning English, their learning to read will be in vain; for the Indians have not the Bible, nor any other book, in their own language. Without this, their teachers cannot converse with them, & so can have no advantage to instruct them. Hence, all possible means must be used, in the first place, to introduce the English marginal tongue among the children. To this end, much pains should be taken to teach them the English name for every thing, & English words that denote such & such actions; & an Interpreter might be used for a while, to interpret their lessons to them, & to teach them to construe them, or turn them into Indian. And a number of English children might be put into the school with the Indian children. But the most effectual method of all would be, to put out some of the Indian children, first into some good English families, one at a place, to live there a year or two, before they are brought into the school; which would not only be above all others the most successful method, but would be absolute ly necessary, at least at first; but truly a great deal of care must be taken to find good places for them, & to look well to them, & to see that they are well taken care of, in the fam ilies to which they are sent. It is probable that the parents of the children might, with proper endeavours, be persuaded to such a measure.

But it will doubtless be very easily & quickly determined, by your Excellency, that, if such methods as those which have been mentioned or any like them, or indeed any other effectual measures, are taken, that it will be absolutely necessary, that the school should under the constant care & ins pection of Trustees, who live upon the spot, or very near at hand. marginal It will be in vain for any to expect, that any woman can look after such a school, & provide for & govern so large a family, & take care continually to order & regulate so many & great affairs relating to it, within doors & without, without much assistance of some always at hand, who are able & faithful, & are interested empowered. If she has under her a second, or a kind of usher, & has servants of both sexes, yet still she will be under the necessity of having some superiour assistance. And as to the precise method of teaching, & regulating the discipline of the school & family, it must be left very much to their discretion; for experience alone can certainly determine the fittest methods of ordering such an establishment, so new & untried, though many probable conjectures may be made. And Experience will doubtless direct to some new measures which cannot now be thought of. -Hoping that your Excellency will excuse the partic ularity & minuteness, into which I have unintentionally been led, on a subject on which I cannot but feel the deepest interest, I remain,

With very high respect,

Your most humble servant,

Jonathan Edwards.
