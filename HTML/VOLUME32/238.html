A212. Letter to John Erskine, December 11, 1755 212.JE-J.Erskine 12-11-55 Jonathan Edwards

A212. JE to John Erskine, 12/11/55. ANTS, f. 1755, #2-3. Addressed on cover "To the Rev. / Mr. John Erskine / Minister of the Gospel / at Culross / In Scotland."

Stockbridge Decem. 11, 1755.

Rev. & dear Sir,

I last wrote you in July 24. 55. Since that, in the latter

Part of last October, I received a Letter from you, dated June 23. 55.

together with the Essays on the Principles of Morality, from Mr. Hogg;

and the Analysis of the moral & religious Sentiments of Sopho, from

your Self.1 I thank you for your Letter & Present: and shall write a

Letter of Thanks to Mr. Hogg, for his Present by your Hands, added

to former Instances of his Generosity. I had before read that Book of

Essays; having borrowed Mr. Bellamy's; and also that Book of Mr. david

Hume's which you speak of.2 I am glad of an opportunity to read such Cor-

rupt Books; especially when written by men of considerable Genius; that

I may know have an Idea of the Notions that prevail in our Nation.

You say that "some People say that the Lord Kaims's being made a Lord of

session would have been prevented, if Chancellor Hardwick, & A. Bp.

Herring3 had seasonably seen his Book." I should be glad to know

who this Chancellor Hardwick is, & and what his Character. By your

mentioning him in such a manner, I am ready to suppose he may be

in some Respects of Good. And it is a matter of Thankfulness, if a man

of good Character, and a Friend to Religion be Lord Chancellor.

As to our warlike concerns, I have not heretofore been very parti-

cular in writing about them, in my Letters to Scotland, supposing it high-

ly probable, that you would have earlier accounts from Boston, N.

York & Philadelphia, than any I can send you, living at so great

a distance from any of the sea-ports. Nevertheless, seeing you propose

my sending some Account of the present Posture of Affairs, I would

say, that it appears to me, that notwithstanding some remarkable

Favours of Heaven, of which we are very unworthy, It has in the gene-

ral been a year of great Frowns of Providence on the British Ameri-

ca. Notwithstanding our success at Nova Scotia, and our having the

Better in the Battle near Lake George, and taking the French General

Prisoner ; yet, considering the Advantages the Enemy hath obtained

against us, by General Bradock's defeat;4 especially in gaining over

& confirming the Indians on their side, and disheartning & weakening

our Friends, and what we have suffered from our Enemies, and how

greatly we are weaken'd and almost sunk with our vast Expences,

especially in N. England, and the Blood as well as money we

have expended; I say, considering these things, and how little we

have gained by all our Cost & Trouble, our Case is no better, but

far worse than it was in the Beginning of the year. At least,

I think it certain that we have obtained no Advantages in any

wise to balance our Expence of Blood & Treasure. The Expedition

to the Eastward has been remarkably succeeded; But the other three

Expeditions, That against the French forts on the Ohio, That against

Niagara, & that against Crown Point, have all been unsuccessful

as to their main design. And tho' the Army under General John-

son5

son had a Kind of Victory over the French and took the Baron marginal

dieskeau, their General ; yet we suffered very greatly in the Battle;

and the taking of the French General, probably was the saving of

his Army; for by telling a lie to our Army, viz that the French

were in continual Expectation of being greatly inforced by a large

Body that marched another way, and had appointed to meet

them near that Place, Our Army was prevented from pursuing the

Enemy after they had repell'd them ; which if they had done, they

might have been under great Advantages to have cut 'em off, and pre-

vented the return of almost all of them to Crown-Point ; which could

be no otherwise than through the Water, in their Batteaus. Our

Army never proceeded any further than the Place of the Engagemt;

but having Built a Fort there, near Lake George, alias Lake St Sa-

crament, after they had built another near Hudson's River, about

14. Miles on this side, & left Garrison's, lately returned, as

also has the Army under General Shirley,6 who went with a

design against Niagara, after having built some vessels of Force

in the Lake Ontario, & strengthen'd the Fortifications at Oswego

and sent for the Remains of Gen. Bradocks Army to Albany there

to go into Winter Quarters. The Governours of the several Provinces,

have in the latter Part of the last month, had a meeting to confer

together concerning our warlike Affairs, And to agree on a Plan

of Operations, to be recommended to the Government at Home, for

the next year. But I have heard nothing of their determinations.

The Indians have not done much mischief on the Frontiers of N. England,

since Our Army have been above us ; but have been dreadful in their

Ravages in the back settlements of Virginia, New & Pennsylvania.

It is apparent that the ministry at home miss it very much in

sending over British Forces to fight with Indians in America, &

in sending British officers to have the Command of Our American

Forces. Let them send them send us Arms, Ammunition, & money

& shipping ; & let N. England men manage the Business their

own way ; who alone understand it. And to appoint British officers

over them is Nothing a Hindrance and Discouragement to 'em. Let

'em be well supplied & supported and defended by sea, and

then let 'em go forth under their own Officers, and manage in

their own way, as they did in the Expedition against Cape

Breton. All the Provinces in America seem to be fully sensible,

that New-England men are the only men to be employed against

Canada : as I had Opportunity abundantly to obtain observe in

my late Journey to N. York, N. Jersey & Philadelphia.

However we ought to remember that neither N. England men

nor any other, are any thing unless God be with us. And when

we have done all at finding fault with measures taken & Instru-

ments employed wh we can't expect Prosperity unless the ac-

cursed Thing be removed from our Camp.

God has lately frown'd on my Family in taking away a faithful

servt

Servant,7 who was a great Help to us. two of my marginal

Children have been under threatning Infirmities,

but are something better. I desire your Prayers for

us all. My Wife joins me in affectionate

& respectful salutations to you & Mrs Erskine.

I am, Rev. & dear sir,

your obliged Brother & affectionate Friend

Jonathan Edwards.

1. Henry Home, Lord Kames, Esays on the Principles of Morality and Natural Religion (Edinburgh, 1751); and John Bonar, An Analysis of the Moral and Religious Sentiments of Sopho [Lord Kames] and David Hume, Esq. (Edinburgh, 1755). ↩
2. Titles by David Hume in JE's "Catalogue of Reading" (Beinecke Library) are A Treatise of Human Nature (2 vols. London, 1739;nd40), Philosophical Essays Concerning Human Understanding (London, 1748), and An Enquiry Concerning the Principles of Morals (London, 1751). See also Works, 1, 14, n. 1; and 8, 604, n. 1. ↩
3. Philip Yorke, Earl of Hardwicke, and Abp. Thomas herring. ↩
4. On July 9, 1755, near Ft. Duquesne. ↩
5. Sir William Johnson. ↩
